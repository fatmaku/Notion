To my daughters: I walked this path so that you would never feel my absence as a kind of invisibility. I am not afraid of death. I am not particularly brave, either. It is only that my ache runs very deep. And one thing you should know from the start: this book tells perhaps only ten percent of the traumas I have lived through. The rest I have held back — to protect myself, but above all to protect my family, my children, and my bond with them. Everything written here is true; yet what is left unsaid is far more. Foreword This is not a success story. This is the story of a person who wanted to understand himself — someone who broke into a thousand pieces along the way and put himself back together. If you are reading this book… you are either searching for yourself or you have lost yourself. They are the same place. I will not show you a path. But I will show you that you are not alone on this one. Because the person described here is me. And at the same time… it is you. A small warning before we begin: you will find a great deal of pain in this book. But you will find a great deal of humor too. This is not a contradiction. For years, humor was the only tool that let me sit in the same room as my own mind without either of us slamming the door and walking out. So if I suddenly crack a joke in the middle of a dark scene, that is not me running away. It is the very technique that kept me alive. There is one more thing I want you to know from the start, because it is the true voice of this book. My mind never goes quiet. While I am telling you one thing, another film is running inside my head at the very same time: "What if it had gone differently? What if I had said something else that day? What if I had stayed, what if I had left, what if I had never spoken that one sentence?" What happened is lived only once, but inside me it replays a hundred times, each time with a different ending. I keep reconstructing the past; I feel possibilities that never happened as though they had; I tie every detail to another. Some have called this "overthinking." But it is not a flaw — it is the way a neurodivergent mind works. My brain never stops making connections, never stops weighing possibilities, never stops asking. Is it exhausting? Very. But it is also the very thing that made me write this book: because I did not simply live my life — I ran it again inside myself a thousand times over. Those Inner voice boxes you will see throughout the book are exactly that unstoppable voice. As you read them, you are hearing that constant "what if?" inside a person's head. If you carry such a mind yourself, you may see, for the first time, someone putting that voice into words. And perhaps, for the first time, you will feel this: that voice is not a malfunction. It is simply an engine working very hard. At the end of each chapter you will find three things. The first is a calm, expert assessment — I call it the Therapist's Note : what is happening, what could happen if it goes unhealed, and what the path to healing looks like. The second is a short section titled Where I Stand Today ; there I honestly answer the one question that truly matters: have I left behind what that chapter describes, or am I still carrying it? And the third is for you: Your Turn . There are lines left for you there — fill them without hesitation. This book is as much yours to write in as it is to read. Don't just read the book — talk with it. After a certain point, this book repeats itself. On purpose. Some sentences come around again and again, some lessons meet you anew in different chapters. Because I did not learn life only once; I learned the same lesson many times, each time a little more deeply. Like a good teacher, this book gently reminds you of what you have forgotten — without your noticing. Perhaps you will pause at a sentence and say, "this is exactly me." That is the moment the book is doing its work: it is not teaching you something; it is showing you what was already inside you. And the whole book has a single heartbeat. Let me say it from the start so that you recognize it when you hear it at the end of every chapter: I am never finished; I am becoming, each day, right up to my final breath. Understanding this is, I think, the key to everything. Because there is no such thing as a "finished," "complete," "arrived" person. There are only people who become a little more themselves each day. I am one of them. So are you.

Chapter 1 – Who Am I, or What Am I? I always lived inside my head. My body was in one place, I was in another. The teacher would explain something at the board, and I would watch the bird passing by the window: where is it going, why did it choose that branch, how many leaves does that branch have. “Tuncay!” When I heard my name, I’d flinch, come back, but I wouldn’t know what had been asked. The bird was still in my mind. They called me inattentive. But it wasn’t that I couldn’t give my attention — my attention went to everything at once. Back then I didn’t know it had a name. No one did. “This child’s a bit up in the clouds,” they’d say. I was up in the clouds, yes. But what I saw up there, no one else could see. Even my name was chosen by my grandfather; my mother told me later, “I didn’t want to say anything, no one asked me.” Before I was even born, even my name was someone else’s decision. Maybe that’s why I spent my whole life asking: who am I? My mind never went quiet. Never. Even while I was living something, another film was running inside me: what if it had been like this, what if I’d said something else that day, what if I’d stayed, what if I’d gone. What happened, happened once — but in me it replayed a hundred times, each time with a different ending. Sometimes that film sped up so much that I was afraid I’d lose myself. A day I wrote to my mother is still in my mind: “Pray for patience for me, Mum, so I don’t lose myself, so I can keep control, pray for that.” Against that film in my head, I was sometimes so helpless that I needed a mother’s prayer. They called it “overthinking.” I call it surviving; without thinking I’d have fallen apart, and thinking wore me out. Both were true at once. With me everything was always like that — true in two ways at the same time. I'm in Istanbul. In my father's caravan. Night. In Üsküdar. Alone. It's coming up on three o'clock. Outside, a streetlamp burns all by itself; an orange, flickering light seeps in through the fogged window of the caravan and forms a pale stain on the ceiling. I stare at that stain. The light shifts a little every time the wind makes the glass tremble — as if it's breathing in and out. The inside of the caravan is dim; the corners are dark, and in the middle that single orange trace. And beneath that trace, me. The cold is merciless. It doesn't come from under the blanket; it comes from somewhere inside, starting at my skin, working its way into my organs, and down to the bone. After a certain point, being cold turns into pain — my jaw clenches, my shoulders rise toward my ears on their own. The metal walls of the caravan shiver faintly in the wind: a constant, never-ending, thin vibration. As if someone is running their fingertips over the metal and softly saying, "I feel you. You're here. You're still here." My breath turns to mist; every exhale that leaves my mouth becomes visible for an instant in that orange light, then scatters. My hands are stiff. My fingertips are numb, and when I try to bend them they tingle. A mixed smell fills my nose: old engine oil, rusty metal, damp fabric, and beneath them a faint trace of cheap cleaner — that smell my father wiped away years ago but that never fully left. This smell makes me feel both at home and like a stranger at the same time. Sounds come. A ferry horn in the distance — muffled, deep, stretching out across the water. Then a siren; long, sharp, tearing someone's night to pieces somewhere. A dog barks in broken bursts — lonely, tired, a bark that can tell no one its trouble. Two streets over, the tea sellers are packing up their last stalls; the metallic scrape of trays, the rattle of chains, a chair dragging against stone. Outside, life goes on with its usual indifferent rhythm. I lie there without moving. In the middle of all those sounds, it's as if I'm inside a glass dome: I hear everything, but I can touch none of it. The phone screen lights up for a moment, showing the time. But I'm really looking at another clock — the one inside me, the one that never stops. And that clock has only one question: "How much longer?" How much longer will I fight? How much longer will I hold on? How much longer will I try to understand? How much longer will I sit alone, at night in a caravan, inside my own head? And beside me, right here, lying in the fold of the blanket, there is something. Something that hasn't left my side for years. A question. "Who am I?" Inner voice: I haven't been asking this question since yesterday. I've been asking it for years. As a child at the back of the classroom, as a young man in the middle of a shift, as a husband at the edge of the bed, as a father looking into my daughters' faces… always the same question, never falling silent. But shall I be honest? I never found a clear answer. And now, inside this cold metal box, I notice something strange: maybe I'm not the one searching for an answer. Maybe, after carrying the question for so long, I became the question itself. I walk through the world like a question — open-ended, its answer deferred, but alive. Life's Contract For years I asked myself: "Did I understand life, or did I just memorize it by taking blow after blow?" Then I noticed something. Life runs on this small, cruel rule: whatever you want, you first get its opposite. The universe gives the exercise first and unlocks the gift afterward. I asked for patience. What did I get? Waiting. Injustice. Wasted time. People. ★ Patience isn't learned — it's forced on you. I wanted to be strong. What did I get? Loneliness. Helplessness. Those "there's no one here" moments. ★ True strength is born not from support, but from its absence. I wanted an easy life. What did I get? Debts. Responsibility. Burden. ★ Money isn't freedom — it's a test. That's when I understood: this isn't a punishment. This is… an education. The source, the universe, the system — call it whatever you like. The rule doesn't change: "I give you the opposite of what you want. If you succeed… you'll have earned it, and then it will be given." Age 36 – The Caravan – Ground Zero I'm 36 years old. In Istanbul. Living in my father's old caravan. I'm not a tourist. I didn't come to have fun. After my second divorce, I set out — on a long journey across the world and into research. I wanted to find myself again. On other continents I worked intensely on neurodivergence, gathered studies and reports, and saw how different cultures relate to neurodivergent people. In that period I had my dog Happy with me, a Labrador puppy. He was about to begin training as a therapy dog to work with autistic children. He was the real reason I was able to set out: with him I didn't feel completely alone, and I had a responsibility. I could take care of him. But the cost was heavy. During the separation in my second marriage, my cat Mokka was killed by a car. Later, here in Turkey, Happy went the same way. These losses taught me loneliness and the journey all over again. Life has a strange sense of humor: it sends you a companion so you can learn to let go — then takes them back so you really learn. I was born in Germany, in Landau an der Isar. A small town. But for me, a great beginning. I'm a Turk with a German passport, a Bavarian-Swabian mix — even my origin is a question with more than one right answer. We are three siblings. I was the eldest. But I never truly managed to be the "big" one. Two marriages. Both ended. Two girls from the first marriage, Ceylin and Ceysu; two more from the second, Aykız and Balkız. Four girls in all. About a year and a half lies between each sister, and nearly ten years between the children of the two marriages. ★ I never had a son. But inside me I had to raise a boy, a child who was never born. Diagnosis: Understanding Myself for the First Time At 32: ADHD. At 33: autism. Today they call the combination of these two AuDHD. This was no ordinary diagnosis. This was the explanation of my life. Inner voice: Until that day I kept asking, "Why am I like this?" Then I began to say, "So this is who I am." The puzzle pieces fell into place: why I think so fast. Why I can't focus yet see everything at once. Why I feel people so intensely. Why I trusted the wrong people. Why I always gave and got so little in return. ★ Neurodivergence is not a malfunction. It's a correct system that grew up in the wrong environment. Love, betrayal, separations, conflict, illnesses — there were very hard lessons in my life. Some of them made me stronger. Some nearly broke me. But today I see it: all of them brought me to exactly this place. And yes… it's hard to say, but it's true: "It's good that it happened this way. Otherwise I wouldn't be the person I am today." I lie inside a thin sleeping bag, in my father's old caravan. By choice. But the cost was heavier than I'd guessed. Outside, the wind strikes the metal wall — tap, tap, tap. As if someone is knocking on the door saying, "Open up, let's talk." I answer from within: "Not now, brother. Right now I'm talking with myself."

== ection0004 [] class="body0" xmlns:epub="http://www.idpf.org/2007/ops"> Therapist's Note – Existential Crisis What is happening here: A person with high self-awareness and AuDHD sits at ground zero, asking the most radical of questions: Who am I? This is not self-pity. It's the typical nighttime state of a late-diagnosed neurodivergent brain. For decades the brain has been oscillating between two basic states: the autistic side scanning relentlessly for threat (the environment, sounds, faces, the "what ifs"), the ADHD side relentlessly seeking stimulation and reward. When both run in the same head, a chronic hum remains in between: "Is something wrong with me?" Where it comes from — body and evolution This "How much longer?", this nighttime vigil, is actually a very old piece of software. Deep in the human brain, the amygdala is the alarm bell that has kept us alive for millions of years; Bessel van der Kolk calls it "the brain's smoke detector" ( The Body Keeps the Score , 2014). In the Stone Age, our ancestors who stayed alert at night and interpreted a rustle as "maybe a predator" survived; whether the ones who relaxed survived is unknown. So threat-scanning is not a malfunction but an inheritance . Stephen Porges's Polyvagal Theory calls this "neuroception": without our awareness, our body scans the environment at every moment, asking "am I safe?" (Porges, 2011). In a neurodivergent nervous system, this scanning runs more sensitively, more loudly. The "constantly thinking mind" also has an address: the brain's default mode network kicks in when we're not occupied with the outside world and constructs the past and the future. When it overworks, this is called rumination — the same thought coming around again and again (Nolen-Hoeksema's depression research, 1991). Your staring at the light on the ceiling at three in the morning and saying "how much longer?" is, scientifically, the night shift of this network. If left unaddressed — possible bad outcomes If the existential question is left unanswered and unsupported, it can turn into an existential pit. The possible course: an entrenched belief of "I am wrong," recurring depressive episodes, social withdrawal, disrupted sleep, chronic anxiety; in advanced stages, a sense of meaninglessness and — it must be said plainly — suicidal thoughts. The question "Who am I?" quietly slides toward "Why do I even exist?" In late-diagnosed adults this slide is unfortunately common; that's why a diagnosis can sometimes, in the fullest sense of the word, save a life. Paths of healing — not a single road, but several doors At this point there are a few methods that help, and they're all built on "turning the question from an enemy into a companion": • Writing / externalization: Instead of spinning the inner voice around in your head, writing for 5–10 minutes a day. Pennebaker's research on "expressive writing" shows that putting emotion into words improves the immune system and mood (Pennebaker, 1997). This book itself is a product of that therapy. • Internal Family Systems (IFS – Richard Schwartz): Seeing those different voices inside you (the critic, the frightened one, the protector) not as enemies to be suppressed, but as "parts" that are all trying to protect you. What you call your "inner voice" is exactly IFS's field of work. • Somatic approaches (Peter Levine – Somatic Experiencing) and body awareness: A brain in constant alarm is calmed not by logic, but only by the body. Planting your feet on the ground, slow breathing (a long exhale stimulates the vagus nerve and pulls the system into "safe" mode — Polyvagal Theory). • Self-compassion (Kristin Neff) and Compassion-Focused Therapy (Paul Gilbert): Answering the "I am wrong" voice as if speaking to a friend. • Connecting: Contact with other neurodivergent people. There's no medicine that dissolves the "I am wrong" belief as fast as the sentence "I have the same thing too." Tuncay's bridge For years I applied most of these methods on my own, without knowing their names — then, as I read more, I'd say, "so this had a name." When I read Porges, I understood that freezing as a child at the sound of my father's keys was not a "lack of character" but neuroception; I felt relieved. Van der Kolk's sentence, "the body keeps the score," explained why all those stomach clenches are still there. When I learned IFS, I began to see the crowd in my head not as "madness" but as a family meeting — and I took the head of the table. What I really did was this: I took the science and spread it like a map over my own life. That map turned the sentence "I am broken" into the sentence "I am an explainable system." And so every therapist's note in this book is an attempt to hand you that map too. Sources: B. van der Kolk, The Body Keeps the Score (2014); S. Porges, The Polyvagal Theory (2011); R. Schwartz, Internal Family Systems; K. Neff, Self-Compassion; J. Pennebaker, expressive writing research; I. D. Yalom, existential psychology. Where I Stand Today I still ask the question "Who am I?" The difference is this: it no longer frightens me. It used to be an accusation; today it's a door. I stopped forcing a definitive answer, and the moment I stopped forcing it, things got easier. So I didn't manage to get rid of the question — I managed to make peace with it. Today I can say this: the problem wasn't me; I was just in the wrong environment for a long time. And that is a difference that changes an entire life. "Yesterday I was clever, so I wanted to change the world. Today I am wise, so I am changing myself." — Rumi I am not a destination, I am a becoming. And this becoming ends only at my last breath.

== ection0006 [] class="body0" xmlns:epub="http://www.idpf.org/2007/ops"> Chapter 2 – Childhood: The Silent Scream The house was warm. Genuinely warm. And here is the strange part: I still remember that warmth on my skin today, yet in that house I never once felt truly "seen." A body can grow warm while the inside keeps shivering. I learned that much later — as an adult, inside a cold caravan, on a strange night when my body froze while something inside me grew warm. It was one of the many backwards lessons life had saved up for me: as a child I was warm on the outside and cold within; once I grew up, I flipped it around. The radiator burned so fiercely that on cold winter evenings I would wedge my sock-clad feet between two of its panels. The cast iron would first sting through the sock, almost sizzling; you want to pull your foot away but you don't — because right after that sting comes something else: the warmth climbs slowly from the soles to the calves, from there to your waist, your back, up under the nape of your neck. My shoulders dropped. My jaw loosened. That constant knot in my belly came undone for a moment. And right then, my fingers stuck to the hot metal, I would say quietly to myself: "Now I am safe." A child's safety could sometimes fit inside one great big radiator. On top of the radiator, between the iron grooves, there would be a thin line of dust; with the heat, the smell of that dry, faintly scorched dust would rise — the smell of a radiator. Even today, if I catch that same smell somewhere, my body remembers before I do: my shoulders drop. So safety has a smell, then; and I memorized it at five years old, without any words. Inner voice: "Why can I bear the sting of the heat so well, yet I can't bear that nameless chill inside me at all?" Then I would laugh at myself: "Because you know where the radiator burns, little man. As for the chill inside, nobody ever gave you its address." My mother's cooking smells would drift in from the kitchen. First the sharp scent of onions sweetening as they fried; then the sizzle of minced meat in oil, the deep aroma of tomato paste, a pinch of cinnamon, a bay leaf. This smell would seep under the door, travel like a trail through the cold air of the corridor, and reach the door of my room. The moment it touched my nose my stomach would clench, my mouth would water — but that hunger did not belong to the stomach alone. That smell told me, "you are here, you have a home, evening will come, the table will be set." Through smell, food spoke the reassurance that words never could. Perhaps that is why my relationship with food was always this tangled: I looked for love most of all there, on the plate. The table would be set, plates clinking, forks rattling against full bowls. My stomach would fill. And yet, when I rose from the table, some place inside me stayed hungry — a place I couldn't name, couldn't fill. As a child you don't notice this; you only think, "would it pass if I ate a little more?" It didn't pass. Because that hunger had nothing to do with food. Inner voice: "How can a person still feel something missing at a table that smells this good, that is filled this full?" Years later I found the answer: a plate says, "I am feeding you," but it cannot say, "I am hearing you." I had confused the two. And I went on confusing them for years. The Voice That Always Talked, Never Asked My mother often muttered to herself. Sometimes out loud, sometimes only a faint hum. But most of the time the phone was at her ear, and she talked on and on. The conversations never ended. She always told the same story: what she had been through, the pains she carried, what had piled up inside her. Her voice would rise and fall. I sat at the kitchen table, listening with my legs swinging. I didn't understand everything. Or rather: I didn't understand the words, but I felt them. A strange, heavy feeling would form inside me. I remember the tone of her voice more than her words. That tremor left hanging in the air when a sentence ended. The telephone cord — phones had cords in those years — would wind around her finger, unwind, wind again. It was as if I counted every turn of that cord. The kitchen light was yellow, and beneath the lamp there was a halo like soot. Onions, tomato paste, cigarette smoke perhaps, and my mother's voice — all of it hung suspended in the same air. I was there, yet it was as if I were behind glass: I heard everything, I could touch none of it. This feeling would later become very familiar to me; in a caravan, in the night of Istanbul, I would find myself behind that exact same glass. Inner voice: "Why does my mother always talk but never ask me anything?" Then I would get angry at myself: "Maybe I'm the one who doesn't know how to be asked." And a smaller, more insidious voice would add: "Maybe you're just not interesting enough to be asked about." Now, as an adult, I can look at that scene with different eyes. The woman on the other end of that phone wanted to be seen somewhere too. She too was waiting to tell her own pain to someone, for someone to say, "I am hearing you." She carried a burden she couldn't bear and had found no other place to unload it. As a child I couldn't have known this — I only felt that my turn never came. Yet perhaps no one's turn ever came. Perhaps in that house everyone sat with the same hunger to be seen, only in different corners. Inner voice: "What if one day she had sat down at the table, hung up the phone, and said, 'How was your day?' Just once." I have composed that sentence so many times that it sits in my mind like a memory that really happened. In truth it never did. But it is a strange thing, isn't it, that a sentence that never was can make you long for it this much? The Television That Never Fell Silent, and the First Heroes The television in the house never fell silent. The newscaster's voice, the theme music of some series, the maddening jingles of commercials… There was no silence. There was a constant drone. That drone turned into something physical in me: a tension at the nape of my neck, a pressure inside my ears, a constant "flee" feeling. Now I know its name — sensory overload. Back then I only knew that I couldn't bear it. At some point I would break and withdraw to my room. I would close the door, and that "click" of the door was the most beautiful sound in the world: the noise dropped, the pressure fell, my breath deepened. I would sit cross-legged on the bed and turn on the television. Inner voice: "Isn't it funny? I flee the television in the living room and turn on the television in my room." But there was a difference: the sound in the living room hit me, while in my room I chose the sound myself. The issue wasn't the noise. The issue was whose hands it was in. The one noise I had control over stopped being noise at all. Dragon Ball. Every single time, Goku got beaten, knocked to the ground covered in blood. His face battered and bruised, his breath ragged, his knees giving out. I would press right up to the screen, my nose nearly touching the glass. Because that was the critical moment for me: will he get up? Then, with that big, dumb grin, he would rise to his feet again. His hair turned gold, a golden light burst around him, his power surged. Something inside me leapt out of place; my chest swelled and I clenched my fists. Inner voice: "How does this work? He nearly dies almost every time, yet he rises stronger. Should I be like that too? Is enduring this easy?" Then a deeper question would come: "Or is it not easy — is there just no other choice?" Goku became my first therapist. A cartoon hero glowing blue and gold taught me what no human had: getting back up is not a matter of strength, it's a matter of stubbornness. ★ What makes a hero a hero is not that he is never struck, but that he rises every time he is struck down. I would use this lesson many more times — in two divorces, in losses, beneath debts, in the cold of a caravan. That golden-haired boy, without knowing it, had prepared me for life. When I first watched The Lion King, something inside me broke. The scene where Mufasa dies… Little Simba stands beside his father's lifeless body, trying to wake him. He shoves his tiny paw under the huge body and pushes, nestles against his ear, says, "Dad, come on, get up…" In that moment my throat knotted. A heavy stone settled on my chest. My eyes filled, a warm tear slid down my cheek, and I felt ashamed — so that no one would see. I had switched off the light in the room; thank goodness I had. Inner voice: "Am I like that too? If my father died, would I feel guilty too? Would I want to run away too?" And right after, with a child's logic: "I wonder if all the bad things happen because of some mistake of mine?" This film truly broke me. Because that little lion was me. Alone. Guilty. Not knowing what to do. Here is the interesting thing: a cartoon could touch a place in me that the real people around me never touched. Perhaps that is why I took refuge in stories — because stories expected nothing from me, they only made me feel. A screen had "seen" me more than the people across the table. Even writing this hurts; but at the same time I am grateful to those screens. Without them, perhaps I would never have learned to feel at all. A Different Frequency I didn't like school. I didn't like the classroom. I didn't understand people. I wanted friends. Truly. But they didn't want me. Their way of talking was different, their jokes were different, their laughter was different. I was on a different frequency. I couldn't keep up. A group of kids would laugh at something, and I would start laughing where the laughter had already ended — a second too late. That one second always left me on the outside. As if everyone had been handed a manual, and mine hadn't been handed out. I remember the recesses: I would stand in the corner of the yard, against the wall. The sun warmed one side of me, while my other side shivered in the shade. The children's shouting, the ball slamming against the concrete, a girl's shrill scream, the sharp ring of the bell — all of it piled one on top of another. I would look at the crowd and think, "should I join in?" But I didn't know the rule for joining. How do you get into a ball game? How do you jump into the middle of a conversation? Everyone knew, I didn't. Well, actually I did know — I rehearsed it over and over in my head. It was just that my feet never matched that rehearsal. Inner voice: "If I went over, if I said, 'can I play too?'… What if they said 'no'? What if they laughed?" And I would stop, staying there against the wall. Not even risking rejection was safer than being rejected. So even as a child I was running calculations like this. The coward's arithmetic, it seems, is learned early. I fought constantly with my siblings. From the outside it looked as if we couldn't get along at all. Shouting, squabbling, slamming doors. But in truth I wanted to play with them. I wanted to be near them. I just didn't know how. A fight was a child's clumsiest, most painful way of saying, "look at me, I'm here too." A shove was a kind of contact after all. == ection0008 [] class="body0" xmlns:epub="http://www.idpf.org/2007/ops"> Therapist's Note – Childhood Emotional Neglect What is happening here: This is a textbook example of something rarely noticed, because it is so invisible: emotional neglect. Careful — this does not mean "bad parent." It means this: bodily needs (food, warmth, a roof) are met, but the basic emotional need — "I see you, I ask after you, your inner world matters to me" — is left wanting (Jonice Webb, Running on Empty , 2012). A child who is well cared for but not emotionally seen learns this insidious sentence: "I'm here, my stomach is full, I'm warm — I have no right to complain. So the problem must be me." That contradiction between the warm house and the chill inside is exactly this: the plate gets filled, but the "I am being heard" bowl stays forever empty. Where it comes from — body and evolution The human infant is one of nature's most helpless young; for years it cannot survive on its own. That is why evolution has wired us with a survival cable: the attachment system . John Bowlby and Mary Ainsworth showed that a child clings to the caregiver not only for food but as a "secure base" — when frightened, it seeks a face to turn back to for shelter, a face to look to in order to be seen (Bowlby, Attachment , 1969; Ainsworth's "strange situation" experiment, 1978). When the baby cries, the softening of the caregiver's face, the voice turning toward it — this is called emotional "attunement." When this attunement is missing, the child survives but learns something: "There is no answer to the world inside me." Stephen Porges's Polyvagal Theory gives this a biological foundation: our nervous system constantly reads, from another person's face, tone of voice, and gaze, the signal "am I safe and am I seen?" — he calls this "neuroception" (Porges, 2011). If the mother's voice is constantly flowing toward someone else , the child's nervous system can never quite receive that soothing signal; it stays full but on alert. And when an AuDHD brain is layered on top of this, the matter compounds. The autistic nervous system experiences sensory input more intensely (a television that is always on becomes a real physical burden), while the ADHD side hungers for dopamine — that is, for attention, novelty, reward. Decoding social signals (how to enter a conversation, the timing of a joke) is not an automatic, built-in program; it is a puzzle that must be solved by hand; "laughing a second too late" is the classic sign of this. The child's response is clever, even ingenious: it seeks warmth in the concrete (feet on the radiator — the body trying to compensate through skin for what the soul cannot find), and flees into a world built in the mind — its name: maladaptive daydreaming . Imagination becomes the safest place, because it is the only place where control belongs entirely to the child. His attachment to Goku and Simba is no coincidence either: the mirror neuron system in the brain makes us live the emotion of a character we watch as if it were our own; while no one in real life attuned to him, a screen character taught him empathy. If left unaddressed — possible bad outcomes The child internalizes the "I'm not wanted / I'm not seen" algorithm and carries it into adulthood unchanged. Possible course: difficulty recognizing and naming one's own emotions ( alexithymia — "I'm feeling something, but I don't know its name"); a chronic loneliness that chafes even in full rooms, even among those who love you; in relationships, either over-giving or withdrawing on the expectation that "I won't be seen anyway"; confusing love with food, success, or approval; recurring depressive episodes and low self-worth. The most insidious of all: the person cannot find the cause of their unhappiness, because "nothing bad even happened" — the pain of neglect is the pain not of something that happened but of something that didn't happen, and it is very hard to put your finger on something that isn't there. Paths of healing — several doors The direction is clear: the inner world must now come out, and this time someone must attune to that child — and if need be, that "someone" must be the grown-up self. • Expressive writing (Pennebaker): Pouring the inner world onto paper. James Pennebaker's research shows that writing about emotions several days in a row improves mood and even immunity (1997). Answering the question "what really happened inside me today?" in writing is asking yourself the question no one asked. This very book is proof of that. • Internal Family Systems (IFS – Richard Schwartz): Approaching that unseen child within you as an "exile," and having the grown, calm "Self" turn toward it and attune. That is, stopping waiting for your mother and instead sitting down at that table yourself and asking the child, "How was your day?" • Compassion Focused Therapy (Paul Gilbert) and Self-compassion (Kristin Neff): Responding gently to the "the problem is me" voice, as if speaking to a child you love. Placing a hand on the heart (the "radiator mode" in the afterthought) activates precisely the soothing system Gilbert worked on. • Somatic approaches (Peter Levine – Somatic Experiencing) and body awareness: That "chill inside" and "knot in the belly" live in the body and cannot be undone with words. A slow, long exhale stimulates the vagus nerve and pulls the system into "safe" mode (Polyvagal Theory). • Reparenting and forming a bond: Experiencing attunement, late though it may be, within a safe relationship — therapist, friend, partner, or a community. The brain is plastic; the experience of "being seen" can lay down a new cable even in adulthood. • Turning imagination into a resource: That inner world that was once an escape — that safe room where control belonged to the child — turns in adulthood into story, into painting, into a book. The place you fled to becomes the place you create. Tuncay's bridge For years I had labeled those scenes "a lovely childhood I have no right to complain about" and shelved them. Because truly nothing bad "had happened" — the house was warm, the table was full. The day I read Webb's concept of "emotional neglect," something inside me clicked into place: so pain doesn't necessarily have to come from a slap; sometimes pain comes from a question that never came, from a gaze that never turned back. That was when I understood why I stayed hungry when I rose from that table — it wasn't the stomach being fed, it was the hunger to be seen. When I read Porges, I saw why my nervous system could never quite say "safe" while my mother's voice always flowed toward someone else; this relieved me, because I wasn't an "ungrateful child," just a system that hadn't received the signal. And the best part: I stopped being angry at those screens, at Goku, at Simba. They were my first mirror neuron exercises; while no one in real life attuned to me, they were the ones who taught me to feel. Today I am the one who attunes to that unseen child — I am the one who sits at the table, I am the one who asks, "how was your day?" It would be a lie to say I have completely "filled" that emptiness I couldn't fill for years; but now I can look at it not with panic, but with compassion. And that too is a kind of healing. Where I Stand Today I still recognize that emptiness from back then. But I have learned to answer it now not with panic, but with compassion. When the old "no one sees me" feeling comes, I place my hand on my heart and — yes, a little cliché — I say: "Radiator mode is on, but this time I warm myself." And so today I am the one who asks after the child no one asked after. I have forgiven my mother too; because now I see that she too, somewhere, was trying to tell her own pain to someone at the end of a phone, with the same hunger to be seen. No one was bad; it was just that no one had been able to ask after anyone. This is what I have truly left behind: in order to exist, I no longer wait for someone else to see me. And to my four daughters — I am grateful that I can give them that sentence I never knew by heart, the one I learned only later: sitting at the table and asking, "how was your day?" "Children are educated by what the grown-up is and not by his talk." — Carl Gustav Jung I am not yet complete. I complete myself daily — until my last breath.

== ection0010 [] class="body0" xmlns:epub="http://www.idpf.org/2007/ops"> Chapter 3 – Bullying, Shame, and the First Fracture School, for me, was not a building. It was a battlefield. I am not exaggerating when I say that. For most of my childhood, I woke in the mornings with a warm nausea in my stomach. Before I had even opened my eyes, before the daylight had seeped past the edge of the curtain, my body was already preparing for that day's war: shoulders up, jaw locked, breath shallow. For most children, school was a day. For me, it was a front line; and every morning I walked onto that line with a single shelter in my hand — my own silence. Every year I was the new kid. == ection0012 [] class="body0" xmlns:epub="http://www.idpf.org/2007/ops"> Therapist's Note – Chronic Bullying and Body Shame What is happening here: Here, three things are colliding at once, and each one alone is heavy enough. One: an attachment wound born of constant moving, a wound of never being able to put down roots — every friendship severed before it can even sprout. Two: an AuDHD brain that reads social signals differently, and therefore always seems "half a beat off." Three: a child of immigrant background, already standing between two worlds. When these three overlap, what emerges is a chronic sense of "I don't belong here." Shame here is not "thought about," it is recorded in the body — stomach a stone, cheeks aflame, ears ringing, throat dry. This is a classic freeze or fight response coming not from the thinking part of the brain, but from its oldest part. And the masking begins: looking strong on the outside, breaking on the inside. The fist and the silence are, in fact, two faces of the same mask. Where it comes from — body and evolution That a child should be shaken so deeply in the face of "not being accepted" is not a weakness but an evolutionary inheritance. The human infant is one of nature's most helpless offspring; it cannot survive on its own. That is why our brain reads exclusion just like a physical danger . Naomi Eisenberger's brain-imaging studies showed that social rejection activates the brain regions that process bodily pain (the anterior cingulate cortex) (Eisenberger, 2003) — meaning "my heart is broken" is not just a metaphor, but a state the brain actually records as pain. For our ancestors, being cast out of the group meant death; that's why the alarm of "everyone is looking at me, I'm wrong" is a survival circuit. That the body holds onto this memory is no coincidence either. The very title of Bessel van der Kolk's book says it: The Body Keeps the Score (van der Kolk, 2014). When a traumatic moment is lived, the amygdala — in van der Kolk's words, "the brain's smoke detector" — records the sensory traces of that moment (smell, sound, touch) with a danger label. Years later, when a similar smell or a sudden loud noise arrives, the body mistakes the time and teleports you back into that classroom; this is called a trauma trigger. What you call "catching the smell of chalk at a loud noise" is, scientifically, exactly this. Stephen Porges's Polyvagal Theory completes the picture: our nervous system, without our being aware of it, scans the environment at every moment for "am I safe?" — he calls this "neuroception" (Porges, 2011). In a classroom where you are always on guard, this scanning turns into chronic alarm. And then there is attachment. John Bowlby and Mary Ainsworth's attachment theory shows that a child needs a stable, predictable bond in order to develop securely. In a child uprooted every year and pressed into new soil, the belief "there's no point in forming a bond, it'll break anyway" can take hold; this turns into a pattern that repeats later in adult relationships. Your sentence, "I grew my roots inward," is in fact the extremely clever but lonely response a child gives to instability. If left unaddressed — possible bad outcomes Shame recorded in the body, if left unaddressed, hardens into the life-feeling of "Being different = Being wrong." A possible course: lasting low self-worth, pervasive social anxiety, a chronic fear of "giving oneself away," and an unspoken reservoir of anger — a reservoir that later either spills outward (outbursts, conflicts, a tendency toward violence) or inward (complete withdrawal, depression, identity collapse). This is very common in neurodivergent adults diagnosed late in life. The voice of "I'm wrong" internalized in childhood steps onto the stage again and again in adulthood — in relationships, at work, in parenting. The first punch feels like salvation — but let's be honest, it's only the second-best solution: it stops the threat, but it doesn't dress the wound. The best comes later. Paths of healing — several doors There is no single way to heal this wound; there are several doors, and all of them are built on "bringing shame out of silence and into the light": • Somatic approaches (Peter Levine – Somatic Experiencing): Since the body holds the trauma, healing too passes through the body. Grounding exercises — feet planted firmly on the ground, breath felt, the vagus nerve stimulated by long exhalations — pull the nervous system out of alarm and into "safe" mode (consistent with Polyvagal Theory). • EMDR: A method with strong evidence for trauma, which allows those old, frozen memories (the desk, the look, the first punch) to be reprocessed in the brain and filed away on the "past" shelf. • Compassion-Focused Therapy (Paul Gilbert) and self-compassion (Kristin Neff): Learning to respond to that bully working for free in your head not as an enemy, but as if speaking to a frightened child. Turning the sentence "I'm wrong" into "I was a child doing the best he could in a hard situation." • Schema therapy / CBT: Re-examining, with today's adult mind, the "defectiveness/shame schema" that took hold in childhood; updating that program left over from 1998. • Narrative therapy and expressive writing (Pennebaker): Shame cannot withstand a story that is told. Writing down what you've lived through — just like this book — helps the brain reframe that memory; Pennebaker's research shows that writing improves both mood and immune function (Pennebaker, 1997). • Reframing: Shame is not my flaw, it is other people's mirror — they were reflecting onto me their fear of the unfamiliar. And the drawings in the margins of the notebook, the thing I once thought was an escape, are now a recognized strength: someone who builds whole worlds and systems is not "broken," but creative. Tuncay's bridge For years I thought the child in that classroom was the one who was wrong. When I read Eisenberger's finding that "social rejection is processed in the same place as physical pain," I understood why that stone in my stomach was as heavy as a real stone — so I wasn't exaggerating after all, my brain really had recorded it as pain. Van der Kolk's sentence "the body keeps the score" explained why I still catch the smell of chalk together with a sudden noise; that smell isn't an illusion, it's a recording. When I read Bowlby, I found the answer to the question "why did I never put down roots": a child whose roots are torn out learns to give up rooting, because that's the path that hurts least. I lived through all of this for years without knowing the names; then, as I read, I saw that each one had a name, an explanation, and I felt a strange relief. I now remember that first punch not with shame, but with compassion: it was the only tool that child had in his hand. Today I have words in my hand; that day he had a fist. Both were saying the same thing: "I am here, and I refuse to disappear." Sources: B. van der Kolk, The Body Keeps the Score (2014); N. Eisenberger, studies on social pain (Science, 2003); S. I no longer need a fist to say "no"; I have my words, and most of the time they're enough. When the old shame rises, I now recognize it for what it is: a program left over from 1998, one that should long since have been updated; not a threat, but a visitor. What remains is the body's memory — the smell of that classroom at loud noises. Perhaps I'll never fully get over it, and I've come to accept that. But it no longer runs me. Then write below where you could set a small boundary this week — a "no," a "this isn't good for me," a "I don't want to be spoken to this way." Just one concrete place, just one concrete sentence. == ection0014 [] class="body0" xmlns:epub="http://www.idpf.org/2007/ops"> Chapter 4 – Father, Fear, and Respect My father… was one man out in the world. At home, he was someone else entirely. It was as if I had two fathers. One lived outside the door, the other inside it. The one outside was sunny; the one inside was rainy. And I could never know in advance which father I would find — so I stayed braced for both at once. What heavier burden can be laid on a child's shoulders than this: to be ready, in the same instant, for both moods of the same face. Out in the world, I would watch him. I would draw back into a corner and study him closely. How he spoke with people — leaning in, listening, patting a shoulder, laughing out loud. His voice was warm; the words took on a softness as they left his mouth, as if he tucked a little honey into every sentence. His laughter was contagious; if he laughed at one end of a table, the other end laughed too. The woman next door would say, "What a courteous man." Shopkeepers would step out from behind the counter just to shake his hand. Everyone loved him. Everyone. Inner voice: "Why isn't he like this with us? Who is this man? Where does that laughing man outside go, that he gets lost somewhere on the way home? Or is the real one the one inside, and I'm the one being fooled by the one outside?" To see that warmth out in the world and then come home to its absence… For a child, there is nothing more bewildering. Because a bad father is easy to understand; but a father who is good outside and distant inside is impossible to understand. It meant he was capable of it. It meant the warmth was there in him. It just never reached me. And a child's mind reads this only one way: "So the fault is mine." But the moment the apartment door clicked shut, everything changed. The Sound of the Door The turn of the key in the lock… Click. I will never forget that sound. To this day I still sometimes hear it in my dreams — and when I wake, my heart has already started running before I've even opened my eyes. So the brain keeps that watch even while it sleeps. Before that sound arrived, there was a period of preparation in the house. When the hour hand reached a certain point, the density of the air would shift; it was as if the pressure in the room rose. My mother would busy herself as if clearing the table, we would withdraw to our rooms, our ears turned toward the hallway — the whole house tensing toward the moment that was coming. Then, outside, the muffled drone of the elevator. Then footsteps, a pause at the door, the jingle of keys. And then: Click. At that "click" my body would change automatically. As if a button had been pressed. My heart would suddenly race, pounding wildly in my chest — like a fist hammering my ribs, saying "get out, run, hide." My palms would sweat; my fingers would go cold, damp on the inside. A heavy, cold stone would settle into my stomach; I can still picture that stone, I remember it down to the kilo. My breath would go shallow, as if someone were sitting on my chest. My eyes would lock onto the door — involuntarily, the way a rabbit locks onto the rustle in the bushes. And time… time would slow down. Seconds grew heavy, turned into hours. Those three or four seconds before the door opened are the longest hours of my childhood. Inner voice: "What's going to happen now? Did I do something wrong? Was I too loud? Did I forget to tidy my room? Which father is coming today — the one who laughs, or the silent one? What can I do to keep the weather sunny? Maybe if I'm very good, maybe if I make myself invisible, maybe if I don't even breathe…" What a sorrow it is when a child pours all of his intelligence into a single thing: becoming invisible. In class I struggled to make sense of math, but at home I was a genius at reading the pressure. From how my father took off his shoes, where he threw his jacket, the tone of his "hello," I could map out the entire evening. A child's genius is never wasted; it is simply spent in the wrong place — on survival. I never once felt at ease near my father. Even sitting side by side, there was an invisible pane of glass between us. There was respect — yes, the respect was complete, flawless, without a single crack. But love… I never felt it. I knew respect, because respect lived in the body as tension. I did not know love, because love lives in the body as release — and not once, beside him, was I ever able to release. A Moment One day I gathered my courage. I hadn't planned it. It simply rose up in me — maybe that day I'd seen the laughing father from outside, maybe the child in me had hoped, for a moment, "maybe this time." We were in the living room. He was sitting in an armchair. The light came from the side, half his face lit, half in shadow. Something stirred in me; I wanted just a crumb of that warmth too. Just a single crumb. I went over to him, gently. I still remember how slowly my feet moved across the carpet. I only wanted to lay my hand on his shoulder. It wasn't a big thing. Not a sentence, not a gift, not an achievement — just a hand, a shoulder, a moment. Just to be close. Just to say, "I'm here, you're here too, let's be together for a moment." My hand was in the air, about to come to rest on his shoulder. Inner voice: "Maybe he'll smile. Maybe he'll put his hand over mine. Maybe just once, just this once…" He looked at me. His eyes were stern. Cold and calm. And he said: "I am your father. I am not your friend. Watch how you behave." My hand stayed in the air. Then it sank quietly, fell to my side. That sentence drove into me like a knife — it didn't even hurt at first, the way very sharp knives don't; the pain came afterward. Silence. The light in the room was the same, the furniture was the same, but something had shifted permanently. Inner voice: "So… I was too much. What I wanted was too much. Even wanting love was too much. Closeness is a crime, and I didn't know it. Never again. Never again will I reach out this hand to anyone." Something broke in me in that moment. A small shard of glass. It made a sound, but no one heard it — only I heard that little crack, inside, somewhere behind my breastbone. And from that day on, for years, whenever I wanted to be close to someone, I remembered the way my hand had hung in the air. Once the body learns "don't reach out," it does not easily forget. Inner voice: "What if he'd said something else that day? What if he'd laid his hand on my shoulder, what if he'd said, 'come here, son'? That single sentence would surely have made me into someone entirely different. But then again, maybe if he had been a father who could say that sentence, I wouldn't have become the man writing this book. Strange — the place where the wound is is also the place where the pen sinks in." Love My father never once said, "I love you." Not a single time. As a child, as a teenager, as I grew — not once. He never said, "I'm proud of you." Even when he saw a grade, an accomplishment, an effort, those words never left his mouth. I grew up waiting for those words; the way one waits for rain in a desert. Sometimes my mother would step in and say, "Your father loves you very much." Maybe to console me, maybe because it was really true. Inner voice: "Then why doesn't he say it? If he loves me, why is the word so expensive? Does love run out when you speak it, does it shrink? If it's there inside, why doesn't it come out? Or am I the one who can't hear it — maybe he says it in another language, and I just don't know that language?" Years later there was an answer I learned through my mother, a sentence that belonged to that generation: "If we say it, we'll spoil them." The first time I heard it, I froze. So the issue wasn't that they didn't love me; the issue was that they saw love as a danger, and saw me as something to be protected from that danger. In their eyes, love was like water — too much of it would drown you. And yet I was withering from thirst. Inner voice: "Does love really spoil you? Or is it the opposite — is it the absence of love that cripples you? And what was that thing they called respect, anyway? Was it a bowing of the head born of fear, or an admiration that comes from within? Because I bowed my head, but inside me there was no admiration, only a trembling. Is it fear that creates respect?" My grandfather was different. He was an entirely different man. He always said one sentence, whose meaning I only grasped years later: "Respect doesn't come from a cigarette." What he meant was this: he would not accept a hollow respect born only of fear, stuck to the surface, empty inside. We didn't smoke our cigarettes in front of him — but not out of respect, out of fear of being scolded. And it was precisely this kind of "respect" my grandfather refused; it was as if he were saying, "don't pretend not to smoke for my sake; respect me from within, or don't respect me at all." Two men of the same family were teaching me two utterly different lessons: one was packaging fear as respect, the other was unwrapping that package and saying, "this is empty." Inner voice: "How can this be? The same blood, the same table, the same roof — two people this different. One tells me, 'know your distance,' the other tells me, 'be genuine.' Who do I take after, who do I want to take after? I think both of them are inside me; one is the father who raises through fear, the other the grandfather who teaches authenticity. Which one will speak through my mouth, to my own child?" Maybe in those days, without realizing it, I had begun to make a choice. I had chosen to take in not my father's sentence, but my grandfather's. ★ Respect born of fear is not respect; it is only fear, well disguised. Control and Punishment My father controlled everything. Money? We never knew what there was, where it came from or where it went; he was the household's sole and invisible center. Decisions? No one was consulted. There was no debate, no exchange of ideas, no "what do you think?" He decided, we obeyed. The house was not a democracy but a silent monarchy; and the rules were unwritten, so you only learned which one you'd broken, and when, once the punishment landed. When my grades were bad, there was punishment. But this time it wasn't a beating — it was something more insidious. A blank sheet of paper. Ten pages. Writing the same sentence over and over. I would sit down at the desk. Before me, those blindingly white, seemingly endless pages. I would pick up the pen. Write. Write. Write. On the first page the hand is still fresh, the letters come out neat. By the third page the space between the fingers grows hot. By the fifth page that red line the pen leaves on the side of my index finger appears, then turns purple. My hand would ache; a throbbing pain rising up from the wrist. The pen dug into my fingers. The letters grew uglier and uglier, the lines slid downward, my eyes welled up. But I wasn't allowed to stop. To stop meant to start over. Inner voice: "I am wrong. I am not enough. I have to do better. I have to work harder, make fewer mistakes, be more invisible. Maybe one day, if I'm good enough, then… then what? Then will he love me? Or even then, will he just raise the bar higher?" When you make a child write the same sentence a hundred times, you aren't making him write the content of that sentence — you're making him write a belief: "You are a mistake that needs correcting." That belief rises off the pages and seeps into the bone, and years later, even when no one is punishing you, you keep punishing yourself. There is a bitter irony here, one I only noticed as an adult, and when I noticed it I laughed for a long time — a sorrowful laugh, but a real one: they punished me with writing. And writing is the very thing that heals me today. The pen I'm writing these lines with is the grandchild of that pen from the punishment. My tool was born of the punishment; my medicine, of the wound. Life sometimes gives you the medicine disguised as the wound — the packaging so terrible that it takes years to see the gift inside. Inner voice: "Imagine if I could go to that child crying at the desk and tell him: 'One day this hand, of your own free will, will write through whole nights — and that writing will save you.' Would he laugh at me, would he cry? Maybe he'd just fall silent. But maybe, just maybe, for a moment, the pen in his hand would feel to him not like a punishment, but like a promise." Two Truths The Table and the Field There was also the dinner table. Today I know: because of my autism, I couldn't eat everything — some textures, some smells were too much for my body, and my stomach pushed them back. But at our table that was called "being fussy." My father had a very deep, frightening voice; and a gaze that went straight through you, that pinned you where you stood — a lethal look. "No one gets up before the plate is empty." I would stand over the plate, tears running, snot running, trying to force down what my body refused. I wasn't allowed to get up — because getting up would have made him angrier. That is how I remember it: a child, a plate, and across from him, a fear. Inner voice: "My body says 'no,' but 'no' is forbidden. So is the body wrong — or am I wrong?" Then the scale tipped the other way. That fear lying between me and food flung me in the opposite direction: I began to eat too much, I gained weight. Now I was fat. And one day, without anyone asking me, they signed me up at a football club — the very club where my friends were. My father came to one of the games. In front of all my friends he yelled at me, called me "fatso," belittled me before everyone's eyes. Was I eight, was I ten — I don't remember exactly. But one thing I remember with certainty: from that day on, I never had anything to do with football again. ★ If you shame a child not for a game but for his own body, he carries that shame for a lifetime. When my father came home, I would tremble. This was not a reflex — that is, not something I was born with, but something acquired afterward. It was a conditioning. Just like the dogs that drool at the sound of a bell, my body too had learned to go on alert at a sound, at the turn of a key. My body would slip into "danger" mode on its own; without my deciding, without my wanting it. Long before the brain could say, "wait, maybe there's no danger," the body had already prepared to flee. And yet — the strangest, the most inexplicable, the most bewildering part: I loved my father. Truly. I loved him from that pure place where a child is meant to love his father — sincerely, deeply. But at the same time I was afraid of him. Both at once, in the same chest, looking at the same face. Inner voice: "How is this possible? To both love the same person and be afraid of him… Aren't love and fear opposites? If I'm afraid, I shouldn't love; if I love, I shouldn't be afraid. But both are there. So there must be something wrong inside me. Am I broken? Or is this something that lives inside everyone, and no one has been able to say?" When I learned my father had cancer, I cried for hours. I shut myself in my room, closed the door, and wept — so much that I couldn't even fully tell what I was weeping for. I was crying for the man I'd been afraid of. I was crying for the arms that had never once held me. In that moment, through the tears, a naked and merciless truth surfaced: my father had never truly been a father to me. I never received that role, that warmth, that "I'm here for you." And yet I loved him. I love him today too. I think that's what I was weeping for: I wasn't weeping for the father I was about to lose, but for the father I never had. A person grieves most for the thing he never got to live. Inner voice: Maybe this was his greatest legacy: he showed me how not to be a father. He gave me one of the most precious lessons of my life, by the most painful road. And it was precisely this that I wanted to do differently. If I ever had children of my own — I promised, in that room, through the tears — I would tell them "I love you" every single day. I would be there for them like a friend; they would be able to lay their hands on my shoulder, and I on theirs. And they wouldn't even have to keep waiting for the "click" of the door to feel safe. For them, a door would be just a door.

== ection0016 [] class="body0" xmlns:epub="http://www.idpf.org/2007/ops"> Therapist's Note – Fear-Based Attachment What is happening here: When a parent is at once the source of both safety and threat, the child's attachment system is plunged into an unsolvable paradox. The work of John Bowlby, the founder of attachment theory, and his student Mary Ainsworth shows that a child is biologically programmed to take refuge in the parent in order to survive — protection is expected to be there. But if the danger is right there too, in the very same person, then the harbor one would flee to is also the source of the storm. The attachment researcher Mary Main calls this "fright without solution": neither approaching nor withdrawing works for the child, because the thing he fears is the same as the thing he wants to take refuge in. The result is traumatic learning: the body binds a completely neutral stimulus on its own — the "click" of the key — to full alarm. Love and fear fuse into a single inseparable emotion and stick to the same face. "To both love him and fear him" is not a contradiction; it is the mathematically necessary outcome of this equation. Where it comes from — body and evolution There is a very old explanation for why your heart starts running at that "click." Deep in the brain, the amygdala is the alarm bell that has kept us alive for millions of years; Bessel van der Kolk calls it "the brain's smoke detector" ( The Body Keeps the Score , 2014). This detector does not reason, it works fast: the instant it hears a sound it has associated with danger, it sets the body in motion before you can think. Your chest pounding at the sound of the key, your palms sweating, that stone settling into your stomach — all of it is the bodily signature of the amygdala's "flee or freeze" command. Stephen Porges's Polyvagal Theory takes this a step further: our body, without our awareness, scans the environment moment by moment, asking "am I safe?"; Porges calls this neuroception (Porges, 2011). A loving gaze draws the nervous system into "safe" mode, while a cold and threatening tone pulls it into "defense" mode. For years your body learned to read, from your father's footsteps and the tone of his "hello," whether that evening would be safe — this was not "oversensitivity" but a radar working flawlessly to keep you alive. Just as in Pavlov's famous experiment (the bell–saliva conditioning), your nervous system too paired the "click" with danger; this is classical conditioning, not a weakness of character. And as the title of van der Kolk's book says, the body keeps the score : even when that fear is erased from consciousness, your muscles, your stomach, and your heart go on remembering it for years. If left unaddressed — possible bad outcomes If this bond is left unprocessed, the brain permanently encodes the following equation: "Closeness = Danger." In adulthood this often steers a life silently, unnoticed. The likely course: disproportionate fear before authority figures and a swallowing of one's own voice; pulling back precisely when real closeness is about to form (because closeness triggers the alarm); great difficulty expressing emotions, especially love; either an excessive need for control or, on the contrary, an erasing of the self. The most insidious of all is this: a person, without realizing it, chooses partners who recreate that hot-cold oscillation he knew in childhood, because he finds them "familiar"; instead of an unknown peace, he mistakes the known unrest for safety. The familiar feels more comfortable than the healthy — and the cycle repeats itself. (Spoiler: this is exactly what happens in Chapter 6.) Paths of healing — not one road, but several doors Here is the good news: if "Closeness = Danger" is a learned bond, then, like everything learned, it can be rewritten. There are several doors: • First, noticing — without shame: Catching the automatic fear response like an observer. "Aha — the old fire alarm went off, but there's no fire." This single sentence separates the response from your identity. • Internal Family Systems (IFS – Richard Schwartz): Seeing that trembling child inside you, that part trying to make itself invisible, not as an enemy but as a "part" that protected you for years; turning toward it with compassion. Those inner voices in your head are precisely the working ground of IFS. • Somatic Experiencing (Peter Levine) and EMDR: Trauma is resolved not with logic but with the body. Slowly discharging that frozen "flee" energy stuck in the body; EMDR, for its part, is a powerful method for reducing the emotional charge of an old memory. • "Inner parent" work and Compassion Focused Therapy (Paul Gilbert – CFT): The secure bond that was missing back then, a person today gives to himself — by developing a soothing, dependable, compassionate inner voice. Learning to answer the sentence "I am wrong" the way you'd speak to a friend. • A very concrete repair: Consciously, once, saying to someone you trust, "I feel safe with you." This sentence is not just words in the air; it is a direct correction made to the nervous system — it offers the body new evidence that "closeness was safe this time." If you repeat it enough, "Closeness = Danger" is slowly rewritten as "Closeness = Safe." Tuncay's bridge For years I didn't know why that "click" took over my body; I just thought it was a "weakness," and I was ashamed of it. When I read Porges, I felt relieved: it turned out that my freezing at the sound of my father's key was not a lack of character but neuroception — my body's effort to protect me. Van der Kolk's sentence, "the body keeps the score," explained why that stone in my stomach was still there decades later; so the body doesn't forget, but it can relearn. When I read Bowlby and Main, I found the answer to the question, "Am I broken because I both loved him and feared him?": No. I wasn't broken; I was just a child wedged into an equation with no solution, and in that equation, the inability to separate love from fear was not a mistake but the only possible outcome. IFS taught me to turn to that trembling child and say, "I'm here now, I'm the one opening the door." And the most beautiful part: before I ever read about that "inner parent" work in theory, I had already practiced it in life — by telling my own daughters "I love you" every single day. It turned out that what I was giving my children, I was at the same time giving to that child crying at the old desk. I took the science and laid it over my own life, and that map turned the sentence "I am broken" into the sentence "I am a system that can be explained, and repaired." Sources: J. Bowlby, A Secure Base (1988); M. Ainsworth & M. Main, research on attachment and "fright without solution"; B. van der Kolk, The Body Keeps the Score (2014); S. Porges, The Polyvagal Theory (2011); R. Schwartz, Internal Family Systems; P. Levine, Waking the Tiger (Somatic Experiencing); P. Gilbert, Compassion Focused Therapy. Where I Stand Today I made an inner peace with my father — not by endorsing what happened, but by being able to hold the two side by side: both the love and the wound, in the same hand, without one canceling out the other. I still sometimes hear the "click" in my dreams; that old radar never shuts off completely, its volume just turns down. But awake, I am no longer that trembling child — or rather, that child is still inside me, but now there's an adult beside him, someone holding his hand. The thing I truly overcame was this question: "Am I broken because I both loved him and feared him?" No. I was just a child wedged into an impossible equation, and I did the best I could. And that promise in the inner voice — to tell my own children "I love you" — came true, every single day. My daughters' first word to me was never "click." It was "Daddy." What my father could not give me, I gave to them; and strangely, in giving it to them, I gave it to that old child too. I broke the cycle in the very place where I had lived it. "Into a house that fear has entered, love comes only as a tenant." — Anatolian proverb Never finished, always becoming — that is how I go, until my breath runs out.

== ection0018 [] class="body0" xmlns:epub="http://www.idpf.org/2007/ops"> Chapter 5 – Escape, Growing Up Too Soon, and the First Rebellion Then I grew up. Early. I’d make a decision and dive into it headfirst, thinking about the consequences afterward. Every time I started to truly put down roots somewhere, they pulled the ground out from under me. My family had already left before the exams. Throughout the exams I lived nearly a month alone in a boarding house. The faucet on the sink dripped; in the silence of the night that drip ticked like a giant clock: tick… tick… tick. The wallpaper had curled away from one corner and yellowed. The light of a gas station fell through the window; a bluish, cold glow that drew a perfectly straight rectangle on the ceiling. In the mornings I would wake in that light, get dressed, and go to the exam. My stomach clenched — not from the exam, but from something else: from the emptiness of a morning where no one said "good luck." The certificate came by mail. Inside a small envelope. I opened the door, took the envelope, set it on the table. For a while I just looked at it. The paper of the envelope was thin, slightly rough; it left a formal, cold feeling between my fingers. Inside was my first place. And around me, there was not a single face to tell. Inner voice: "Congratulations, Tuncay. You did it. But no one saw." — And another voice answers from within: "So what if they didn't see? You know." But that night, even that answer wasn't enough. Because a fourteen-year-old child doesn't want what he knows; he wants what someone else sees. Not applause — just a glance. A "well done." A hand to be laid on his shoulder. Eight Weeks of Waiting Summer vacation began. Eight weeks. I was home. Nothing to do. No plan. There was only one promise: "You'll go to the Turkish youth camp." Every week it was postponed. "We're going this week." "Next week for sure." And then? Canceled. For eight weeks I went around and around inside the house. The walls were closing in on me — really, physically. I would walk from one room to another for no reason. Kitchen. Living room. Hallway. Kitchen again. I opened and closed the refrigerator, not out of hunger, but to touch something with my hand. Time grew heavy. That hot, stagnant air of summer afternoons hung suspended in the room; the curtains never stirred, and the air left a stale taste in my mouth. The hours simply would not pass. The tick-tock of the clock on the wall seemed to slow down more and more; the gap between two seconds stretched longer and longer. I would look out the window and see children passing on bicycles. That faint whir of tires on asphalt, someone's burst of laughter, the double click-clack of a bell. Life was on the other side of the glass. I sat inside and waited. The glass was that thin, invisible wall separating me from life — I could see everything, I could touch nothing. Inner voice: "Why is my whole life just waiting? Other kids are on vacation, going places… Why am I always stuck in 'later'?" — And right behind it, that sly thought: "Maybe 'later' will never come. Maybe 'later' is their way of saying 'never.'" I was suffocating. Really. Something inside me clenched, like a fist pressing down on the center of my chest, and it never let up. I would breathe, but the air seemed to reach only halfway into my lungs and snag there. My jaw clenched without my noticing. Then my head would ache. Fourteen — the age when the body begins to scream what it cannot say. Inner voice: "What if this camp were real? What if, just once, a single time, a promise were kept? I would get on the bus, with other kids, kids who spoke my own language… Maybe one of them would ask, 'Who are you?' and I would tell them." Then my mind shuts that imagined scene down — because imagining something that won't happen too beautifully hurts twice as much when it never comes. Work as a Door to Escape Then came work — and with it, the first real door opening to the outside. Because working was the most legitimate way to escape the house. If a person is working, no one can blame them. A payslip is an alibi. A shift schedule is the polite way of saying "leave me alone." My father rented me a basement place, a single room, an hour away from home. It was cold. The kitchen was overrun with mice. At night I would hear those tiny scratching sounds inside the wall — scratch, scratch, then silence, then again. The smell of damp, that moist, mineral odor of concrete, was always in the air. There was a single bulb, bare, hanging from the ceiling; its light was yellowish and merciless, never fully lighting any corner, splitting them all with shadow. It wasn't presented as a kind of freedom. No one said, "here, this is your own home." But for me, that's exactly what it was. A freedom with mice, with cold, with a bare bulb. And I preferred it to a warm but suffocating home. Inner voice: "This place is terrible. This place is mine. For the first time there's a door I can close behind me, and no one will open and close it for me. The mice? Even they aren't telling me I'm a burden here." Here I have to add something that has stayed inside me to this day. I had been accepted into an art school — my father swept it aside with the back of his hand: "What will you do there, it's pointless." That sentence. That "pointless." With a single word, the curtain is drawn over a child's courage to show something inside himself. Later I won a place in Radiology, in the field of medicine. I was one of six taken from a thousand applicants. Six in a thousand. Just think — six in a thousand. But the program had tuition, and my father wasn't keen. I didn't want to be a burden to them, so I didn't accept. I was tired of being a burden. Tired to the bone. Inner voice: "Maybe I'm not worth someone investing in me. Maybe it's easier to want nothing at all." — Because wanting was dangerous. If you wanted, you could be refused. If you never wanted, no one could say no. ★ If a child has learned to want nothing, it is not because he doesn't want, but because he is afraid of wanting too much. Even today, I still open that radiology letter in my mind. And my mind, for years, simply will not close that scene; each time it replays it with a different ending. Inner voice: "What if I had accepted? Would I now be a man in a white coat, walking hospital corridors? What if my father hadn't said 'tuition,' what if I had, just once, found the courage to say 'I want this'? Would I have been alone in that life too, or would someone there have seen me? Or would that road have broken me even more?" These questions don't want answers. They just turn, and turn — like the ghosts of a life that never happened. In the end I always arrive at the same place: I didn't choose that door, but the door I didn't choose brought me to exactly here, to the man writing these pages. Talking to Machines My first real job was in a factory. We made gearboxes. The smell of iron, oily floors, vibrating machines. When I walked through that door in the morning, the way the air grew heavy — the mingled smell of cold metal, cutting oil, warmed-up electric motors filled my nose. The floor was coated with a film of oil; as I stepped, the sole of my shoe would stick slightly, then release. The fluorescent lights on the ceiling hissed. And the machines — that constant, deep hum came up through the soles of my feet into my bones. People got tired. I listened. When a machine's sound changed, I noticed it immediately. The others lost it inside that hum; for me, the hum was an orchestra, and when a violin slipped out of tune within it, I heard it. The vibration, the rhythm, the brief hesitation — all of it told me something. One machine would say "I'm fine," another would say "something inside me is wearing out." No one had taught me this. It came on its own. I would lay my palm on the metal and read the vibration in my hand. The machine spoke to me, and I listened. I didn't know it then, but I got along with systems far better than with people. Because machines don't shout. They aren't capricious. They don't say "yes" one day and "no" the next. There is a cause-and-effect relationship. If you press here, something happens there — every time. Same cause, same result. That gave me trust. People were a riddle to me; machines were an answer. I trained as an industrial mechanic. Inner voice: "Finally, a place where rules carry meaning. 'Do this' — I do it. 'You did it wrong' — I can ask why. At home that was never possible. At home, 'wrong' meant 'be quiet.' Here, 'wrong' is a door opening onto the next right thing." But the system had a price: it only saw the worker in me. I had ideas. I noticed mistakes — an unnecessary step in a process, an illogical flow in a layout. I thought of other solutions, rebuilding the whole line inside my head. No one cared. "Just do your job." That sentence was familiar. It was my father's "pointless" dressed in a work apron. Only the voice had changed; the content was the same: "You're not meant to see." I learned incredibly fast, and got bored just as fast. Once I understood something, repeating it felt like torture; my brain wanted a new stimulus, a new riddle, a new leap. Responsibility was handed out according to a plan and a clock — but I was timeless, learning as an autodidact, in leaps, at my own rhythm. I might learn a subject in one breath at three in the morning and then not show up for the class the next morning. They couldn't cope with this. Everything had to march like soldiers: many rules, strict order, fixed hours. I couldn't do it. It seemed illogical to me. When I asked, "Why exactly at nine? Why in this order?" the answer was "because that's how it is" — and "because that's how it is" is the one food my brain cannot digest. I took a lot of sick leave right up to the exam. Inner voice: "The problem isn't me, the problem is this mold. They're trying to fit me into a box, but I'm not box-shaped. Their mistake isn't thinking the box is wrong — it's thinking I'm the one who's wrong." And the funniest part: I was supposed to attend school for three and a half years. I went for one year and skipped the rest. Even so, I sat the exam. And I graduated top of the class. The teachers were left speechless. I won't forget the expression on one of their faces: half astonished, half angry, a look that said "how is this possible?" The AuDHD brain sometimes works like this: fails attendance, wins the exam. Doesn't fit the system's form, but answers the system's question better than anyone. Like an Adult at Sixteen The money I earned was mine. This — at sixteen — was dizzying. To hold in my palm the paper of money I had earned myself was a strange, intimate kind of power. I bought my own shoes. My own food. My own silence. Even silence was something that could be bought now — a room where no one said "do this, don't do that," with my own money. But I didn't get my childhood back. I had already let it go. While my peers were playing games, I was calculating shifts. While they talked about the score of a match, I was working out the end-of-month accounts. This, too, became a mask: "the hardworking boy," "matured early." People praised it. "Look how grown up he is, bless him." But no one asked: why did this child have to escape so early? A child growing up too soon is not an achievement; more often it is a wound — just a wound wrapped in a pretty word. Inner voice: "They think I'm 'mature.' I'm not mature. I'm just afraid, and fear silences a person early. Maturity is something you choose; mine is a garment forced onto me — too big for my body, too tight for my soul." I drank alcohol for the first time. At a friend's house. The bottle was cold in my hand — that sweat-like dampness of the glass on my palm. The first sip burned my throat; bitter, sharp, a taste I wanted to recoil from. With the second, the world softened a little — the corners blurred just slightly, that fist in my chest loosened a little. With the third I thought, "I don't care." And there it was — that "I don't care" — the first place a child who until that moment had cared about everything far too much finally took a breath. Inner voice: "So this is all it takes. So that thing that's always clenched can loosen, just for a moment. This is a dangerous discovery — because once you learn there's a button for loosening, you have to find a reason not to press it." Inner voice: "Rules, waiting, 'later'… enough. From now on I set my own rules." My father's rules were clear and rigid. But I no longer bowed to them. I raised my voice — that rising tone of my own voice was foreign to me; it both frightened me and gave me strength. I slammed doors. That slam exploded inside the house like a gunshot; the silence that followed was even louder. For the first time I said "Enough." And when I said that word, a door locked inside me for years cracked open with a single creak. Inner voice: "If I stay here, I'll be destroyed. This house is killing me slowly." — I'm not saying this for the sake of drama. A fourteen-year-old body really does feel it: a slow drowning, an invisible pressure. And when a body feels this, escaping is not a rebellion, it's a survival instinct. At night I would lie in bed and stare at the ceiling. From outside came the sound of cars, the whistle of a distant train — that whistle, drawn out and far away, a sound saying "there are other places." The sound of a train is the sound of everything that can leave. "One day I'll go. Far from this house, these rules, this endless waiting," I would think. On the darkness of the ceiling I drew the map of that departure — not knowing where; but far away, definitely far away. I never really lived a childhood. I went straight to surviving. Some children grow up; some learn straight away to endure. Inner voice: "Am I a child, or already an adult? I'm fourteen but I feel like thirty. Or the exact opposite: at thirty I'm still fourteen. Both are inside me — one a child who never played, one a man who tired far too soon. The two are constantly talking to each other, and neither ever fully falls silent." A decision ripened inside me: "I will be myself." The only problem was this: I didn't know who I was. I had set a direction but had no compass. I was walking toward a "myself," toward someone — but I had never seen that someone's face. Inner voice: "I don't know, but I'll find out. I won't stop until I do. I won't give up. Like Goku: even if I'm lying on the ground, I get back up." That summer, those eight weeks, that repeated school year, my first room with mice, the first sip that burned my throat, the first "Enough" — all of it was preparing me for something. For escape. For rebellion. And, most of all: for the search for myself. I didn't know it then, but all of this was not a loss, it was a beginning. It's just that the face of beginnings is never beautiful at the moment they begin. Therapist's Note – Premature Independence and Identity Fragmentation What is happening here: A child who cannot find a stable, safe environment shifts early into survival mode. When constant relocations, a lack of emotional support, and an AuDHD brain that already struggles to hold direction and motivation come together, forced premature maturation is the result. In developmental psychology this is called a state close to "parentification": the child is forced to take on a level of responsibility and self-sufficiency far beyond his years — but he pays the price for it inside, as a childhood never lived. Work becomes the door to escape, alcohol the first valve, and a distorted sense of time ("waiting" grows heavy in the body) becomes a permanent condition. Important: this is not a "difficult adolescent." This is a nervous system doing what it must to get by. Always sitting near the door in class, pacing from room to room at home — these are not laziness or moodiness, but a body on high alert searching for a way out. The relationship formed with machines is also striking. A child being able to "read" machines better than people is no coincidence; it is a classic feature of the autistic side: systems are predictable, people are not. The talent that will later turn into a profession appears here early. Where it comes from — body and evolution That "fist pressing down on the center of the chest," that feeling of suffocation, is not random. A nervous system that is constantly relocated, that can never get a clear answer to the question "when am I safe?", remains chronically on alert. In Stephen Porges's Polyvagal Theory , this is called impaired "neuroception": the body, without our awareness, constantly scans the environment and asks, "can I put down roots here?" (Porges, 2011). When the answer is always "maybe, but soon you'll leave again," the system never learns to rest. In Bessel van der Kolk's words, "the body keeps the score" : what cannot be spoken is recorded in the muscles, in the clench of the stomach, in the tightened jaw ( The Body Keeps the Score , 2014). Your jaw clenching at fourteen, your headaches, are precisely this record. The "heaviness" of waiting is also a real neurological phenomenon. In the ADHD brain, the perception of time (linked to "temporal discounting" and low dopaminergic stimulation) works differently: time without stimulation, without reward, stretches out in a way that is physically painful (Barkley's work on ADHD and executive functions). So those eight weeks "simply not passing" was not an exaggeration; it was the direct experience of the brain's hunger for stimulation. Turning to machines is the other side of this: the autistic brain's tendency toward "systemizing" (Simon Baron-Cohen's "empathizing–systemizing" framework) finds a deep sense of safety in a predictable world of cause and effect. That a machine doesn't shout, isn't capricious — for a child coming from a chaotic home, this is, in the fullest sense, a refuge. Learning to refuse, to want nothing, is connected to attachment and emotional neglect . As John Bowlby and Mary Ainsworth showed, if a child cannot develop trust that his needs will be met, he learns to shut down his needs in the name of self-protection. Jonice Webb calls this Childhood Emotional Neglect ( Running on Empty , 2012): the equation "if I don't want, I won't be refused." Saying "no" to the radiology letter was not a logical error — it was a protective reflex a child had learned over years. If left unaddressed — possible bad outcomes The real risk is identity fragmentation: the brain turns "I can't put down roots anywhere" into "I don't know who I am," and carries this into adulthood as a chronic feeling of "Where am I? What am I doing?" On top of this comes an increased risk of addiction — the discovery of the first loosening button is rarely the last escape route; once you can say "I don't care," becoming attached to a substance, to an escape, becomes easy. Self-harming tendencies and a chronic belief in one's own worthlessness can be added to this. The rejected Radiology is symptomatic here: someone who has learned "I am a burden" says no even to his own chances in order to protect himself — and years later the regret of "what if I had done it?" settles into the mind as rumination (replaying the same scene again and again with different endings). If left unaddressed, the stubbornness of "I will be myself" cannot find a direction and can turn into drifting, into constantly starting over, into being unable to complete anything. Paths of healing — several doors At this point there are a few doors that help: • Narrative therapy: Rewriting the story of "the child who had to grow up too soon, whom no one saw." You can't change the events, but you can rewrite their meaning: instead of "I was a burden," "I was a clever child who shut down his needs in order to protect himself." This book itself is a practice of narrative therapy. • Internal Family Systems (IFS – Richard Schwartz): The dialogue between the "child who never played" and the "man who tired far too soon" inside you is exactly IFS's field of work. Neither is the enemy; one is a wounded "exile," one a "manager" protecting him. The goal is for a compassionate "Self" to take charge of both. • Expressive writing (Pennebaker): Putting into words those eight weeks, that boarding-house room, that "no one saw" moment. Pennebaker's research shows that writing about emotion improves mood and immunity (1997). • Somatic approaches (Peter Levine – Somatic Experiencing) and body awareness: The "fist in the chest" cannot be solved with logic; it loosens only with the body. A long exhale, feeling your feet on the ground, consciously relaxing the muscles — by stimulating the vagus nerve, this draws the system into "safe" mode. • Self-compassion (Kristin Neff) and Compassion Focused Therapy (Paul Gilbert): Being able, today, as an adult, to say "good luck" to that fourteen-year-old child, to lay that hand on his shoulder. Saying to yourself what no one said. • Identity work within Erikson's framework: Erik Erikson showed that identity is not found but built — piece by piece, by trying, by saying "this is me / this is not me." The stubbornness here — "I will be myself" — is not a problem, it is raw material; it just needs to find a direction. Tuncay's bridge Years later, when I read Porges and van der Kolk, I understood why those eight weeks simply would not pass — I hadn't lost my mind, my nervous system had just been writhing from a hunger for reward. That was also when I figured out why I felt so close to machines: they didn't betray me, because they weren't capricious. This wasn't an escape, it was an attunement; it was my autistic brain finding a language that felt safe to it. And today I can finally smile at the AuDHD brain's story of "failing attendance and winning the exam" — because it was not a broken student, but a right mind forced into the wrong mold. The hardest thing was making peace with the Radiology I had refused: for a long time I kept replaying that scene with different endings, asking "what if I had done it?" Then I understood that the child, with the only information he had, had made the most logical decision to protect himself. I stopped being angry at him. I began to understand him. That was exactly the bridge: taking science and laying it over that fourteen-year-old child like a blanket — saying, "you were not wrong; no one ever gave you the right environment." Sources: S. Porges, The Polyvagal Theory (2011); B. van der Kolk, The Body Keeps the Score (2014); J. Bowlby & M. Ainsworth, attachment theory; J. Webb, Running on Empty (2012); R. A. Barkley, ADHD and executive functions; S. Baron-Cohen, empathizing–systemizing theory; R. Schwartz, Internal Family Systems; J. Pennebaker, expressive writing research; E. Erikson, theory of identity development; K. Neff, Self-Compassion. Where I Stand Today I truly lived out the sentence "I will be myself, but I don't know who I am" all the way to the end — it just took twenty years. The child who understood machines later became a project manager, then an Artificial Intelligence specialist and Data Scientist. The talent was there from the start; it just waited a long time for an environment that didn't tell it "just do your job." What I have overcome is this: I no longer take refuge in work to escape feeling; working has become not an escape but a choice. That mouse-ridden basement, and that "no one saw" night, I now remember not with shame but with respect — that child endured. And what I carry with me, and have held on to, is this: stubbornness. "Even if I'm lying on the ground, I get back up" did not remain a child's saying. It became my operating system. "What does not kill me makes me stronger." — Friedrich Nietzsche I am not the end, I am the way to it. A step each day, until my last breath.

== ection0020 [] class="body0" xmlns:epub="http://www.idpf.org/2007/ops"> Chapter 6 – First Love, Marriage and Guilt Love was always a big thing for me. Not something ordinary or everyday, not a "comes and goes" sort of thing — but big, heavy, almost sacred. Because as a child I received so little of it that, when it came, I had no idea how to behave. If you set a plate in front of a child who has gone hungry for years, that child won't just eat from it; he'll guard it, hide it, hunch over it so no one can take it away. I was like that too. When love came, I would cling to it with all my strength — not so much to love, as to keep from losing it. There was a constant voice inside me: "Hold on tight. If you let go, it leaves. Like everything else, it leaves." Inner voice: Back then I didn't know that loving out of the fear of losing something isn't really loving at all. It's clinging. Like a drowning person grabbing a life buoy. And no life buoy can carry a whole person's weight forever. The First Bond I was eighteen. My first serious bond. I got engaged. I remember that moment — or rather, I remember the warmth it left inside me. For the first time, someone had said "yes" to me. To me alone. For someone who had spent his whole life sitting in the back rows, always found a little too much, always a little wrong, always a little "hard to understand," that "yes" was no simple word. It was a door. I felt something somewhere in my chest loosen; as though I could finally exhale a breath I'd been holding in for years. My shoulders dropped. My jaw unclenched. For the first time, it felt as though I had a place in the world. Inner voice: "At last I'm not alone. There is someone. Someone sees me. Me — that me inside, the one no one ever saw — she sees it." But before long I learned: I had been betrayed. Even writing that sentence, nearly twenty years later, makes the same spot in my chest ache. The outside world hadn't changed at all that day. The sun had still risen, the streets were still crowded, the buses still passed by. Nothing, absolutely nothing, betrayed from the outside what was happening within me. A person's world collapsing while the world couldn't care less — that is the strange loneliness. A heavy, cold stone settled right in the middle of my chest. Not an imaginary stone; a real weight, one that took up space and wouldn't let me breathe. Swallowing became difficult. A knot in my throat, an emptiness in my stomach, a strange trembling in my hands. At night I would lie in bed staring at the ceiling, and the hours would pass. In the dark you couldn't even see the ceiling; still, I would stare — because the moment I closed my eyes, the same film would start over from the beginning. My heart truly hurt. People say "heartache," and most think it's a metaphor. It isn't. It's a real, physical pain. Behind the breastbone, a concrete pain that tightens, that closes in, one you could point to with a finger. My body was shouting at me a truth my mind couldn't yet accept: You've been left again. Inner voice: "Wasn't I enough? Wasn't I good enough? Why didn't she choose me? What was lacking in me — what was too much?" And the most poisonous, the most insidious was this question, the one that always returned to the same place: "Or maybe the problem is… me? Is it always me?" That night, for the first time, I clearly realized: this pain was familiar. It wasn't new. It only had a new face. In truth I had formed this sentence — the "so there must be something wrong with me" sentence — much earlier, when I was much smaller. The betrayal had only confirmed it one more time. ★ The trauma of abandonment razes a person's self-worth to the ground. Inner voice: What if that "yes" had been real? What if someone, once, just once, had stayed all the way to the end? Maybe then I would never have formed this sentence. Maybe I would never have written this book. Strange — my deepest wounds turned out to be the places where I learned the most. But that night I couldn't have known this. That night I was just eighteen years old, a child staring at the ceiling, carrying a stone in his chest. Marriage I married at nineteen. Why? I asked myself this question for years, and each time a different answer came up. But the most honest answer was this: because I wanted to leave. Because I didn't want to sin. Because I wanted to "do the right thing." Inner voice: "This must be the right way. If I marry, everything will fall into place. I'll have a home, I'll have order, this feeling of being chased inside me will end. Marriage will make me safe." How young I was. How… hopeful. Inside me a child was preparing to play the heavy role the world had cast for him, the "proper man" — but there had been no rehearsal, the lines were borrowed from others, and the stage was far too big. I didn't marry out of maturity. I married out of two needs to escape at once: away from home and away from guilt. For me, marriage was not a meeting but an exit . Not a door, but that green light under the emergency-exit sign. Inner voice: Looking back now, I want to laugh — but it's a bitter laugh. Marrying in order to leave home… It's like running from a fire and shutting yourself in another room. You close the door behind you, you say "I'm saved," and then you realize: the smoke came in with you. Because it wasn't the house that was burning. It was you. A marriage built as an emergency exit is rarely a good idea. One always understands this afterward — long after passing through that door. I approached marriage as if it were a solution, but marriage isn't a solution; it's a mirror. Whatever you carry into it, it magnifies. And I carried into that home an unresolved guilt, an unfilled emptiness, an unnamed loneliness. And that home — the home to which I was faithful, which I never once betrayed — reflected all of it back into my face, growing a little larger each day. Closeness That Felt Like Danger Closeness always came to me in two languages. One language was warm, inviting, full of promise; it said, "come, it will be good here." The other was sharp, cautionary, harsh; it said, "step back, this place is dangerous." And the worst part — the two spoke at the same time. When someone drew near me, a door would open inside, light would come in, I would warm up; and at that very moment, somewhere else, deep down, an alarm would sound. This alarm was loud, it came from within, it had no off switch: Stop. Stop. You've come too close. Closeness was not a safe word in the dictionary of my childhood. The people I loved were also the people who could hurt me most — I had learned this not in words but with my body, at a very early age. Touch, too, was no light thing in my body. For others, when a hand rests on a shoulder, perhaps it's only a hand; for me it was a question mark. When a hand touched my shoulder, sometimes I would fill with love, sometimes with defense — and I couldn't know in advance which would come. My body decided before my mind: it would pull back, tense up, brace itself. My back would stiffen, my shoulder would rise, my breath would stop for an instant. I bore no ill will toward anyone. The person across from me had, most of the time, only wanted to show their love. But my body took every possibility seriously. Because my body had never once been joking. My body still remembered the things my mind wanted to believe it had long forgotten. Inner voice: Two voices were always fighting inside me. One would say: "Come closer, life is here. Don't be afraid. This time it's different." The other would answer at once: "If you come closer, you'll get hurt. Like always. Have you forgotten?" And I would stay in the middle — one foot inside, one foot ready to flee. And each time I would notice the same thing: loneliness was familiar, closeness uncertain. A person more often prefers the familiar pain to the unknown healing. Because familiar pain is, at least, a pain whose outcome you know. ★ Loneliness was familiar, closeness uncertain — and fear, more often than not, chooses the known evil over the unknown good. Sexuality and Guilt My first sexual experience gave rise to deep feelings of guilt. That moment, which for someone else might have been a discovery, a coming together, even a joy, set up a tribunal in me. Because what I had learned was this: "Not before marriage." "It's shameful." "It's a sin." These sentences hadn't been said to me just once; over years, through veiled glances, through timid silences, through all the capillaries of a devout upbringing, they had worked their way into me. At the same time I was curious. My body wanted — because the body is designed to want. My mind said "wrong." There was a war inside me, and both sides were me. Inner voice: "Is this wrong? Or is it normal? Is everyone like this, or am I the only one who's broken? Why am I fighting myself so much? Why does a punishment come right after a pleasure — who set down this punishment, and when, and how did I get bound to a contract I never signed?" Even masturbation was something I hid as a child, something I was ashamed of. At night, in my room, silently, in the dark. And right after — almost at the very same moment — the guilt would come. As if there were an eye in the ceiling, as if someone were watching me, as if even in my most private moment I was not alone but being judged. That invisible gaze didn't let go of me for years. I had mistranslated my own body, the way one mistranslates a sentence: Desire = danger. Pleasure = sin. Closeness = loss. I didn't write this equation; I was taught it, and I was not old enough to object. Yet the body was never the enemy. It was only speaking a language I had been forbidden to hear — and I had learned to treat my own mother tongue as a crime. Inner voice: What if they had told me, "the body is beautiful, desire is human, pleasure is not a sin"? What if that invisible eye had never existed at all? Maybe then getting into bed would have been a relief, not a tribunal. But my own parents had only taught me what they themselves had been taught — they too were a link in a chain. I no longer blame anyone. I only see the chain. And seeing is the first step to breaking it. Years later, I had a wife. I was faithful — I never cheated, not even once. This wasn't a matter of pride for me but a matter of identity; betrayal was the name of my deepest wound, and I had forbidden myself to open that wound in anyone. But between us there was an emotional indifference. In the same house, the same bed, at the same table — and still, miles of distance between us. A great emptiness was growing inside me. Sometimes it felt as though someone were punching me right in the middle of my stomach; that real, physical sense of emptiness, a hunger that resembled hunger but couldn't be filled by food. Inner voice: "Am I the one who's wrong? Why isn't it enough? Others are happy with this much, so why can't I be? Why do I always want more — something deeper, more real, more naked?" And, as always, I would look for the way out in the same place: in myself, as the guilty one. "I want too much." "I'm the one who's wrong." "I'm insatiable." "I don't deserve to be loved." I blamed myself so much that blame turned into a habit, almost a comfort. Blaming myself gave me, at least, the illusion that the situation was under my control: if I was the problem, then I was also the solution. But I wasn't the problem. The problem was the sentence I had never been taught: To need is not a deficiency. Surrender, Tantra and the Wrong Target I didn't merely love. I surrendered myself. There are mountains of difference between these two, but learning that took years of my life. Watching the other person, reading the smallest shadow on their face, making them happy felt good to me — or rather, it felt as though I were filling my own emptiness with their happiness. Their smile was my oxygen. If they were in good spirits, it meant I was "good enough." Inner voice: "Just looking at them is enough. As long as they're happy. What I want doesn't matter — I don't even fully know what I want anyway. I want their happiness. Yes. That's enough. That must be it." But the truth was this, and it's hard to admit: I was living the other person, not myself. I had a life, but I wasn't in it; I was the spectator, the servant, the décor of someone else's life. At some point a doubt fell into me — sharp, unsettling, unrelenting: "Could it be that I want too much sex? Could the problem be in my desire?" This question wouldn't leave me in peace. I searched for an answer. And I searched in my own particular way — the way few others would, by opening files, researching, traveling to three different countries. After my second divorce I took Tantra trainings in three different countries. Love, bond, energy, touch — I went to learn what these were not from books but from the body. And I learned something surprising, one of the great turning points of my life: it was never about sex at all. Inner voice: "Is it really this simple? For years I judged myself as 'insatiable,' 'wanting too much,' 'wrong' — and the thing I wanted wasn't even sex? I only wanted to be seen and to become whole. Not with another body inside a body — but with another soul beside a soul." I wanted to unite. To truly merge — body and soul at once, the two never separating. I didn't want short things: a one-night consolation, a momentary proof. I wanted depth. I wanted continuity. Not half, but whole. I wanted to be able to exist with a person without losing myself. But this was the mistake I made for years, and Tantra showed it to me with a bitter clarity: ★ I was trying to live the right things with the wrong people. With the right thirst, but always running to dry wells. Lovebombing and a Theory of Narcissism Later I learned a word: lovebombing. The moment you name it, what you've been living turns from a riddle into a pattern — and seeing the pattern is the beginning of getting out of it. First comes an intense love; so intense that everything you've hungered for your whole life pours over you at once, like a flood. Attention, admiration, "you're special," "I've always been looking for you." An addictive beginning. Then — suddenly, for no reason, without explanation — a withdrawal. Coldness. Distance. And you, having once tasted that flood, what do you do? You give even more. You try even harder. You make yourself even smaller for them to come back. Inner voice: "Maybe they'll come back… Maybe this time if I give enough, they'll stay. Maybe the fault is mine, maybe if I fix it… One more chance. One more chance." And that "one more chance" sentence devoured years of my life. Inner voice: Now I understand that the urge to "give even more" was no coincidence. It was a reflex learned in childhood: "If love is withdrawing, then I wasn't good enough — I must try harder." Lovebombing touched precisely this old wound, with a surgeon's precision. As if there were a lock inside me and they knew the key. Most of the time they weren't even conscious of it — they too were speaking the language of their own wounds. And here, after years of observation, something I came to call my own theory formed inside me. Not an expert's laboratory theory — a theory a life tested in its own flesh: some narcissists are not born without empathy. The empathy either never developed, or it was once there but closed off by a trauma — just as a hand pulls back when it touches fire, that person too, somewhere, having once been burned too badly, locked the capacity to feel from the inside. ★ My personal interpretation: in my own relationships, trauma, neurodivergence, and protective defenses sometimes formed patterns that could look narcissistic or emotionally closed. This is not a diagnosis of neurodivergent people or anyone else; it is simply how I make sense of my own experience. Inner voice: In saying this I'm not letting them off the hook — what they put me through was real, the pain was real. But understanding is a different thing from forgiving. I wanted to understand, because understanding lifted me out of victimhood. The question "why did they do this to me?" diminishes a person; the question "what door inside them closed?" sets a person back on the path of seeing again. And seeing is the only way not to fall into the trap again. The outcome was the same every time, repeating like a refrain: I always gave. I opened my palms, opened my insides, opened my door. But in return I received almost nothing. And the more I gave, the emptier I grew, and the emptier I grew, the more I gave — because I thought I could fill the emptiness by giving. Yet I was carrying water to a leaking bucket. Inner voice: "Do I not deserve to be loved? Or is there no such thing as love at all — is everyone really just trading something, and I was the last to notice? Or… or is the problem that I always knock on the wrong door? The right door exists, but because I'm always so hungry, I run to the first one that opens." Therapist's Note – Trauma Bonding, Sexual Guilt and Dependent Love What is happening here: In this chapter three materials combine to form a stubborn pattern that works almost like a law of nature. One: early emotional neglect — a childhood in which love came scarcely and unpredictably. Two: a religious-restrictive sexual upbringing that ties pleasure to sin and desire to guilt. Three: the intense impulsivity, hunger for depth and "all or nothing" perception of AuDHD. Out of these three the brain produces an unquestionable equation: Love = Pain + Guilt. And here's the insidious part: lovebombing cycles don't break this pattern, on the contrary, they reinforce it; because familiar pain feels "safe" to the nervous system. The system confuses familiarity with peace — what we take for calm is, most of the time, merely the familiar . Where it comes from — body and evolution The answer to the question "why do we attach so strongly to familiar pain?" lies not in logic but in the depths of the nervous system. John Bowlby and Mary Ainsworth's attachment theory (Bowlby, Attachment , 1969; Ainsworth's "Strange Situation" experiments, 1978) shows that for an infant, staying close to the caregiver is a matter of life and death; that is why the infant attaches to the caregiver even if that caregiver is inconsistent or hurtful — because not attaching is, in evolutionary terms, death. A child who grows up in an environment where love comes with pain cannot separate the two; as an adult, he recognizes the "warmth + danger" mixture as "home." This is called insecure/anxious attachment, and it explains exactly why lovebombing is so effective: that cycle replays, beat for beat, the "come close–go away" rhythm of childhood. The tensing of the body in closeness, the rising of the shoulder, the freezing at a touch — these are not weakness of character but what Stephen Porges, in his Polyvagal Theory , calls "neuroception" (Porges, The Polyvagal Theory , 2011): the body, before we are even aware of it, scans every contact for "is this safe, or dangerous?" In a nervous system that has lived through trauma, this scanning is hypersensitive; even a loving hand can trigger the alarm. In Bessel van der Kolk's famous phrase, "the body keeps the score" ( The Body Keeps the Score , 2014) — the body still remembers and guards what the mind believes it has long forgiven. Sexual guilt, too, has an address. The shame that kicks in at the moment of pleasure fires the brain's threat and punishment system (the amygdala and related circuits); when pleasure and punishment signals arrive at once, the brain pairs desire with danger. This is a merciless form of classical conditioning — like Pavlov's bell, except this time the bell is your own body. Add to that the dopamine system of AuDHD: the search for depth and intensity is not "insatiability" but the reward system seeking a stronger stimulus; what you call "I want too much" is, most of the time, not a flaw but a neurological signature. If left unaddressed — possible bad outcomes If this pattern is not recognized and worked on, it repeats itself for a lifetime: always the same type of wrong partner choice (because healthy love feels "boring/foreign," while chaotic love feels "passionate/familiar"). A complete loss of self in relationships — where you end and the other begins becomes blurred. Relationship addiction (codependency). Recurring, chronic depressive episodes. Burnout in the perpetual "giver" role, and a silent anger building up beneath that burnout. A person becomes a master at filling other people's emptiness, a novice at feeling his own — and the day he is left alone with his own emptiness, he won't know whom he'll find inside. The question "do I deserve love?" turns into an open wound that won't close, that festers; every new "wrong person" can press a finger into it. Paths of healing — several doors There is no single prescription; but there are several doors that complement one another: • Naming the needs (linguistic clarity): The first liberation is linguistic. "Sex is not the same thing as emotional merging." Separating the two needs and calling them by name maps the chaos within. What has a name becomes manageable. • Internal Family Systems (IFS – Richard Schwartz): Your "inner voices" — the one that wants to come close, the one that wants to pull back, the one that blames, the one that protects — are not enemies; each is a "part" that once protected you. Rather than banishing the part that says "give more," talking with it: "Why are you trying to protect me?" • Somatic Experiencing (Peter Levine) and body-based work: Trauma bonds and the touch alarm cannot be solved with logic; they can only be retrained with the body. The slow re-teaching of safe contact to the nervous system, in small doses. • EMDR and trauma-focused therapies: Tracing ingrained beliefs like "no one chooses me / I don't deserve it" back to the moments they were first wounded, and reprocessing them. • Compassion Focused Therapy (Paul Gilbert) and self-compassion (Kristin Neff): "I'm not wrong, I'm just wounded." Learning to answer your inner critic the way you'd speak to a friend. • The practice of setting boundaries: For someone who has learned to dissolve himself, saying "no" is a training program in itself. Every small "no" is a signature of writing yourself back onto the list. • Expressive writing (James Pennebaker): Pouring guilt and shame onto paper instead of turning them over in your head; research shows this improves mood and even immunity. This book, too, is in a way the fruit of that therapy. The shared aim of all these doors is the same: not to love less — but at last to add yourself to the list of the ones you love. Tuncay's bridge I lived this pattern for years without knowing its name — then, the more I read, the more trainings I attended, I said, "so this thing has a name," and each name let me breathe a little more. When I understood Bowlby, I grasped why I always attached most strongly to the people who hurt me most; this wasn't stupidity, it was the survival strategy of an eighteen-month-old infant still running in my adult self. When I learned the word lovebombing, a pattern in my life suddenly lit up — and I have to be honest: learning that word gave me faster relief than years of therapy had, because now I was dealing not with a riddle but with a mechanism. The Tantra trainings I took in three countries showed me something I could never have learned from books: what I was searching for wasn't sex but union — and once I knew this difference, I acquitted myself of that "insatiability" verdict I'd condemned myself with for years. As for sexual guilt — I couldn't solve it with logic; that guilt didn't sit in the mind but in the body, and I could only soothe it with the body, slowly, patiently. What I did was always the same: take the science and spread it over my own life like a map. That map turned the sentence "I'm insatiable, I'm wrong, I'm broken" into the sentence "I am an explainable system, and systems can relearn." Sources: J. Bowlby, Attachment (1969); M. Ainsworth, Patterns of Attachment (1978); S. Porges, The Polyvagal Theory (2011); B. van der Kolk, The Body Keeps the Score (2014); R. Schwartz, Internal Family Systems; P. Levine, Waking the Tiger; P. Gilbert, Compassion Focused Therapy; K. Neff, Self-Compassion; J. Pennebaker, expressive-writing research; trauma bonding literature. Where I Stand Today This is the part of my life I've worked on the longest — and let me say it honestly: it hasn't fully closed, but it has been understood. I now recognize lovebombing not ten years later but as it's happening; when that flood pours over me, a small voice inside says, "slow down — we've seen this before." When familiar pain feels "like home," I know it's lying; that house burned down, I got out, I don't have to go back there. I didn't only learn the sentence "sex ≠ union" — I lived it, in three countries, in my own body. What I've overcome is this: I no longer put everything I have, myself included, on the table to prove I deserve love. What I'm still working on: being able to stay there when closeness is good — instead of pulling back because the good thing feels unfamiliar. This is the hardest lesson of my life; because they programmed me not to stay in goodness but to flee from harm. But I'm on the way. And this time, most importantly: I'm taking myself along with me. There's one more name on the list now — mine. "Your task is not to seek for love, but merely to seek and find all the barriers within yourself that you have built against it." — Rumi I will never be complete, only a little more myself each day — to the end.

== ection0024 [] class="body0" xmlns:epub="http://www.idpf.org/2007/ops"> Therapist's Note – Hyper-Attachment and Separation Trauma What is happening here: Here, something beautiful and something fragile are happening at the same time. The beautiful part: a man who has never experienced safe closeness is living it for the first time with his children. Touch without alarm. Unconditional love. That state described on the night walks — "I relaxed along with them" — is, neurobiologically, a real thing: a parent and a baby in calm contact regulate one another; this is called co-regulation . And the dangerous part is right here too: precisely because his own childhood was so empty, the bond formed with his children grows excessively strong. A projective repair is at work: "By giving them what was missing for me, I am healing my own wound." The deep empathy and sensory bonding capacity of AuDHD intensify this even further. The attachment grows very strong — and this makes the separation far more devastating. There is also a very healthy, conscious effort here to break the cycle: "I will not do to them what was done to me." Recognizing one's own neurodifference in one's children, and framing it not as a flaw but as a gift, is precisely the right move to stop the transmission of intergenerational trauma. Where it comes from — body and evolution What happens in the body when you take a baby into your arms is a program millions of years old. According to John Bowlby's Attachment Theory , the infant's clinging to its caregiver and the caregiver's bonding to the infant is a biological system that ensures the survival of the species (Bowlby, Attachment , 1969); the ancestor who did not keep a defenseless young one close could not carry on their line. Mary Ainsworth's work showed that this bond takes secure, anxious, or avoidant forms (Ainsworth, 1978). A child's first word being "Daddy" and reaching into the arms with surrender is a classic picture of secure attachment — and for a parent who often lived through their own insecure childhood, this is, in the fullest sense of the word, restorative. That "my hands stopped trembling" is no coincidence: calm, loving contact releases oxytocin ; this is the hormone of bonding and of calming. At the same time, in Stephen Porges's Polyvagal Theory , the "vagal brake" engages — the body's shift from threat mode into "safe social bonding" mode (Porges, 2011). As Bessel van der Kolk says, the body keeps the score; but the body keeps the score of healing contact too ( The Body Keeps the Score , 2014). The sentence "for the first time, closeness was safe" is the account of a nervous system being retrained. The capacity to recognize oneself in one's child has to do with mirror neurons and high empathy: the brain echoes another's emotion within; this intense echo is often stronger in neurodivergent individuals. And, after the separation, smelling their pillows and sinking into rumination for hours is the grieving state of the brain's default mode network . If left unaddressed — possible bad outcomes If the bond becomes the sole source of a person's self-worth, the algorithm "Love = Completeness" is born. If separation shatters that algorithm, a deep loss of identity follows: severe depressive episodes, constant self-blame ("I couldn't be a good enough father"), withdrawal from life, extreme anxiety in new relationships or, conversely, complete avoidance of closeness. To lose the child then becomes not just the loss of the child, but the loss of oneself. Unprocessed grief of separation can also settle into the body — sleep disturbances, chronic tension — and most insidiously of all, with the decision "I will never love this much again," it can shut a person down emotionally; and this, in turn, produces within oneself a likeness of the very emptiness one was trying to repair. Paths of healing — several doors The key sentence: Hold the bond, but do not grip it tight. Love does not vanish with distance. There are a few doors that work: • Self-compassion (Kristin Neff) and Compassion Focused Therapy (Paul Gilbert): answering the voice that says "I couldn't be good enough" as you would speak to a friend: "You did what you could; what was missing was not love." • Internal Family Systems (IFS – Richard Schwartz): recognizing and soothing, separately, that fearful part asking "Will they hate me?" and the protective part saying "I will break the cycle"; both are different voices of the same love. • Somatic approaches (Peter Levine – Somatic Experiencing): releasing the part of separation grief that accumulates in the body — the knot in the throat, the tearing in the chest — not with logic but with slow breath and bodily awareness (a long exhale stimulates the vagus). • Expressive writing for grief (James Pennebaker): putting the nights of farewell, the longing, into writing; this book itself is a product of that writing. • Giving the bond a new form: a message, a photo, a heartfelt "I love you." The bond shifting from "possession" to "guidance" — the practice of Kahlil Gibran's words, "they belong not to you." The old sentence "If I lose them I am finished" slowly turns into "I love them and this love is still within me." • Maskless parenting: a child's nervous system internalizes not acted-out peace, but real peace. That is why the healthiest healing is the parent's own genuine healing. Tuncay's bridge I learned the names of these concepts only later — while living them, I only felt them. On the night walks, when I noticed that as I soothed my child I too was being soothed, I didn't know it had a name; later I read of something called "co-regulation" and said, "so that's what it was." That my hands stopped trembling in the delivery room had seemed like a miracle to me — then I learned of oxytocin and the vagal brake, the miracle turned into science, but it lost nothing of its beauty. The sentence "the one thing you cannot give your child is the thing you do not have within yourself" — I learned it not from books, but from that night of handing them over, from that question at the threshold, "Daddy, are you leaving?" And let me be honest: I cannot say I have fully overcome the wound of separation. Every night of handing them back still gently reopens that first night. But there is one thing I overcame — I learned not to turn that wound into self-hatred. I took the science and spread it over my own life like a map; that map turned the sentence "I was a bad father" into the sentence "I was a wounded man and I did the best I could." And this note in this book, too, is an attempt to hold that map out to you. Sources: J. Bowlby, Attachment (1969); M. Ainsworth, Patterns of Attachment (1978); S. Porges, The Polyvagal Theory (2011); B. van der Kolk, The Body Keeps the Score (2014); R. Schwartz, Internal Family Systems; P. Levine, Waking the Tiger; K. Neff, Self-Compassion; P. Gilbert, Compassion Focused Therapy; J. Pennebaker, expressive writing research. Where I Stand Today Becoming a father became both my wound and my healing. I kept the healing: I now know I am able to give safe closeness — the promise in Chapter 4 came true, not "click" but "Daddy." The wound of separation I still carry. But I learned not to turn it into self-hatred. The love I feel for my daughters is not a possession that can be lost. No matter how great the distance grows, it is a piece that remains within me. And most important of all: I am determined to show them my real face, to genuinely heal rather than act out happiness. Because the only way they don't learn the mask is for me to truly be myself. Breaking the cycle is not a sentence — it is a decision I make anew every single day. "Your children are not your children. They come through you but not from you, and though they are with you, yet they belong not to you." — Kahlil Gibran, "The Prophet" I live in becoming, not in being finished. And that becoming stays with me to my last breath.

Years later I understood that I had eaten not from hunger but from fear. My body had never learned to say "enough," because to say "enough" was dangerous. I had no idea where to find a feeling called fullness. After the surgery, for the first time I was able to leave the plate half-finished. I had changed — but not really. The façade had changed, the load-bearing columns were the same. From the outside: strong, fit, disciplined, "a man who's pulled himself together." From the inside: emptiness, searching, lack, and still that dripping sound from the basement. Inner voice: "There's more. This isn't all of it. The outer shell changed but the child inside still stands in the same place. And one day I'll have no choice but to sit down beside that child. Therapist's Note – Outer Transformation, Inner Void What is happening here: This is one of the most honest lessons in the book — and really, a lesson that concerns an entire culture. What is being lived here has a clinical name: self-worth tied to external sources (contingent self-worth). A missing inner worth is being papered over with outer change. The body transforms radically, but the mind goes on carrying its old traumas just as they were: that fear at the table, the shame of "finish your plate" carved into the body, and the belief that "I am not enough." The body sheds 55 kilos; that belief doesn't shed a single gram. Because that belief won't step on the scale. In AuDHD, a neurobiological accelerant is added to this: every external success — one kilo less, one muscle more, a compliment, a smiling glance — releases a small hit of dopamine. It feels wonderful for a short while, then drops; the person becomes dependent on the next bit of approval. Meanwhile the hole stays exactly where it was. Researchers call this the "hedonic treadmill": you run and run, but you're always in the same place. Where it comes from — body and evolution First, the trace that childhood table left in the body. The "if you don't finish your plate…" threat pairs a small child's most vital need — being fed — with a danger. In Bessel van der Kolk's famous phrase, "the body keeps the score" ( The Body Keeps the Score , 2014): years may pass, but the stomach, the shoulders, the breath remember that fear; the tensing of your shoulder as you raise the fork to your mouth isn't "weakness of character," it's an old alarm written into the body. Stephen Porges's Polyvagal Theory explains this: the nervous system, without our awareness, scans the surroundings at every moment for "am I safe?" — this is called neuroception (Porges, 2011). If the table wasn't a safe place in childhood, the body will go on perceiving the table as a battlefield in adulthood too. So why is the illusion of "I became worthy once I lost the weight" so powerful? Because for tens of thousands of years, the human being survived in the eyes of the group . For our ancestors, being cast out of the tribe meant death; that's why our brain processes the question "how are others looking at me?" as if it were a matter of survival. When we receive social acceptance, dopamine rises in the brain's reward system — this is an ancient wiring that kept our ancestors close to the tribe. In an AuDHD dopamine system this swing is sharper: it spikes high in the moment of a compliment, then drops fast, and the need for a new compliment arises. So the feeling of "a woman smiled, which means I have worth" is not a flaw of character, but the voice of a very old biology. And then there are mirror neurons and "seeing yourself through the other's eyes." A human being largely constructs the self through the eyes of others; the child "reads" itself in the caregiver's face (Winnicott's "mirroring function"). If you saw constant criticism, fear, or disappointment in that face, the mirror that settles inside you is cracked: whatever the outside says, you always see yourself as "not enough." Even at 82 kilos, that cracked mirror shows the old child. If left unaddressed — possible bad outcomes If the belief "Worth = Appearance" is left unworked, a chronic dissatisfaction settles in despite every visible success. The likely course: compulsive exercise edging into addiction, a never-ending spiral of cosmetic interventions ("if I fix just one more thing it'll be complete"), distortions of body image and the re-disordering of eating patterns, a relational ground built anxiously on "am I attractive enough?" Approval dependency: a person enters an endless chase after a feeling that, in principle, cannot be bought from outside — the feeling of "I have worth." In its heaviest form, in the midst of all the external successes, a deep meaninglessness sets in, a collapse into "I put in all this effort, and still I'm empty." Because no matter how hard the wrong door is forced, it won't open. Paths of healing — several doors The direction must turn 180 degrees: from outside to inside. There isn't a single way to do this, there are several doors: • Self-compassion (Kristin Neff): The question, "how would I speak to a good friend today?" Looking at the man in the mirror as you would look at a friend, not an enemy. Neff's research shows that self-compassion is different from, and sturdier than, self-esteem: self-esteem depends on success, while self-compassion stays with you in failure too. • Compassion Focused Therapy (CFT – Paul Gilbert): Building a "compassionate voice" inside you to counter that harsh, critical voice ("you did this to yourself"). This isn't about fighting, it's about shifting the balance. • Internal Family Systems (IFS – Richard Schwartz): That "fat, shy, lonely child" is not a source of shame but a part to be protected. IFS teaches you to sit down beside that child, to ask him what he needs. You built up a body; now it's time to hold that child. • Schema therapy / CBT: Making deep beliefs — schemas — such as "Worth = Weight" and "Food = Danger" visible on paper and rewriting them. A belief can't change until it becomes visible. • Somatic approaches (Peter Levine – Somatic Experiencing): If that old alarm from the table lives in the body, the solution is in the body too: slow breathing, exercises for noticing fullness, learning where the feeling of "enough" settles in the body. A long exhale stimulates the vagus nerve and pulls the system into "safe" mode (Polyvagal Theory). • Expressive writing (Pennebaker): Writing a letter to that child. Pennebaker's research shows that putting emotion into writing heals both mood and body (Pennebaker, 1997). One sentence runs beneath them all: "My worth is independent of my appearance." Once inner worth begins to grow, the outer chase slows of its own accord. The sentence "Either her… or the children" echoed in my head without pause — not like an ultimatum but as though it were a law of nature, inevitable, beyond debate. Inner voice: There were two voices in my head, and both were shouting at the top of their lungs. One said: "You cannot be alone. Once again everyone has left, only this one remains. If you lose this too, what will be left in your hands?" The other, quieter but the truer one, whispered: "They are your daughters. They are not a bargaining chip. A father cannot even turn such a sentence over in his mind." But a weary man does not know which voice to choose. Exhaustion makes the wrong voice sound right. Fear presents the worst decision as the most reasonable one. The Greatest Mistake of My Life I made the greatest mistake of my life. I gave my daughters to their own mother. The day I packed their suitcases, my hands were shaking. The metallic sound of the zipper made a strange echo in the room — with every pull, something inside me seemed to close a little more. I was folding their little T-shirts; the fabric still carried their scent, that indescribable child smell — milk, soap, sweat, and a little sun too. Once again I picked up one of their little teddy bears. It still had their scent. I paused for a moment, brought it to my nose, and my tears dripped onto the fabric. Warm drops, on the cold toy. Something was growing in my throat; I could not swallow. The eldest stood in the doorway. The light came from behind her, her face was half in shadow. In a trembling voice she asked: "Dad, are we leaving now?" I could not answer. I opened my mouth, but there was no word inside — only a pressure, a void, a humming. I only held her tight. One last time I pulled her warm body close to me; I felt her small, fast heartbeat against my chest, I drew the scent of her hair into me, I tried to seal all of this into my memory — because a part of me already knew this was an "ending." When they got into the car, the little one waved at me through the window with her tiny hand. The glass was covered with fingerprints. I waved too, but my knees gave way. As the car turned the corner, I held on to the wall just to stay standing. Inner voice: "It's not too late yet. Run, stop the car, take it all back, say now the sentence you couldn't say at the table." This sentence flashed in my head like an alarm. But my body had turned to stone. Sometimes the loudest thought lives in the most motionless body. Inside, I was running. Outside, I was a statue. That night the house was utterly empty. The beds they slept in were still warm. I went into the room, sat on the floor, picked up a little shoe and smelled it. The silence of the house had turned into a humming — that silence ringing in my ears, reminding me of what had truly happened. The clock was ticking, the refrigerator was humming, a car was passing outside; but the house felt dead. And then, quietly, for a long, long time, I wept, sobbing. My body was shaking, my breath was catching, and in that moment — let me confess it — a part of me wanted that shaking never to stop, because when it stopped, only that void would remain. For a whole year I was in the deepest depression. That year was not a year in which the days were separate from one another; it was as though it were a single, long, gray day. Searching each morning for a reason to get up; getting up anyway when I found none; and once up, forgetting why. Food was tasteless, music came from far away, the man in the mirror was familiar yet a stranger. My body was heavy — as though lead had been poured into my bones. To this day I still feel bad about this. It is no longer a wound now, but a scar. ★ I traded them for a new beginning. The reason for this was the lack of love in my childhood — that lack that made me dependent on love. I did not love myself, I did not respect myself — and that is exactly why I allowed this. Let us not fool ourselves: I am the guilty one. I did this. We had promised each other: "We will not become strangers to each other." But we became strangers. Another man had already entered my ex-wife's life. The children began to call him "Dad." When I heard that sentence, I gripped the steering wheel in the car so hard that my knuckles turned white. The leather cover of the wheel creaked in my palm. The windows had fogged up; outside blurred, inside narrow. My tears were running down my face but I did not make a single sound. Only a scream rose inside me — one that could not get out, that struck the cage of my chest and turned back. The word "Dad" had once been my name. Now it had become someone else's name. The owner of a word had changed, and this was the quietest theft in the world. The Film I Played a Thousand Times And then my mind kicked in — that mind that never stops, that recomposes everything. That night, those suitcases, that question "Dad, are we leaving?" — I played them out a thousand other ways. Inner voice: "What if I had said 'no'? What if I had stood up at that table and said, 'My children are not a bargaining chip'? What if I had never let that woman into my life, what if I had stood up to her family's looks even once? What if I had reopened those suitcases that night? Would we be in the same house right now, would that laughter still be heard at the breakfast table? While they called someone else 'Dad,' where was I?" This film always freezes at the same spot, always magnifies the same frame. And I, inside that life that never happened, suffer as though I had truly lived it. For a neurodivergent person this is the cruelest gift: to be able to feel what never happened as vividly as what did. My brain stages the scene so well that I hear that sizzle in the kitchen again, I feel the light of that morning that was never lived on my face, I can weigh on my tongue the heaviness of that sentence I never said. It is so real that on some mornings, when I open my eyes, for a moment I think I am in that house; then the ceiling, the real ceiling, comes crashing down on me. The vision is so clear that when I wake I grieve twice: once for what was, and once for what was not. Imagination, which was a blessing, turns into an executioner. I regret it. To this day I still regret it. But the one thing I have learned at the end of that film I played a thousand times is this: had I not made that decision, this road would never have begun. Perhaps I would have become another Tuncay — but not the Tuncay who writes these lines, who turns this pain into something. Therapist's Note – The Traumatic Decision and the Loss of the Father Identity What is happening here: This is the hardest point in the book, and it deserves honesty more than consolation. A decision is made under maximum pressure — family pressure from outside, fear of loneliness from within, manipulation (lovebombing) on top of that, and an ultimatum. In such moments AuDHD intensifies both rumination (obsessive thinking) and emotional overload — until the system overflows and a decision is made "just so it ends." The loss of the father role is one of the deepest wounds a person can suffer. An important distinction must also be established right from the start: guilt and responsibility are not the same thing. Taking responsibility: "I made this decision." Drowning in endless guilt: "Because I made this decision, I am worthless as a human being." The first heals. The second destroys. Where it comes from — body and evolution Beneath this decision, it was not logic that was working, but a far older circuit. Constant high pressure, conflict, and an ultimatum like "either her, or the children" push the nervous system into chronic threat mode. In Bessel van der Kolk's words, when the brain's alarm bell, the amygdala — "the brain's smoke detector" ( The Body Keeps the Score , 2014) — rings for a long time, the thinking brain (the prefrontal cortex) weakens; a person no longer makes the "good" decision, only the one that "ends the threat." Stephen Porges's Polyvagal Theory sheds light on this: when threat becomes unbearable, the nervous system shifts from the fight-or-flight response to a collapse/freeze (dorsal vagal) response (Porges, 2011) — your inability to answer in the doorway, your body turning to stone, is precisely this freeze state, not a "lack of character." What you call "dependence on love" also has an address. The attachment theory of John Bowlby and Mary Ainsworth shows that a child who does not see secure love early in life experiences, in adulthood, the fear of being alone almost like an existential danger (Bowlby, 1969). Jonice Webb names this silent wound emotional neglect ( Running on Empty , 2012): the failure to see a child's inner world while on the surface "everything is fine." A person who does not value themselves enough to feel whole on their own seeks that missing value outside, in a relationship; and in order not to lose it, they may risk even their most precious thing. On top of that, the "what if it had been like this" film you played a thousand times is, neurologically, an overactivity of the default mode network , that is, rumination (Nolen-Hoeksema, 1991) — the same scene coming round and round again. If left unaddressed — possible bad outcomes The brain hardens the sentence "I failed as a father, I was not enough" into a chronic cycle of guilt and identity crisis. Possible course: self-hatred, living constantly in the past, excessive anxiety in new relationships and either clinging or fleeing, deep and recurring depressive episodes, disruption of sleep and appetite. A person judges themselves for a decision made under conditions they would never allow today — forgetting that the person of that day did not have today's knowledge. Unaddressed grief turns, over time, into "frozen grief": a pain that can neither be cried out nor let go of, petrifying inside. And unresolved guilt quietly slides into the sentence "I do not deserve happiness" — which is one of the most common ways a person sabotages their own future. Paths of healing — several doors There is no single prescription for this wound; there are several doors, and all of them are built on "carrying it without denying the truth and without executing yourself": • Compassion Focused Therapy (CFT – Paul Gilbert) and self-compassion (Kristin Neff): Answering that judging voice inside you as if you were speaking to your dearest friend, not a convict. "In that moment, with that knowledge, that person did the best they could." • Internal Family Systems (IFS – Richard Schwartz): Seeing the "part" that made that decision not as a murderer, but as a frightened and weary protector trying to shield you from loneliness; and returning to that wounded part who froze and remained standing there with the question "Dad, are we leaving?", to keep it company. • EMDR and Somatic Experiencing (Peter Levine): Processing, at the bodily level, the scenes nailed into the body — that doorway, that tiny waving hand — and releasing the frozen energy. • Grief work and expressive writing (James Pennebaker): Putting unfinished grief into words — it has been shown that putting emotion into writing improves mood and even immunity (Pennebaker, 1997). This very book enters through that door. • Post-traumatic growth (Tedeschi & Calhoun): Binding the pain to a meaning, not by saying "it was good," but by asking "where did this take me?" Meaning does not erase pain; it makes it bearable (this is also the essence of Frankl's logotherapy). Tuncay's bridge For years I played this scene in my head under the heading "I am a monster." Then I read Porges, and for the first time I could see my inability to answer in the doorway, my body turning to stone, as a "freeze response" — my shame did not lessen by a single gram, but the meaning changed: in that moment I had no will, because my nervous system had already shut down. When I read Bowlby and Jonice Webb, the answer to the question "why did I give away my most precious thing so as not to lose a love?" became painfully clear: because inside me I had raised a child who valued himself so little he was not worth losing. I did not erase the guilt — I still carry the responsibility, I carry it knowingly. But when I learned IFS, I stopped cornering and beating the part that made that decision; I could sit across from it and say, "I know, you were so afraid, you were trying not to leave me alone." And when I learned that the "what if it had been like this" film was actually rumination, a habit of my brain rather than an illness of it, even though I could not stop the film, I could at least say, "this is a film, not reality." Have I overcome it? Entirely, no. But I no longer sit alone in that scene, in the guilty chair — there is someone beside me who understands, and that someone is me. Some decisions heal not into smooth skin but into a scar — and we have the right to show the scars. What I have overcome: self-hatred. Today I can hold the two together — "I am responsible" and "I was a wounded person, dependent on love because I saw no love as a child." Both are true. I have never changed the love I feel for my daughters, only the physical closeness was lost. And the pain, no matter how great, did something: it forced me to ask why I loved myself so little that I would give away my most precious thing. That question started everything that came after. Therapist's Note – Loss, Silent Grief, and Unspoken Guilt What is happening here: Not every grief looks like grief. There's the loud, weeping kind — and there's the silent kind, the one that shows itself through silence: the thinning smile, the shortening sentences, the shoulders sinking inward. In people who took on the role of "the one who copes" early in life, it is almost always the silent kind at work, because collapsing was never a permitted option. On top of this comes a particularly stubborn form: an "ambiguous" grief for children — they are alive, they are reachable, and yet they are absent. There is no farewell, no ritual, no clear point of ending. That is why this grief does not end on its own. In psychology this is also called a quiet, unrecognized loss : there is no funeral, no grave, no socially recognized loss; so those around you look on and ask "what is there to be sad about?" and the grieving person is forced to hide their pain. This is called disenfranchised grief (Kenneth Doka, 1989), and it is one of the loneliest kinds of grief there is. Where it comes from — body and evolution There is a very old answer to why grief settles in the body, in the chest, in the stomach. The human being is a creature whose species' survival depends on forming bonds. John Bowlby's attachment theory (Attachment, 1969) describes exactly this: the infant cries, searches, and clings in order to stay physically close to its caregiver — because in our ancestors' world, closeness meant survival, and separation meant the risk of death. That is why, when a bond breaks, the brain reads it not as simple sadness but as danger . In Mary Ainsworth's "strange situation" experiments, the panic response in the body of an infant separated from its mother is the raw form of our adult grief. It's no coincidence that you feel "as if cast out of your own home" — for your brain, breaking from a bond truly is being torn from your homeland. The bodily weight of grief is real too. As Bessel van der Kolk put it, "the body keeps the score" (The Body Keeps the Score, 2014): unprocessed loss accumulates in the shoulders, the jaw, the chest; because the brain remembers threat not with logic but with the body. According to Stephen Porges's polyvagal theory , when we break from someone we love, our nervous system can drop out of "safe bond" mode and into a kind of freeze/withdrawal mode (Porges, 2011) — what you call "dropping by one degree," "a loss of warmth," is the very withdrawal of the vagus nerve. And that scene replaying over and over at night: the overactivity of the brain's default mode network , that is, rumination (Nolen-Hoeksema, 1991) — the mind, trying to solve a loss it cannot solve, looping the same tape endlessly. The whisper of guilt has a root too. The voice saying "you could have done more" is, more often than not, an inner-voice pattern learned in childhood. In people who experienced emotional neglect (Jonice Webb, Running on Empty , 2012), the finger automatically turns inward at the moment of loss — because the message "you're always the one responsible" was internalized early in life. If left unaddressed — possible bad outcomes Stones stacked one atop another without ever touching: when losses are merely "handled" instead of grieved, at some point they turn into a baseline weight that surfaces as burnout, loss of meaning, or depression. What grief researchers call complicated (prolonged) grief can develop: months chase years but the pain doesn't soften, it freezes. The whispering guilt ("you could have done more") becomes a constant background hum, gnawing at self-worth from the inside. The sentence "maybe I don't belong anywhere" can harden into an isolating way of moving through life — social withdrawal, the expectation in relationships that "I'll be abandoned anyway," disrupted sleep and appetite. In the most severe course, unresolved grief can lead all the way to a sense of meaninglessness and the dimming of the will to live. That is why finding a witness to your grief is not a luxury but a need. Paths of healing — not a single road, but several doors There is no single right way to grieve, but there are several doors that work: • Expressive writing (James Pennebaker): Grief needs a witness — and that witness can even be a journal. Pennebaker's research shows that putting a loss into writing for 15–20 minutes a day improves mood and even immunity (1997). The whispering guilt must be said aloud or written on paper, because once spoken it loses its power. • Internal Family Systems (IFS – Richard Schwartz): Seeing that voice asking "was I lacking, was I too much?" not as an enemy but as a wounded "part" trying to protect you. Instead of getting angry at that part, sitting beside it and asking "what are you afraid of?" • Somatic Experiencing (Peter Levine) and body work: That stone sitting in the chest is dissolved not by logic but only by the body. Planting your feet on the ground, long exhalations (which stimulate the vagus nerve and draw the system into "safe" mode), allowing bodily releases like trembling or rocking. • EMDR and narrative therapy: Reprocessing that scene that plays over and over and turning the story of the loss from a "I was inadequate" narrative into a "I did what I could under hard conditions" narrative. • Compassion-Focused Therapy (Paul Gilbert) and self-compassion (Kristin Neff): Answering the judging voice inside you with compassion, as if speaking to your own child. • The ambiguous loss framework (Pauline Boss): Boss's most powerful suggestion is this — stop seeking a clear closure, learn to live with the reality of "both present and absent." Keeping the bond open despite the distance is more healing than forcing a closure. Tuncay's bridge I didn't get over this loss like a head cold — I learned to live with it. When I read Pauline Boss's phrase "ambiguous loss," something in me eased in a strange way: so this nameless weight had a name after all, so I wasn't going mad after all, so the feeling that "no one died but something inside me did" was the description of something real. When I read Bowlby, I understood that my sense of being "cast out of my own home" was not a flaw of character but the human species' tens-of-thousands-of-years-old attachment software — and I got less angry at myself. Van der Kolk's "the body keeps the score" explained why that stone was there in my chest; instead of brooding over it, I learned to breathe out. It helped most of all in the part about my daughters: when I understood that I could be a father by giving reality rather than by paying off guilt, that whispering voice of guilt agreed, for the first time, to fall a little quiet. What I did was take the science and spread it like a map over my own loss — and that map turned the sentence "I'm the worthless man who was abandoned" into the sentence "I'm a father who keeps loving, imperfect but real." Where I Stand Today I didn't "get over" this loss like a head cold — I learned to live with it, which is a different and more honest thing. What truly changed: silence is no longer my enemy. It used to be proof of my worthlessness, today it's a space where I can breathe. I no longer merely believe the sentence "some things do not choose you, this is not your worthlessness" — I feel it. And I keep my bond with my daughters open without clutching it tight. I stopped waiting for a closed door to open again, and started truly living where I actually stand. The stone in my chest is still there — I won't lie. But it's no longer on top of me, it's beside me. I'm not carrying it; I'm walking with it. And the difference between the two is a whole life. "Let everything happen to you: beauty and terror. Just keep going. No feeling is final." — Rainer Maria Rilke I am becoming, so I am still on the way. Day by day, until my last breath.

== ection0032 [] class="body0" xmlns:epub="http://www.idpf.org/2007/ops"> Chapter 11 – Rock Bottom and the Diagnosis I often set off at three in the morning. “I’ll leave at three,” I’d say, and I would; because standing still in my head at night was harder than being on the road. Movement did me good. If the phone lost signal for a moment, I’d say “I’m going to lose it.” A small thing, I know. But with me small things turn into big waves; when a connection breaks, it’s as if the world breaks. For years I couldn’t explain this to anyone. Turns out it had a name. The night I hit rock bottom, I was in Istanbul. In my father’s caravan, in Üsküdar, at night, alone. It was getting on toward three o’clock. Outside, a single street lamp was burning; an orange, flickering light seeped through the fogged glass and made a pale stain on the ceiling. I stared at that stain. The cold was merciless, it seemed to come from within, cutting from skin to bone. The metal walls of the caravan trembled softly in the wind, without pause; as if someone were running their fingertips over the metal and saying, “You’re here, you’re still here.” And right at that moment, I laughed at myself. “The man’s hit rock bottom and he’s still writing a poem about the light stain on the ceiling.” That very laugh saved me. And one day, after so much searching, after so many question marks, I found a room inside me that had a name. The diagnosis. AuDHD. Both autism and ADHD, together, two worlds constantly at war inside me. One part of me wanted order, routine, silence; the other wanted excitement, novelty, movement. Two drives fought each other, and I tore apart in the middle. “Are you scattered or obsessive, which is it?” they’d say. Both. When I first heard that word, I cried — not from sadness, but from the relief of “so I’m not lazy.” That feeling I’d carried my whole life, “something’s wrong with me,” turned all at once into an explanation. It wasn’t me who was wrong; it was a world that didn’t understand me. My inner war was, in truth, a search for balance. I’m not between two worlds — I’m the bridge between two worlds. And the view from this bridge is beautiful. There came a moment when everything fell silent. Not outside. Within me. It was a strange kind of silence. The world's sounds were all in their place — the hum of the refrigerator, the tires of a passing car hissing over the asphalt outside, a muffled laugh leaking through from the neighbor's television — but inside, right in the middle of my head, that engine which had not gone quiet for years had suddenly stopped. Not the thinking engine; that was still running. What had stopped was hope. That thin, stubborn murmur of "maybe tomorrow will be a little better." Once it was cut off, all that remained was a ticking. There was no place left for me to escape to. Not a relationship, not work, not the body — nothing filled the emptiness anymore. There had always been a door before: a new love, a new project, a new city, a new decision. I would fill the emptiness inside me by running toward one of those doors. But that night I realized I had tried every door, one by one, and behind each of them was the same room. The same emptiness. The same me. At night I would sit alone at home. Most of the time I wouldn't turn on the light; the dark was less tiring than too much brightness. The walls would close in on me — really, like a physical sensation, I would feel the room shrinking centimeter by centimeter. The clock would tick, but time seemed to have stopped. Tick. Tick. Tick. Each tick fell into the same emptiness, arriving nowhere. I was breathing, but I was not living. My chest rose and fell; a reflex. My body was still programmed to survive, while the me inside had long since let go. Inner voice: "What now? How much longer will it go on like this? Is it me who's wrong, or is the system wrong?" I withdrew from people. Family: very far away. Friends: almost none. I didn't want to talk to anyone. A name would flash on my phone screen, my finger would hover over it, then pull back. Talking took energy; all I had left was just enough to stay silent. But here was the bitter part: I wasn't talking to the outside, and inside I never fell silent. There was a relentless crowd inside my head — one accusing, one defending, one wanting to get up and leave, one saying "hold on a little longer." At night I would stare at the ceiling in bed. Hours would pass, sometimes until morning. My eyes open, my body still, my mind at full throttle. This is the cruelest kind of exhaustion: your body is spent, but your brain won't shut down. Inner voice: "Why am I like this? Why is life so hard? How do other people live through all of this so easily?" Inner voice: "Or maybe they don't live it easily, and I just can't see their mask? No… that can't be. They can laugh. They can get up in the morning and go somewhere. They have something I don't. Or I have some flaw they don't." One day everything stopped. Truly stopped. My second divorce was predictable — even being able to say that was a kind of pain, because I was like someone who watches the oncoming train and still cannot pull himself off the tracks. I saw it, I knew it, but I couldn't move. I can't do it. I can't get up. I had sat on the edge of the bed, and standing up felt like the most impossible movement in the world. My body was made of lead. I'm breathing, but somewhere inside me something has died. I can describe it: in the middle of the chest, just above the heart, where there should be warmth, a cold stone. Inner voice: "Should I keep going? Is this enough already? Or should I give up?" In that moment I was at absolute rock bottom. In the darkest place of the darkness. No light was visible, no exit. And the most frightening thing about the bottom wasn't the pain — I had grown used to the pain. The most frightening thing was the indifference. I had even stopped suffering. To want something to end, you have to care about it; and I was no longer in any state to care about anything. That numbness is the quietest danger of all. The Doctor Then I went to a doctor. I don't fully remember how I got there — I must have dragged myself, because in those days every step was a decision and every decision was an exhaustion. I remember sitting in the waiting room: the sound of the clock on the wall, others' whispers, an antiseptic smell, the rustle of a paper tissue. Inside, I expected nothing. "He'll give a prescription, it'll pass," I told myself. "Or he won't, doesn't matter." For the first time, someone truly listened to me. Even writing this, my throat tightens, because how many times in my life had I been listened to? Being listened to is one thing — being heard is another. People often heard me, waited for me to finish speaking, then gave their own answer. This doctor did not do that. He looked long into my eyes — he didn't look away, didn't glance at his phone, didn't abandon me by bowing his head to take notes. He just looked. And under that gaze, something I had carried for years grew lighter. He said: "Tuncay… get away from all this. You need love. You need care. Leave everything behind. But don't do anything to yourself. You are very intelligent. You are aware of everything. This may be hard." Silence. In that moment I froze. The words echoed in my head, but one of them rose above the others, like a gong: ★ You are very intelligent. I couldn't digest that sentence. My brain tried to reject it — like a body rejecting the wrong blood type. Inner voice: "Me, intelligent? Me? All my life I thought myself stupid… inadequate… wrong… This man must be mistaken. He doesn't know me. If he saw me for two hours, he'd understand." Inner voice: "But… wait. He said, 'You are aware of everything, this may be hard.' He spoke of the awareness as a difficulty. As if he knew that seeing too much has a price. No one had ever said it that way before." Why the sentence "don't do anything to yourself" was spoken on that exact day, in that exact order, I understood only later. That man knew who he was looking at. He had gently taken the hand of someone at the edge of a cliff — without shouting, without creating panic, just by saying "I'm here, don't jump." Sometimes a single sentence does the work of an entire therapy. Then tests were done. An intelligence test. While answering the questions, I was mocking myself inwardly — "let's see how you embarrass yourself now," I said, because my expectation was always the worst; expecting the worst was an old armor I had developed against disappointment. The result: high intelligence. Inner voice: "How can that be? For years I thought myself stupid. At school, at work, in relationships — I always thought 'I'm the one lacking.' All those 'you can't do it' looks, those 'why can't you be like everyone else' questions… were they all wrong? Or is the paper wrong?" There was a strange feeling inside me: neither joy nor pride. More like a confusion. As if for years they had called me by the wrong name, and I had taken that wrong name to be my own; now someone had come and spoken my true name, and I had become a stranger to my own name. The Diagnosis Then it came: ADHD. And then: autism spectrum, high-functioning. That is, AuDHD. Silence. That silence again — but this time there was a hum inside it, the sound of something falling into place. In that moment all the pieces of my life came together. As if someone had poured a puzzle onto the table and all the pieces suddenly fit. All those pieces that had lain scattered on the table for years, none of which I thought matched any other — why I remembered people's faces only hours later, why I instantly sensed the smallest change in a setting, why I would get stuck on a subject and forget the world, why I was drained in crowds, why I reacted to injustice far more fiercely than others — they all locked together. The picture appeared. And the picture was me. Inner voice: "This is why I think this way. This is why I feel this way. This is why I live this way. So the problem wasn't me — my system was just different. For thirty-some years I had been trying to run a correct machine with the wrong user's manual." At first I didn't believe it. Of course I didn't — believing was always the hardest thing for me. I researched. I read day and night, articles, criteria, other people's accounts. As I saw myself in every line, a strange thing happened: I felt both relieved and afraid. I had the tests repeated. A third time. Three times the same diagnosis. I was so meticulous about not trusting myself that I had to test even my own explanation three times. Inner voice: "Look again. Maybe I answered wrong the first time. Maybe I wasn't feeling well that day, and the result was inflated. Maybe this time it won't come out the same." Looking back, this very behavior of testing three times was a very autistic behavior — the search for certainty, the intolerance of uncertainty, the inability to accept a pattern without confirming it. In other words, even the way I rejected the diagnosis proved the diagnosis. When I realized this, I laughed inwardly; it was one of the rare moments in those days when I could laugh. Inner voice: "So it's real. I'm not wrong. I'm different." But right beside the relief, that familiar mind kicked in again — it began to replay all the scenes of the past through this new knowledge. Like a film projectionist, I took the reels of my life down from the shelf and ran them through the projector again with a new lens. Inner voice: "What if I had known this at fourteen? What if, in that rat-infested basement room, someone had told me, 'You're not broken, your brain just works differently'? What if my teachers, my father, the kids in that classroom had known? Would I have turned down radiology then? Those two marriages, those betrayals, that night I gave up my daughters — which of them would have ended differently?" I reconstructed an entire life through new glasses. Every scene hurt, because in every scene I saw that mapless child — angry at himself, apologizing to everyone, not knowing why he didn't fit. It hurt. But this time, at the end of the reconstruction, there was not sorrow but tenderness: I couldn't be angry at that child. No one had given him the map. To blame him for going the wrong way was like scolding someone who'd never been handed a compass with "why are you lost?" Inner voice: "What if it had been like this?" I kept asking that for years. Then I learned: that question is a trap. You can't change the past; you can only exhaust your present self with it. Instead of "what if I had known?" I tried saying "I know now, what will I do?" For the first time, the question pushed me forward instead of spinning me in circles. Until that moment I had thought "I am wrong." From that moment on, I knew "I am different." And this changed everything — not overnight, but from the foundation. I no longer blamed myself. I no longer tried to explain myself to everyone. How tiring that effort of explaining had been, I understood only once I stopped. I had only been trying to reach the right place. Inner voice: "This is who I am. And this is enough." Therapist's Note – Existential Collapse and Late Diagnosis What is happening here: The collapse here is not an end but, paradoxically, a turning point. A decades-long definition of "I am wrong," layered over a never-recognized AuDHD, creates a brain stuck in the "Why me?" loop. The autistic side endlessly scans for threat (sounds, faces, the smallest change in the environment, the "what ifs"), the ADHD side endlessly seeks stimulation and reward — and when enough unprocessed trauma accumulates, the system overheats and crashes. What Tuncay describes is clinically familiar: immobility, a body like lead, the stopping of time, the "cold stone" in the middle of the chest, and the most dangerous of all, that numbness — pain being replaced by indifference. This is not simple sadness; it is the nervous system shifting into "shutdown" after prolonged overload. Where it comes from — body and evolution That numbness at rock bottom is not random; it is very ancient survival software. Stephen Porges's Polyvagal Theory describes how, when a threat becomes unbearable, the nervous system goes beyond even "fight or flight" and drops into the most primitive brake of all, dorsal vagal shutdown (freezing, collapse, numbing) (Porges, 2011). Just as an animal in nature "plays dead" in a predator's jaws, when the body calculates that it can no longer fight, it shuts the system down. Tuncay's state of "I can't get up, I'm breathing but not living" is precisely the human counterpart of this primitive brake. This is why Bessel van der Kolk says that trauma is a record first of the body , not the mind — "the body keeps the score" ( The Body Keeps the Score , 2014); that cold stone in his chest is the score the body keeps. The question "How does everyone else live so easily?" is also not an innocent comparison; it is the product of an overactive default mode network . This network kicks in when we are not occupied with the outside world, replaying and reconstructing the past and the future over and over; when it overworks, this is called rumination (Nolen-Hoeksema, 1991). Tuncay replaying his life with "what if I had known at fourteen?" is this network's classic seizure after a late diagnosis — the brain reopens all the old files with the new information. In late-diagnosed adults, this phase of "rewriting the past" is almost the rule, and it contains both grief and healing. Jonice Webb's concept of Childhood Emotional Neglect also comes into play here: because no one gave that child the map, the child wrote the deficiency into his own character — reading "there was no map" as "I am broken." If left unaddressed — possible bad outcomes If it is left without explanation and without help, the existential collapse worsens: an entrenched belief that "there is something fundamentally wrong with me," recurring and deepening depressive episodes, total social withdrawal, the collapse of sleep and eating rhythms, and — it must be said plainly — suicidal thoughts. The most insidious form of the danger is the very numbness Tuncay noticed: a person is most fragile not when they stop suffering, but when they stop caring. This is exactly why the doctor's sentence — "don't do anything to yourself" — is vital. The most dangerous belief at this point is not "Life is hard," but "There is something fundamentally wrong with me"; because the first is aimed at the world, the second directly at the person's right to exist. Paths of healing — several doors At this threshold there are a few doors that work, and all of them are built on "turning judgment into explanation": • Safety first, insight second: A system that has gone into shutdown wants safety before it wants persuasion by logic. Somatic Experiencing (Peter Levine) and body-based approaches aim to slowly thaw the frozen energy; planting the feet on the ground, a long exhale (which stimulates the vagus nerve and pulls the system into "safe" mode — Polyvagal Theory), simple rhythmic movement. The mind only listens after the body has relaxed. • Reframing the diagnosis (CBT / schema therapy): Turning the question "What's wrong with me?" into "How does my system work?" — what Tuncay did spontaneously is, in fact, the very heart of cognitive reframing. • Internal Family Systems (IFS – Richard Schwartz): That crowd in the head (the accusing, defending, fleeing, "hold on a little longer" voices) are not enemies to be suppressed; they are all protective "parts." Tuncay's "inner voice" dialogue is exactly IFS's field of work. • Compassion Focused Therapy (Paul Gilbert) and self-compassion (Kristin Neff): Feeling compassion for that mapless child instead of anger — Tuncay's shift from sorrow to tenderness while replaying the past is, clinically, a sign of healing. • Narrative therapy and expressive writing (Pennebaker): Rewriting life from the story of "I am broken" to the story of "I am an explainable system." This very book passes through this door (Pennebaker, 1997). • Connection: Reading the accounts of other neurodivergent people. There is no medicine that dissolves the belief "there is something fundamentally wrong with me" as quickly as the sentence "I have the very same thing" — this is why Tuncay seeing himself in those lines as he read was healing. Tuncay's bridge I could have taken this diagnosis like a brand and gone home — many people do exactly that, put the paper in a drawer, and carry on with their lives under the same accusation. Without realizing it, I did something else: I read the paper like a user's manual. When I read Porges, I understood that my "can't get up" state was not laziness or weakness, but the most primitive brake my system pressed to protect me; for the first time I could remember that day without being angry at myself. Van der Kolk's sentence, "the body keeps the score," told me that the cold stone in my chest was not imaginary but recorded. And the moment I realized that taking the test three times was "an autistic behavior," that even my rejection confirmed the diagnosis made me laugh — and that laugh was the first tiny door out of the bottom. Today I can honestly say: I crossed this threshold. I do not belittle rock bottom; looking back, I still shudder; but the "I am wrong" operating system no longer runs in me. Over it has been written, "I am different, and different is an explainable system." Every therapist's note in this book is an attempt to hand you that paper not as a brand, but as a map. Sources: S. Porges, The Polyvagal Theory (2011); B. van der Kolk, The Body Keeps the Score (2014); R. Schwartz, Internal Family Systems; P. Gilbert, Compassion Focused Therapy; K. Neff, Self-Compassion; J. Webb, Running on Empty (emotional neglect); J. Pennebaker, expressive writing research; S. Nolen-Hoeksema, rumination research; I. D. Yalom, existential psychology; T. Grandin (autism and difference). Where I Stand Today This is the moment when I can most clearly say "yes, I made it through." Not because what followed was easy, but because the fundamental equation changed. "I am wrong" had been my operating system for over thirty years; I woke up every morning with that sentence and closed every night with it. The diagnosis overwrote it with "I am different — and different is an explainable system, not an illness." Looking back today, I see that rock bottom was not a punishment but a threshold: because everything fell silent, I could hear my own voice for the first time. I lived through rock bottom, and this is not something to be taken lightly, I know that. If these lines are reaching you and you are sitting in the dark right now: please, don't do anything to yourself. Talk — to a doctor, to someone close to you, to a helpline. The darkest place of the night is most often just before the moment when the pieces are about to come together. "I am different, not less." — Temple Grandin “Finished” is for things, not for people. I am becoming — to the end.

== ection0034 [] class="body0" xmlns:epub="http://www.idpf.org/2007/ops"> Chapter 12 – Awakening and Surrender The diagnosis was there. The pieces had fallen into place. Two words — ADHD, autism — had explained, in a single sentence, the life I had carried for years like a riddle. But something was still missing. Knowing wasn't enough. I was looking into myself the way you look at an X-ray: bones in place, the fracture here, the crack there. I saw all of it. Seeing did not heal. Understanding does not warm a person in the middle of the pain. A diagnosis is like a map pressed into your hand. Every road is marked on it, every mountain named. But a map doesn't walk you anywhere. A map won't keep you from freezing. You fold the map, slip it into your pocket, and then realize you are still standing there, in the cold, alone. That is exactly how I was standing. A perfect map in my hand, and beneath my feet a terrain I had never crossed. I didn't only want to understand. I wanted to live. That chasm between knowing and being — I felt it with my whole body. Inner voice: "All right, I get it. So this is how I am. But why? Why all this pain? Why this loneliness? There must be a reason. Or must there not? Maybe even looking for a reason is a kind of illness. But still I ask — is there something deeper, or am I searching for a door that doesn't exist inside my head?" I looked for an answer. I looked in books — scribbling notes in the margins, underlining some lines three times over. I looked in people — gazing into their faces, asking without ever asking, "Do you know? Have you found something I don't have?" I looked in myself — in the dead of night, listening to the sound of my own breath. And at the bottom of every search, there was a feeling that never fell silent, always coming from the same place: "There is more. There is something beyond the words. You are trying to catch it with words, but it sits in a room where those words don't live." Spirituality First I tried the doors one by one. I took Tantra trainings in three different countries and a shamanic training as well. For hours we worked with the body; I learned breathing techniques, touch, energy work. The rooms were always alike: the coolness of the wooden floor, the heavy, sweetish smoke of burning incense, a drum or a bell sounding softly, someone saying in gentle English, "now follow your breath." At first my body relaxed in those rooms. My shoulders, the ones that had been climbing toward my ears for years, came down a little. My stomach loosened a little. I traveled a great deal to learn about neurodivergence and spiritual cultures, about the place of neurodivergent people in these traditions. I grew more and more "spiritual" — I learned the words, I learned the postures, I learned to close my eyes at the right moment. But every time, the instant I stepped out the door into the noise of the street, the same emptiness quietly came back. As if there were a room inside me that no incense could fill; its door locked, its key lost. Inner voice: "Not this either. Look, not again. I'm learning techniques — good techniques, techniques that work — but I don't want techniques. I'm searching for truth. The difference is this: a technique says 'do this,' truth says 'you are this.' I'm still doing. I haven't yet become." Inner voice: "Or… maybe there is no such thing as truth and I'm just tired? No. No, this emptiness has a shape. Even an empty room has a shape. I'm searching for that shape." Preparation Then I encountered a deep experience of consciousness — in a legal and supervised setting, under expert guidance, a work that went beyond the limits I knew. I deliberately do not name the substance in this book; because my aim is to share an experience, not to steer anyone toward anything. I did not do it for fun. It was not an escape. It was a real, naked, frightening search. I prepared for months. I became vegan. I gave up smoking and alcohol completely. In that period I didn't kill even a fly — I remember taking one that buzzed at the windowsill into my palm and setting it free outside; that tiny, panic-filled tremor in my palm, and a strange tenderness passing through me. In the last days I fasted. My body grew light; when I woke in the mornings, my steps felt lighter, as though some ballast had been cast out of me. My mind cleared; thoughts no longer piled one on top of another as before, but came one by one, slowly. But alongside the lightness there was a shadow that never left it: fear. A stone in the center of my chest, lodged there even when I swallowed. At night, before sleep, I would probe that stone the way you probe a tooth with your tongue. Inner voice: "Am I ready? Am I truly ready, or am I lulling the fear to sleep by saying I'm ready? What if I can't come back? What if I return from where I go not as Tuncay, but as something else? What if I never return?" Inner voice: "But there's also this: what if I never go? What if I wait at this door until the end of my life, on the threshold, saying 'maybe someday'? There are two fears before me. I have to step into one. And I think what I fear more is dying without ever taking a step." The Experience I entered the room. A dim light — tinged with orange, easy on the eye, as intimate as a candle. The scent of incense hung in the air; heavy, sweet, a little earthy. A soft cushion had been laid on the floor. I lay on my back. I looked at the ceiling. My heart was like a drum in my chest — not fast, but deep, every beat echoing in my ribs. I counted my breaths. One. Two. Three. Then I took it. In the first minutes nothing happened. The ceiling was the ceiling, I was myself, my heart was still beating. A small, mocking voice rose inside me. Inner voice: "There. I knew it. All this preparation, all this fear — and nothing. Maybe this doesn't work on me either. Maybe I'm so 'different' that even the universe skips my turn." Then it began. First it came from my body. From my fingertips, from the soles of my feet, a loosening spread inward — but it was more than loosening. My body dissolved. Bones, muscles, skin — all of them, in turn, slowly, dissolved like a candle melting in warm water. I did not know where I ended and where the room began. There was no boundary left. I fell into a boundless ocean. But it was not water. If it had been water I would have drowned; this carried me. It was pure energy. There was no time — neither past nor future, neither "how much longer" nor "once upon a time." There was no space. And strangest of all: there was no longer an "I." That "I" that never fell silent, that stared at the ceiling at three in the morning asking questions — it was gone. Only a feeling remained. Intense, wordless, boundless. Until that moment I had not known a feeling could be so vast, so full. From within — though there was no longer any place called "within" — I asked the one question I had carried for years: "Am I right? What is wrong with me?" A thirty-six-year-old question. The question I asked in the cold of the caravan, asked at the back of the classroom, asked while looking into my daughters' faces. No voice came. No word came. But a feeling came — and that feeling spoke more clearly than millions of words. As if the whole universe paused for a moment, turned to me, and smiled. It embraced me; not the way a mother embraces her child, but the way existence embraces a part of itself. And within that embrace, beneath the words, three things were true at once: "Everything is right." "You are worthy." "Nothing is wrong." I did not understand it with my head. Every cell in me, each one separately, understood it in its own language. Inner voice: "So everything is right. So I am not wrong. All these years… all this 'why am I like this'… It turns out none of it was a defect. My path was simply different. Different doesn't mean wrong. I knew this, actually — I had read it, written it — but for the first time it's true not because I know it, but because I feel it." In that moment I understood everything. But I cannot tell it. Even now, writing these lines, the words slip out from between my hands; just like trying to hold water in your palm. Some things are lived, not spoken — the moment they are spoken, they shrink. It was like dying, but a death without fear. Pure. Clear. The ego dissolved entirely; that armor that had "protected" me my whole life, that had in truth confined me, melted for an instant. "I am Tuncay" was gone. The name was gone, the past was gone, the wound was gone. Only "being" remained. Infinite, peaceful, flawless. And then I slowly came back. First the weight came — my body was a body again, a load pulled toward the ground. Then the breath; I felt the rise and fall of my chest again, as if noticing it for the first time in my life. I moved my fingers, they were there. When I opened my eyes the room was the same: the same dim light, the same incense, the same cushion. Nothing had changed. But I had changed. The room was the same; the eye looking at it was another. Inner voice: "I was born again. And this time, as I was being born, no one greeted me as 'wrong.'" I did this two more times, each time at a different depth, passing through a different corridor. But the message was always the same, always a single syllable, always clear: "Go." Not a question, not a command — a direction. That old compass inside me was pointing, for the first time, to a place without wavering. Surrender and the Journey From that moment on I surrendered myself. My whole life I had tried to control — everything, everyone, the next step, calculating every possible catastrophe in advance. For an AuDHD brain, control is not a habit but a survival strategy. And for the first time I let that strategy go. No plan. No control. Only flow. Inner voice: "I have a task. How I know this, I cannot explain. But I know it — I know it in my bones. And the strange thing is this: knowing it does not frighten me, it rests me. As if I had worked the wrong job all my life, and someone came and said 'this is actually your work.'" I went to Bali. The shamans performed rituals; they cleansed me, they looked at my energy — closing their eyes for a moment, then looking at one another. And they said: "You are one of us. But you are different." Inner voice: "What do you mean? 'One of us' but 'different' — isn't this that sentence again? The sentence I've heard all my life: 'He's like us, but not quite.' But this time they're not saying it as something bad." Inner voice: — "You were a shaman from the very beginning. An old soul…" — "An old soul? Or just a young man worn out far too early? Maybe they're the same thing." In Japan I meditated in mystic temples. The silence of the stone gardens, the creak of the wood, the cold tatami under bare feet, the deep vibration of a bell from afar. There I learned something, and what I learned shifted a door inside me from its frame: some Buddhists, through years of deep meditation, have reached states of consciousness similar to what I lived in that room, without needing any substance, entirely by natural means. Only with breath. Only with patience. Only by looking inward. Inner voice: "So it was always inside me. That ocean, that embracing universe, that feeling of 'everything is right' — it didn't come from outside. A substance didn't put it into me. It only opened the door for an instant. But the door was my door. I searched outside, in distant countries, in those rooms, in books — when they had put the key in my pocket the day I was born." This was not a rote sentence of comfort. It landed in my body like a release. Because if the door is inside, then to reach it I would never again need that threshold, that fear, that risk. The source of what I sought was me. Once I understood this, everything changed — and nothing changed. I was still Tuncay. But no longer an incomplete Tuncay. Happy and the End of Following Here I must pause for a moment and speak of Happy. That Labrador puppy was beside me throughout this whole journey. He did a dog's purest, most unquestioning work: he followed me. Wherever I went he came after me, matching his step to mine, never taking his eyes off me. If I got up to go to the kitchen, he got up; if I headed for the door, his tail quickened; if I sat, he curled up at my feet. His whole world was me. And I — for years, without ever noticing — was doing exactly the same thing. A shaman, a teacher, a book, a country… I was always following someone's path. "Maybe he knows. Maybe I'll find it there. Maybe what that man says is the key I've failed to find my whole life." So I went, in the footsteps of others. In Bali after a sage, in Japan after the silence of a temple, much younger after a Goku, after a cartoon hero. There was always someone "walking ahead"; I was always behind, matching my step to his. Inner voice: "Wait a second. I'm watching Happy — how he follows me, how he never takes his eyes off me — and I feel something strange. As if I'm looking in a mirror. He follows me; and I follow someone. We're both walking after someone. So who is at the very front? Is there someone walking at the front who follows no one? Or have we all latched onto someone else's tail?" When I lost Happy — here, on these lands, when a car carried him off too — a sentence opened inside me. Pain is sometimes like a door; it opens at the moment you least want it to, in the harshest way. What I understood while watching him became a truth once he was gone: even a dog, by following the one it loves, arrives somewhere — but that place is never its own path. It is always a borrowed path. The path of love, but not your own. And for years that is exactly what I had done. Going after someone, tracing their trail, imitating them, saying "I'll do it the way he did it"… None of this was a "path." This was, at best, walking on someone else's path. Inner voice: "The point was never to go after someone. It was never to follow. It wasn't admiration, it wasn't imitation. The point is to build your own path, to walk your own path. Happy followed me; and I followed others for years. But a path becomes yours only when you walk it yourself. A human life isn't to be spent on a dog's loyalty. That loyalty is beautiful — but my loyalty must now be to my own step." That day I bid farewell both to Happy and to that old Tuncay. Both had brought me a certain distance — and I am grateful for the distance they brought me. But at the threshold, both of them had to stop. The rest I had to walk myself. And there that motif came into play again, that inner voice that never falls silent, but this time without haste, this time calm: I have not become who I am, I am becoming every day — and I now walk this path no longer behind anyone, but with my own step.

Therapist's Note – Spiritual Awakening and Mystical Experience What is happening here: After the mental explanation (the diagnosis), a person searches for a lived explanation. To know is one thing; to feel that knowledge in your flesh, your bones, your nervous system is something else entirely. In Tuncay, high awareness, the unusually deep perception of AuDHD, and a heap of unprocessed trauma meet in the need to gather it all into a single whole — not just to say "this is how I am," but to feel in his whole body that "everything is right." In research, the boundlessness described here, the state in which the "I" melts away, is called ego dissolution : an over-protective self whose alarm has been sounding for a lifetime falls silent for an instant. What remains is a sense of wholeness, connectedness, and "everything in its place" that many who have lived the experience describe as "the most meaningful moment of my life" (the work of Roland Griffiths and the Johns Hopkins team; the "meaning, sacredness, boundlessness" dimensions of mystical experience reach all the way back to William James's 1902 work The Varieties of Religious Experience ). An important and explicit warning: This is a personal experience; it is not a method, a recommendation, or an encouragement. This experience was lived abroad, under expert supervision, and within a legal framework. Work of the kind referred to here is not legal in many countries, including Turkey — which is why no substance name, amount, or method is shared in this book. No substance is needed for inner transformation; meditation, breath, and body work can open the same door, more slowly but more safely. The responsibility lies entirely with the reader. Where it comes from — body and evolution Why is a human being so open to such a deep experience of "everything is right"? Because the opposite direction is our brain's natural mode of operation. The default mode network in the brain switches on when we are not occupied with the outside world; it endlessly weaves the narrative of "I," the past-and-future, the story of "who am I, how do they see me." When it works in overdrive, this is called rumination (Nolen-Hoeksema, 1991). What is interesting is this: in both deep meditation and mystical experiences this network quiets down (Judson Brewer's brain-imaging studies with experienced meditators, 2011). When the machine that tells the "I" story pauses for a moment, what remains is a narratorless, boundless awareness — this is exactly what Tuncay calls "the ego dissolved." Stephen Porges's Polyvagal Theory explains another door: a lifelong, over-aroused nervous system scanning for threat only truly relaxes in a deep sense of safety (Porges, 2011); that feeling of "being embraced by the universe" is the nervous system surrendering fully, for the first time, to its deepest safe mode. And the "go" command, the sense of a task, has its root too: Viktor Frankl says that the most fundamental human motivation is neither pleasure nor power, but the search for meaning ( Man's Search for Meaning , 1946). For pain to become bearable, we need a "why" — evolution has shaped us as a meaning-making species. If left unaddressed — if not integrated A mystical experience, on its own, heals nothing permanently. This is the most critical point. If it is not translated into daily life, it evaporates; a few weeks later all that remains is the memory of "I once lived something beautiful." Or something more dangerous happens: a person chases after the next "great experience" and trades one escape for another — becoming someone who collects experiences but whose life never changes. There is also the danger of spiritual bypassing (the term was coined by the psychologist John Welwood): using beautiful, shining words about wholeness as a curtain to flee from concrete and ugly trauma, from unresolved anger, from confrontations left unsettled. Saying "everything is one" can be a polite way of ignoring the real pain on the table. An unprocessed awakening can carry a person even further from reality instead of healing them. Paths of healing — several doors The real work begins after the experience. There are several doors, and they all converge in a single word: grounding. • Integration work: Turning the experience from a mystical memory into daily behavior — like Tuncay turning his "go" message into a real task, into this book. In modern psychedelic research, integration sessions are considered as important as the experience itself. • Body-based approaches (Peter Levine – Somatic Experiencing): Safely calling a "dissolved" nervous system back into the body — feet on the ground, slow breath, touch. A mystical experience disembodies a person; healing is the return to the body. • Internal Family Systems (IFS – Richard Schwartz): That crowd of inner voices in Tuncay fell silent for a moment when the ego melted. But lasting peace comes from recognizing those parts (the frightened one, the following one, the controlling one) one by one and leading them. • Expressive writing (James Pennebaker, 1997): The effort to put even an experience that won't fit into words onto paper processes it in the nervous system and gives it meaning. This book itself is the product of that writing. • A meaning-oriented approach (Frankl – logotherapy): Turning the feeling of "I have a task" from an empty exhilaration into a concrete, sustainable purpose in life. • Meditation and breath: The essence of the insight in Japan — the door is inside. The door once opened by a substance can be found again, regularly, soberly and wakefully, in safety. Tuncay's bridge When I came out of that room I had a treasure in my hands — but half of the treasure was not knowing how to carry it home. At first I thought the work was done: since I had felt "everything is right," now everything would set itself right on its own. It didn't. While running after a sage in Bali, after the silence of a temple in Japan, I was in fact still keeping up the same old habit — following someone. Today I know that the experience did not heal me — it showed me the direction of healing. What healed me was the ordinary, unshowy, sober work I did every morning after it: breath, walking, writing, grounding. The miracle was a moment; healing is a habit. Sources: W. Therapist's Note – Self-Acceptance and Trauma Integration What is happening here: This chapter is, in the textbook sense, a portrait of trauma integration and self-acceptance — and importantly, it shows this not in the abstract but in concrete, measurable changes. One: episodes of anger drop from days to minutes; this is one of the clearest signs of improved emotion regulation . Two: a "gap" appears between trigger and reaction — this is the brain's shift from reactivity to responsiveness. Three: empathy that once overwhelmed turns into a conscious capacity. Four: the body holds less tension; shoulders, jaw, breath relax. But the real change is linguistically tiny, spiritually enormous: the sentence "I am wrong" gives way to the sentence "This is who I am." This is not cheap positive thinking; it is the result of long and genuine labor — the brain slowly rewired its old protective loop. Where it comes from — body and evolution When you said that morning in the kitchen that the sounds "flowed through and didn't leap up," you were actually describing a very concrete neurobiological change. The amygdala , what Bessel van der Kolk calls "the brain's smoke detector," becomes hypersensitive in trauma; it treats the smallest crackle as a threat ( The Body Keeps the Score , 2014). With healing, the regulatory areas of the forebrain (the prefrontal cortex) become able to brake this alarm again — the alarm is still there, but it no longer mistakes every sound for a fire. Stephen Porges's Polyvagal Theory makes this even clearer: without our awareness, our body is constantly scanning the environment for "am I safe?" — he calls this "neuroception" (Porges, 2011). That stillness you called "didn't leap up" is your nervous system beginning to settle into the safe mode (the ventral vagal state). When the body feels safe, the shoulders drop, the jaw loosens, the breath falls to the belly — what you described is exactly this. Your saying that empathy "opened up" is no coincidence either. The brain's mirror neuron systems and emotion-reading circuits are freed to read another's inner state once a person lowers their own threat level; while constantly on alert, there are no resources left to spare for empathy. Your observation, "first my own alarm went quiet, then I could see the other person," is, neurologically, the correct sequence. And anger "dropping from days to minutes" is the loosening of rumination — the brain's default mode network replaying the same scene over and over (Nolen-Hoeksema, 1991). And your sentence "I couldn't understand without living what I condemned" is the essence of the concept of post-traumatic growth : Tedeschi and Calhoun show that, in some people, pain can produce, alongside ruin, deepening, compassion, and meaning (1996). Viktor Frankl's logotherapy says this too: if pain is inevitable, attaching a meaning to it transforms it. If left unaddressed — possible bad outcomes This acceptance is not a permanent possession but a garden that requires tending. The old patterns don't vanish forever; their voices are merely turned down. Under stress, sleeplessness, a new loss, or an old trigger, the "I am wrong" voice, the flight response, and the victim role can all sound again at any moment. A person who entirely abandons the practice of self-compassion can quietly slide back into the old loops — and most insidious of all, miss new opportunities without noticing, because they're viewing them through old glasses. The masks can also be recalled; the "strong mask" in particular is the habit that returns fastest in hard moments. If left unaddressed, that "gap" that was gained narrows again, the door between trigger and reaction closes again, and the person finds themselves in the trap of self-blame, asking "I had healed, why did I go back?" — when in fact this is not a failure but a garden whose tending has lapsed. Paths of healing — several doors At this point there are a few paths that help; all are built on protecting and deepening what has been gained: • Self-compassion (Kristin Neff) and Compassion-Focused Therapy (Paul Gilbert): A small daily practice — "Today I can be kind to myself." To answer the critical voice inside you as you would speak to a friend. This is the most basic daily care that protects what has been gained. • Internal Family Systems (IFS – Richard Schwartz): What you called "they all sat down at the same table, and I took my place at the head" is the very heart of IFS. To see the parts (the angry protector, the frightened child, the analytical manager) not as enemies but all as members trying to protect you; with a calm, compassionate "Self" standing at the head of the table. • Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT): Your sentence "not resisting, but accepting, was the better solvent" is the essence of ACT. Instead of suppressing a thought, making room for it, watching it, and acting according to your values. • Boundary work: Exercises that turn "no" from a drama into a complete sentence — without explanation, without apology. Each small "no" strengthens the shield. • Expressive writing (Pennebaker): This book itself is a product of this therapy. Putting emotion into writing improves mood and even immunity (Pennebaker, 1997). • Body-based regulation: A long exhale stimulates the vagus nerve and draws the system into the safe mode. The calm you've gained is consolidated not with the mind but with the body. Tuncay's bridge I did most of these without knowing their names — then, as I read, I'd say once again, "so this had a name." When I first read that paradox of Carl Rogers's, I stopped: "When I accept myself as I am, only then can I change." Therapist's Note – Understanding Beyond Survival (Generativity) What is happening here: In this chapter a very distinct psychological threshold is crossed: the passage from "I want to heal" to "I want to pass something on." The psychologist Erik Erikson, in his theory of lifelong development, called this generativity — the need to go beyond oneself and to create, leave behind, and nurture something for the next generation. Erikson considered this the central task of adulthood; its opposite pole is "stagnation," that is, a person closing in only on themselves. In people who have lived through trauma and a late diagnosis, generativity is often the most powerful form of healing: your own pain turns into a tool for others; the wound, into a door. The chapter's central thesis is also psychologically very apt — the issue is not missing knowledge, but missing language . Words like "broken" or "deficient" are not neutral descriptions; they shape how a person lives themselves. Language creates reality. Where it comes from — body and evolution The human drive to "turn one's own pain into benefit for another" is not a romantic ornament; it is something etched into our nervous system. The brain's mirror neuron systems (discovered by Rizzolatti and colleagues in the 1990s) activate similar networks within us when we see another's pain or intention — this is the neurological substrate of empathy. "Recognizing" that expression in the eyes of someone who carries the same wound is not an abstract intuition, but data the body reads directly. Evolutionarily, too, human beings achieved survival not through individual heroics but through bonding and transmission; the groups that carried knowledge, protection, and meaning from generation to generation were the ones that endured. The search for meaning is itself a biological need: Viktor Frankl, in logotherapy , argued that a person's fundamental motivation is not pleasure or power but meaning ( Man's Search for Meaning ). When a meaning — especially the meaning of "benefit to another" — can be attached to pain, that same pain becomes bearable. To this is added post-traumatic growth (Tedeschi & Calhoun, 1996): some people, after shattering experiences, not only return to their former selves but gain a new meaning and depth. "Bridge" is precisely the name for this. If left unaddressed — possible bad outcomes If the drive to reach out to others from one's own pain is not worked through on healthy ground, it can veer in two directions. The first is the wounded helper trap : a person, by constantly tending to others, flees from their own unfinished interior ("I look after everyone, just not myself"). Helping becomes a new and more noble-seeming mask; yet beneath it the same flight continues. The second is burnout and compassion fatigue: the one who gives without limit begins to give from emptiness, until they run dry inside. The body, too, pays its price for this — chronic fatigue, touchiness, resentment, and in the end bitterness and withdrawal. The most ironic ending is this: the person who burns their own wick to the end trying to be a light to others can, in the end, be an example to no one. The text's inner voice already senses this; to name the danger is the first step in guarding against it. Paths of healing — several doors There are several doors to staying healthy at this threshold: • Order: first yourself, then others. That cliché on the airplane is true — you put the mask on yourself first. This is not selfishness, but the only condition of sustainability. The one who is full gives; the one who is empty leaks. • Internal Family Systems (IFS – Richard Schwartz): The voice that says "I must save everyone" is often a protective "part"; it keeps you constantly useful so as never to feel an old wound again. Rather than declaring that part the enemy, understand its intention and lighten its burden. • Compassion-Focused Therapy (Paul Gilbert) and self-compassion (Kristin Neff): Learning to set aside a drop of the compassion you show others for yourself. In Neff's words, treating yourself as you would treat a friend. • Boundary work: Learning to say "no," to place distance between a demand and your own need; this is the antidote to burnout. • Narrative therapy (White & Epston) and expressive writing (Pennebaker, 1997): Rewriting your own story in the language not of "a broken person" but of "someone who became a bridge." This book is an application of that therapy. • Meaning-based approaches (logotherapy – Frankl): If the "why" is strong enough, a person can endure almost any "how." Tuncay's bridge When I first read the word "generativity," I smiled — because the thing I'd been doing for years without knowing its name had a counterpart in Erikson. Seeing myself in the eyes of those children was, I later learned, the work of mirror neurons; so that feeling of "familiar kinship" was not a fantasy but real data my body was reading. When I read Frankl, I understood why that question in the caravan, "how much longer?", one day turned into the question "for what?": my pain had not lessened, but once tied to a meaning, it had become bearable. And if I'm to be honest — the most real struggle in this chapter is my struggle with the "wounded helper" trap. All my life I was the man who tried to save everyone and put himself last; being a bridge could also be a noble cover cut to fit that old habit. I know this, which is why I had to learn to set boundaries, to say "no," to fill my own cup first — I'm still learning, and I don't manage it every day. What I overcame was the belief that "what I lived through was in vain." What I haven't overcome, what I'm still working on, is my tendency to turn giving into an escape. Being a bridge is beautiful; but a bridge needs maintenance too, or it collapses in the middle. Sources: E. Erikson (generativity); V. Frankl, Man's Search for Meaning (logotherapy); R. Tedeschi & L. Calhoun (post-traumatic growth, 1996); G. Rizzolatti (mirror neurons); R. Schwartz (Internal Family Systems); P. Gilbert (Compassion-Focused Therapy); K. Neff (self-compassion); M. White & D. Let me say it honestly: being a bridge is not a destination, but a stance I choose anew every day. I traded the secure seat for an insecure meaning, and I'm not sorry; sitting in that seat I wasn't alive, only solid. I know the trap of helping, and I'm careful — I no longer give from emptiness, or I pause twice before giving. The biggest thing I overcame is the fear of being visible: today I can speak openly about things I was ashamed of for decades, and the sky doesn't fall on my head. What I still haven't fully resolved is my tendency to rush to another before filling my own cup; I confess it, because hiding it too would be a mask. And most important of all: I now fully believe the sentence "what I lived through was not in vain." My pain has turned into a language for others. This is not just talk. This mission was the reason I had to walk this whole road. "I am because we are." — Ubuntu, African wisdom As long as I breathe, I become. I am finished only when I no longer do.

Therapist's Note – Post-Traumatic Growth and Finding Meaning What is happening here: What is described here has a name in the research: post-traumatic growth (Tedeschi & Calhoun, 1996). A person is not always broken forever by a trauma; some, after the upheaval, build a deeper and more meaningful structure of life than before — like a broken bone that becomes stronger at the place where it knits back together. This is exactly what Tuncay is experiencing: accumulated pain, instead of collapsing inward, turns outward into a prosocial energy — the desire to "help others." Here the systems thinking of AuDHD operates not as a flaw but as a strength: where others see a single scattered fate, this mind sees a pattern, a structure, a solvable problem. The scene at the beginning of the chapter, "the needles turning the same way," is exactly this — meaning gives a vector to the pain that arrives in the mind as scattered. Where it comes from — body and evolution The "helping" impulse is not a romantic ornament; it is very ancient software etched into our nervous system. The human being achieved survival not alone but within the tribe; the individual who felt another's pain inside and reached out a hand contributed to the survival of the group. The mirror neuron system in our brain (Rizzolatti's discovery, 1990s) allows us to echo another's emotion in our own body — this is the neurological basis of empathy. What Tuncay feels in his body when he says "that child in the very back row was me" is not an abstract memory, but the living operation of this echoing system. Meaning itself is not a luxury but a biological need. The psychiatrist Viktor Frankl , who survived Auschwitz, observed who stayed alive in the camps and formulated the core of logotherapy : one who has a reason to live (a meaning, a mission, someone they love) can endure almost any how (any pain) ( Man's Search for Meaning , 1946). Neuroscience confirms this: a sense of purpose supports the brain's reward and regulation circuits and buffers the destructive effect of stress hormones. And then there is the ADHD side: the dopamine system binds with enthusiasm to new, bright, meaningful goals — which is why the voice rises while telling of the vision, why ten dreams open up at once. This is not a lack of discipline, but the way the brain's reward chemistry works. If left unaddressed — possible bad outcomes If trauma is not bound to a meaning, it remains "meaningless pain"; if the brain cannot build a story out of it, what's left is only emptiness and loss of motivation. The person has survived, but the question "for what?" goes unanswered — Frankl calls this the existential vacuum ; in an advanced stage it can open onto hopelessness, chronic meaninglessness, and depression. At the other extreme there is also a danger: if meaning is built in an unhealthy way, it turns into the "rescuer trap" — the mission to save everyone, boundless giving, and finally burnout. If the vision is not bound to concrete steps, it stays in the ADHD brain as "ten open tabs in the head": much is started, little is finished, and the inability to finish becomes a new source of guilt. Moreover, it must be said honestly: there are wounds that cannot be "closed" with meaning, like the pain of being far from one's children; trying to force these into meaning can pull a person away from genuine grief and into an unrealistic race to make amends. Paths of healing — several doors At this point there are several complementary doors: • Logotherapy (Viktor Frankl): Focuses not on erasing the pain but on binding it to a meaning and a mission . The question "who could benefit from what I have lived through?" is a therapeutic compass. • Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT – Steven Hayes): Instead of trying to destroy the pain, taking small concrete steps in the direction of one's values . It reduces a colossal goal like "a global brand" to a step that can actually be taken, like "touching a prototype fabric sample this week" — a life-saving translation for the ADHD brain. • Narrative Therapy (White & Epston): Rewriting one's own life story from a "broken person" narrative into a "person who carries meaning" narrative. This book itself is, from start to finish, a practice of narrative therapy. • Internal Family Systems (IFS – Schwartz): The "clash of two voices" in the chapter — the enthusiastic visionary and the sarcastic skeptic — is not a fight that needs to be suppressed; both are protective "parts." IFS teaches one to build a calm center inside that governs these parts. • Setting boundaries and delegating: The only way not to burn out in helping. Sharing tasks, trusting an assistant, saying "I don't have to carry everything myself" — this is not weakness but the clearest sign of growth (see Chapter 14). Turkish and German, two worlds side by side. Inner voice: "I am the bridge. Between two cultures, two ways of thinking, between pain and hope." == ection0046 [] class="body0" xmlns:epub="http://www.idpf.org/2007/ops"> Therapist's Note – Self-Actualization and Identity Integration What is happening here: Here we see the visible form of what Abraham Maslow called "self-actualization": once the core wounds have been tended to some degree, a person is pushed not inward but outward — wanting to have an effect, to make meaning, to share, to build. In Maslow's later writings, "self-transcendence" is added to this: fulfillment is no longer individual, but comes from touching others. The creative, system-building, pattern-seeing power of AuDHD finally opens up in the right environment: out of the "wrong piece" comes a builder, a storyteller, a bridge. Especially healing is the word Takiwātanga : a language that does not shame but honors can truly close a lifelong wound of identity. "Learning to stop" in Kadıköy is no small detail either: taking movement out of being an escape and turning it into a choice is an important sign of identity integration (of the path toward what Erik Erikson called "ego integrity"). Where it comes from — body and evolution The impulse to "share" has very deep biological roots. The human being is a species that owes its survival to its group; a lone ancestor would have been swallowed by the savanna, while the tribe lived on. That is why our brains code "being seen" and "making a contribution" as rewards — the dopamine that kicks in when you share a story is the same system that nourished the person who carried information back to the tribe in our ancestors. The relief felt by ten thousand people listening to someone on a stage say "I'm like this too" is no accident either: the brain's mirror neuron systems (discovered by Rizzolatti and his team in the 1990s) allow us to "try out" another person's experience in our own bodies — this is the neural basis of empathy and of the feeling "I am not alone." When Tuncay tells of someone's wound, the listener's nervous system recognizes that wound and relaxes. That "hollow opening in the stomach when the red light of the camera comes on" in the studio is real physiology too: in Stephen Porges's Polyvagal Theory this is called sympathetic arousal — the body reads the signal "I am being evaluated" like an ancient threat (Porges, 2011). The voice trembles, the mouth dries, the back of the neck sweats. But Tuncay's saying "and then I opened up" is the other side of that same theory: the interested, non-judging gaze of the faces opposite (social connection) draws the nervous system back into "safe" mode — into the ventral vagal state. A person can only be calmed by the safe presence of another person. Moreover, the sentence "once I stopped, I became reachable to people" is the adult echo of John Bowlby's attachment theory : without a secure base (an address, a continuity) one can fully approach neither oneself nor others. Tuncay went through life with an avoidant/scattered pattern; Kadıköy is the secure base he built for himself, belatedly. If left unaddressed — possible bad outcomes This threshold is as slippery as it is beautiful. The first risk: turning movement into a new escape — forever forward, so that nothing and no one can catch up. Then the sentence "the road is my form" becomes not a freedom but an excuse for fleeing; relationships always stay half-finished, roots never take hold. The second risk: the burnout of being a bridge — carrying everyone to the other side and being unable to tell anyone of one's own fatigue; in people who are neurodivergent, highly empathic, and prone to the "rescuer" role, this can lead to a silent burnout, to compassion fatigue, and to withdrawing once again. The third risk: the price of visibility — criticism, trolls, comparison; if no boundaries are set, the newly born self-esteem can become dependent on outside approval all over again (just as "ten thousand people loved it" nourishes today, "few likes" will wound just as deeply tomorrow). If left unaddressed, the wing that has opened seeks the cocoon again. Paths of healing — several doors At this threshold there are several doors that help, and all of them are about "protecting yourself while continuing to grow": • Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT – Steven Hayes): instead of solving the dilemma "to stay or to go?", acting according to value. The question becomes not "where am I?" but "which of my values am I serving?" Tuncay's saying "my form is movement" is healthy only if it is tied to a value (to share, to be a bridge). • Internal Family Systems (IFS – Richard Schwartz): seeing the voice that says "keep going" and the voice that says "stay" not as enemies, but both as parts protecting you — and letting the "I" (the Self) mediate between them. Tuncay's stopping those two voices and distinguishing them in this chapter is a textbook IFS move. • Compassion Focused Therapy (CFT – Paul Gilbert) and self-compassion (Kristin Neff): the bridge carries everyone, but the bridge needs maintenance too. Showing yourself the very same compassion you show those strangers you carry. Setting boundaries is not selfishness; it is the structural maintenance of the bridge. • Narrative therapy (White & Epston): the exact name for what Tuncay is doing. Rewriting his life from the story of "a broken person" into the story of "someone making his way in his own time and his own space." This book itself is a session of narrative therapy. • Post-traumatic growth (Tedeschi & Calhoun): what comes after the breaking is not a false "I'm healed" but a genuine deepening: new relationships, new meaning, new strength. What is visible here is exactly that — the wound is turning into material. Tuncay's bridge I lived all of this before I ever read about it — then, when I read it, I'd say, "so this has a name," as it always went. When I read Maslow, I understood that the pressure inside me — "I'll explode if I don't share" — was not an illness but a push toward the top of a pyramid; I felt relieved. When I learned about mirror neurons, I grasped why a listener's eyes well up when someone on a stage tells of their wound, and why I too am relieved by their relief — this is not magic, it's biology. Thanks to Porges, I stopped being angry at that hollow in my stomach in the studio; now I can say to it, "hello, old friend, stage fright again." And what helped me most of all: Takiwātanga . When I heard that word, what all the psychology books were trying to do, a single word did in one stroke — it honored me. Honest bridge: have I crossed over? Is the integration complete? No, not entirely. I still swing between "stay" and "go," I still sometimes notice the fatigue of being a bridge too late. But here's the difference: I now see the swinging not as an illness but as a rhythm. I don't know whether the wings have fully opened — but I am flying, and I am not falling. Like every therapist's note in this book, this too is a map I'm holding out to you: perhaps the place you call "what's wrong with me" is simply your own time and your own space. Sources: A. Maslow, Motivation and Personality (1954) and his writings on self-transcendence; E. Erikson, identity and ego integrity; S. Porges, The Polyvagal Theory (2011); J. Bowlby, attachment theory; G. Rizzolatti, mirror neurons; S. Hayes, ACT; R. Schwartz, Internal Family Systems; P. Gilbert, Compassion Focused Therapy; K. Neff, Self-Compassion; M. White & D. Epston, narrative therapy; R. Tedeschi & L. Calhoun, post-traumatic growth. Where I Stand Today This is not a closing chapter where a box finally gets ticked, but an open door — and that is exactly how it should be. I stopped trying to choose between "staying" and "going" and accepted that my form is movement. But Kadıköy taught me that I can stand still within movement too: for six months I've had an address, I give time to myself, I've become reachable to people. I carry the word Takiwātanga with me like an ID card: in my own time, in my own space. HappyTuncay, the podcasts, the television, Happyiz, the butterfly — all of them became real; not because I am perfect, but because I am real. Let me be honest: I still notice the fatigue of being a bridge too late, I still sometimes have to stop and weigh whether the "keep going" voice is escape or direction. But I can do that now — I can stop and weigh it. The cocoon has torn open. I don't know whether the wings have fully unfolded. But I am already flying. And this time I am not falling. "Traveler, there is no road; the road is made by walking." — Antonio Machado I haven't reached the end. I begin again every morning—and this road has no end.

How to Read Your Results Now look at the numbers. Lines you rated 4–5 are open wounds — the places that most run your life today, which means your priority. Lines you rated 1–2 are healed or healing ; protect them and draw strength from them. Most people think they have a single wound; usually, though, 2–3 themes feed each other (for example, “being invisible” + “over-giving” + “tying worth to looks” come from the same root). Look at the common root of your high scores: almost always it leads back to the belief “I am not enough / not worthy.” ★ A wound is not a shameful flaw; it is a compass that shows you where to begin. 4. Where to Begin — The Order Order matters. People usually want to start with the most painful wound; but a nervous system on alert cannot carry the deep work. So the suggested order is: 1) Body and safety first. Whatever theme dominates, the first task is to calm the nervous system. Because while the body is in “danger” mode, the mind cannot learn (the polyvagal logic from Chapters 1, 4, 11). Several times a day: long exhales, feet on the ground, a short walk. 2) Then self-compassion. No wound heals without softening the inner critic. Learn to speak to yourself as you would to a friend (Chapters 8, 13). 3) Then boundaries. A small “no,” a small “this doesn't feel good to me.” A boundary is self-worth in bodily form (Chapters 3, 6). 4) Then the core wound. The theme you scored highest in step 2. Now that you have safety and compassion as ground, you can touch it — ideally with professional support. 5) Meaning last. Turn your pain into a task, a line that reaches toward others (Chapters 14–16). Meaning is not the result of healing; it is what carries it. 5. Which Theme → Which Method Find your high-scoring theme; the direction beside it is drawn from that chapter's therapist's note. These are not instructions, only directions: all are walked more safely with a professional. • Invisibility / emotional neglect → journaling, sharing “what's in me today?”, connection; schema/compassion-focused approaches. • Shame / body shame → grounding + Compassion-Focused Therapy (CFT, Gilbert); reframing shame as “other people's mirror” instead of “my flaw.” • Fear-based bonding / fear of authority → “inner parent” work, Internal Family Systems (IFS), closeness exercises with a safe person; EMDR if needed. • Love / over-giving / lovebombing → boundary work, IFS, practicing “my needs are normal.” • Attachment / loss / grief → a witness for the grief (therapist/journal), making meaning; for “ambiguous loss,” specialized support. • Worth / body → self-compassion (Neff), practicing “my worth is independent of my looks.” • Guilt / responsibility → self-forgiveness; internalizing “in that moment, I did what I could with what I had.” • ‘I'm wrong’ belief / late diagnosis → the frame “I am different, a system” + structure (routine, sensory control). • Control / anxiety → breath/body, surrender practice, meditation. • Anger / emotion regulation → bodily calming + the awareness “this anger is the echo of old wounds.” 6. When Professional Help Is Essential This book is a friend, not a therapist. In the following cases, please turn to a professional (psychologist/psychiatrist) — that is not weakness, but a wise step: thoughts of self-harm or suicide; being unable to keep up daily functioning (work, sleep, eating); substances/alcohol or another addiction taking over your life; past trauma constantly flooding the present. If you are in immediate danger, call your country's emergency line or a person close to you right away. You don't have to carry it alone. ★ Asking for help is not the end of the road; it is arriving at the right place. 7. A Small Four-Week Start Big plans overwhelm; small steps transform. Four weeks, a few minutes a day: Week 1 — Body: Twice a day, 1 minute of long exhales + feet on the ground, saying “right now I am safe.” Week 2 — Compassion: Each evening, write one single kind sentence for a moment you blamed yourself that day — the way you'd speak to a friend. Week 3 — Boundary: Say one small “no” or “I'm not comfortable with this”; then write down what you felt. Week 4 — Connection: Open up to a trusted person or a neurodivergent community with one single sentence: “Sometimes I feel this way too.” After four weeks, do the assessment from step 2 again. Your numbers don't have to change — it's enough that you notice them. Because healing is not an arrival, but a direction.
