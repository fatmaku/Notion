Now I know: I have a task. Not a reward—not money, fame, or applause. A purpose. And purpose, unlike profit, is the one thing that multiplies the more you give it away.

Inner voice: "I will help. I will tell. I will show them they are not alone. To those who feel as I do, those who stare at the ceiling at three in the morning saying 'is there something wrong with me?', those who sit silent at the back of the classroom asking 'why am I like this?', I will say: 'You are not wrong. You just haven't looked into the right mirror yet. I will hold that mirror out to you.'"

Therapist's Note – Spiritual Awakening and Mystical Experience

What is happening here: After the mental explanation (the diagnosis), a person searches for a lived explanation. To know is one thing; to feel that knowledge in your flesh, your bones, your nervous system is something else entirely. In Tuncay, high awareness, the unusually deep perception of AuDHD, and a heap of unprocessed trauma meet in the need to gather it all into a single whole — not just to say "this is how I am," but to feel in his whole body that "everything is right." In research, the boundlessness described here, the state in which the "I" melts away, is called ego dissolution: an over-protective self whose alarm has been sounding for a lifetime falls silent for an instant. What remains is a sense of wholeness, connectedness, and "everything in its place" that many who have lived the experience describe as "the most meaningful moment of my life" (the work of Roland Griffiths and the Johns Hopkins team; the "meaning, sacredness, boundlessness" dimensions of mystical experience reach all the way back to William James's 1902 work The Varieties of Religious Experience).

An important and explicit warning: This is a personal experience; it is not a method, a recommendation, or an encouragement. This experience was lived abroad, under expert supervision, and within a legal framework. Work of the kind referred to here is not legal in many countries, including Turkey — which is why no substance name, amount, or method is shared in this book. No substance is needed for inner transformation; meditation, breath, and body work can open the same door, more slowly but more safely. The responsibility lies entirely with the reader.

Where it comes from — body and evolution

Why is a human being so open to such a deep experience of "everything is right"? Because the opposite direction is our brain's natural mode of operation. The default mode network in the brain switches on when we are not occupied with the outside world; it endlessly weaves the narrative of "I," the past-and-future, the story of "who am I, how do they see me." When it works in overdrive, this is called rumination (Nolen-Hoeksema, 1991). What is interesting is this: in both deep meditation and mystical experiences this network quiets down (Judson Brewer's brain-imaging studies with experienced meditators, 2011). When the machine that tells the "I" story pauses for a moment, what remains is a narratorless, boundless awareness — this is exactly what Tuncay calls "the ego dissolved." Stephen Porges's Polyvagal Theory explains another door: a lifelong, over-aroused nervous system scanning for threat only truly relaxes in a deep sense of safety (Porges, 2011); that feeling of "being embraced by the universe" is the nervous system surrendering fully, for the first time, to its deepest safe mode. And the "go" command, the sense of a task, has its root too: Viktor Frankl says that the most fundamental human motivation is neither pleasure nor power, but the search for meaning (Man's Search for Meaning, 1946). For pain to become bearable, we need a "why" — evolution has shaped us as a meaning-making species.

If left unaddressed — if not integrated

A mystical experience, on its own, heals nothing permanently. This is the most critical point. If it is not translated into daily life, it evaporates; a few weeks later all that remains is the memory of "I once lived something beautiful." Or something more dangerous happens: a person chases after the next "great experience" and trades one escape for another — becoming someone who collects experiences but whose life never changes. There is also the danger of spiritual bypassing (the term was coined by the psychologist John Welwood): using beautiful, shining words about wholeness as a curtain to flee from concrete and ugly trauma, from unresolved anger, from confrontations left unsettled. Saying "everything is one" can be a polite way of ignoring the real pain on the table. An unprocessed awakening can carry a person even further from reality instead of healing them.

Paths of healing — several doors

The real work begins after the experience. There are several doors, and they all converge in a single word: grounding.

• Integration work: Turning the experience from a mystical memory into daily behavior — like Tuncay turning his "go" message into a real task, into this book. In modern psychedelic research, integration sessions are considered as important as the experience itself.
• Body-based approaches (Peter Levine – Somatic Experiencing): Safely calling a "dissolved" nervous system back into the body — feet on the ground, slow breath, touch. A mystical experience disembodies a person; healing is the return to the body.
• Internal Family Systems (IFS – Richard Schwartz): That crowd of inner voices in Tuncay fell silent for a moment when the ego melted. But lasting peace comes from recognizing those parts (the frightened one, the following one, the controlling one) one by one and leading them.
• Expressive writing (James Pennebaker, 1997): The effort to put even an experience that won't fit into words onto paper processes it in the nervous system and gives it meaning. This book itself is the product of that writing.
• A meaning-oriented approach (Frankl – logotherapy): Turning the feeling of "I have a task" from an empty exhilaration into a concrete, sustainable purpose in life.
• Meditation and breath: The essence of the insight in Japan — the door is inside. The door once opened by a substance can be found again, regularly, soberly and wakefully, in safety.

Tuncay's bridge

When I came out of that room I had a treasure in my hands — but half of the treasure was not knowing how to carry it home. At first I thought the work was done: since I had felt "everything is right," now everything would set itself right on its own. It didn't. A few weeks later that feeling began to fade and a voice inside me said "you must live it once more" — and right at that moment I was, by a hair's breadth, on the verge of trading one escape for another. What saved me was that simple truth in Japan: the door was inside. Then I understood that the real work is not inside the ocean, but in the calm breath that comes after it. While running after a sage in Bali, after the silence of a temple in Japan, I was in fact still keeping up the same old habit — following someone. Seeing myself in the way Happy followed me ended all that spiritual tourism with a single sentence: a path becomes yours only when you walk it yourself. Today I know that the experience did not heal me — it showed me the direction of healing. What healed me was the ordinary, unshowy, sober work I did every morning after it: breath, walking, writing, grounding. The miracle was a moment; healing is a habit.

Where I Stand Today

What I have truly kept from this period is neither the substance nor the spectacle — it is a single sentence that has worked its way into every one of my cells: "There is nothing wrong with me." I did not live this once; I go on living it, a little more each day. Surrender — "no plan, no forcing, only flow" — turned from a passing intoxication into a lasting stance toward life. I no longer control everything to death; where the control loosened, I saw that I could breathe for the first time. And I understood that for the depth I sought I needed no distant country, no sage's courtyard, no room beyond the threshold: it was always inside me. Let me be honest — have I fully transcended it? No. Some mornings that old hunger for control still knocks at the door; I still occasionally miss "something great." The difference is this: I now recognize that voice and I don't run after it. Today I meditate, I ground myself, I walk — sober and awake. This is the real fruit. Not the ocean of energy; the simple, silent breath that comes after it, taken with my own step.
