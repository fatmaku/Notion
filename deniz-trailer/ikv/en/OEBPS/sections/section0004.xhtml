Therapist's Note – Existential Crisis

What is happening here: A person with high self-awareness and AuDHD sits at ground zero, asking the most radical of questions: Who am I? This is not self-pity. It's the typical nighttime state of a late-diagnosed neurodivergent brain. For decades the brain has been oscillating between two basic states: the autistic side scanning relentlessly for threat (the environment, sounds, faces, the "what ifs"), the ADHD side relentlessly seeking stimulation and reward. When both run in the same head, a chronic hum remains in between: "Is something wrong with me?"

Where it comes from — body and evolution

This "How much longer?", this nighttime vigil, is actually a very old piece of software. Deep in the human brain, the amygdala is the alarm bell that has kept us alive for millions of years; Bessel van der Kolk calls it "the brain's smoke detector" (The Body Keeps the Score, 2014). In the Stone Age, our ancestors who stayed alert at night and interpreted a rustle as "maybe a predator" survived; whether the ones who relaxed survived is unknown. So threat-scanning is not a malfunction but an inheritance. Stephen Porges's Polyvagal Theory calls this "neuroception": without our awareness, our body scans the environment at every moment, asking "am I safe?" (Porges, 2011). In a neurodivergent nervous system, this scanning runs more sensitively, more loudly.

The "constantly thinking mind" also has an address: the brain's default mode network kicks in when we're not occupied with the outside world and constructs the past and the future. When it overworks, this is called rumination — the same thought coming around again and again (Nolen-Hoeksema's depression research, 1991). Your staring at the light on the ceiling at three in the morning and saying "how much longer?" is, scientifically, the night shift of this network.

If left unaddressed — possible bad outcomes

If the existential question is left unanswered and unsupported, it can turn into an existential pit. The possible course: an entrenched belief of "I am wrong," recurring depressive episodes, social withdrawal, disrupted sleep, chronic anxiety; in advanced stages, a sense of meaninglessness and — it must be said plainly — suicidal thoughts. The question "Who am I?" quietly slides toward "Why do I even exist?" In late-diagnosed adults this slide is unfortunately common; that's why a diagnosis can sometimes, in the fullest sense of the word, save a life.

Paths of healing — not a single road, but several doors

At this point there are a few methods that help, and they're all built on "turning the question from an enemy into a companion":

• Writing / externalization: Instead of spinning the inner voice around in your head, writing for 5–10 minutes a day. Pennebaker's research on "expressive writing" shows that putting emotion into words improves the immune system and mood (Pennebaker, 1997). This book itself is a product of that therapy.
• Internal Family Systems (IFS – Richard Schwartz): Seeing those different voices inside you (the critic, the frightened one, the protector) not as enemies to be suppressed, but as "parts" that are all trying to protect you. What you call your "inner voice" is exactly IFS's field of work.
• Somatic approaches (Peter Levine – Somatic Experiencing) and body awareness: A brain in constant alarm is calmed not by logic, but only by the body. Planting your feet on the ground, slow breathing (a long exhale stimulates the vagus nerve and pulls the system into "safe" mode — Polyvagal Theory).
• Self-compassion (Kristin Neff) and Compassion-Focused Therapy (Paul Gilbert): Answering the "I am wrong" voice as if speaking to a friend.
• Connecting: Contact with other neurodivergent people. There's no medicine that dissolves the "I am wrong" belief as fast as the sentence "I have the same thing too."

Tuncay's bridge

For years I applied most of these methods on my own, without knowing their names — then, as I read more, I'd say, "so this had a name." When I read Porges, I understood that freezing as a child at the sound of my father's keys was not a "lack of character" but neuroception; I felt relieved. Van der Kolk's sentence, "the body keeps the score," explained why all those stomach clenches are still there. When I learned IFS, I began to see the crowd in my head not as "madness" but as a family meeting — and I took the head of the table. What I really did was this: I took the science and spread it like a map over my own life. That map turned the sentence "I am broken" into the sentence "I am an explainable system." And so every therapist's note in this book is an attempt to hand you that map too.

Sources: B. van der Kolk, The Body Keeps the Score (2014); S. Porges, The Polyvagal Theory (2011); R. Schwartz, Internal Family Systems; K. Neff, Self-Compassion; J. Pennebaker, expressive writing research; I. D. Yalom, existential psychology.

Where I Stand Today

I still ask the question "Who am I?" The difference is this: it no longer frightens me. It used to be an accusation; today it's a door. I stopped forcing a definitive answer, and the moment I stopped forcing it, things got easier. So I didn't manage to get rid of the question — I managed to make peace with it. Today I can say this: the problem wasn't me; I was just in the wrong environment for a long time. And that is a difference that changes an entire life.

"Yesterday I was clever, so I wanted to change the world. Today I am wise, so I am changing myself."

— Rumi

I am not a destination, I am a becoming. And this becoming ends only at my last breath.
