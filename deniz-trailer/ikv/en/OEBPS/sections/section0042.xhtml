Chapter 15 – Mission, Vision, and the Road

Because that caravan was also the beginning of an adventure. One day I jumped into a caravan with a traveling companion and set off from Istanbul. We were going to raise awareness about living with autism, ADHD, and cancer. “The brain working differently is not a disability; it’s possible to beat cancer, get through hard roads, and still be happy” — that’s what we went city to city to say. We traveled to fifteen cities. A caravan, a direction, a feeling exploding inside me. Along the way we prepared answers to questions: “Is medication a must? No, but for some it helps a great deal. Is ADHD something to be ashamed of? No — their brains tire from too much sound and light, they experience sensory overload.” One day I wrote to my brother: “We’ve set off, can you make something like an adventure trailer from the footage you have?” Because this wasn’t just a trip; it was that “I have to tell this,” built up in me for years, finally taking to the streets. We fell, we got up, but we were strong.

But not every adventure ends happily. With that companion, our paths later parted. Some people walk with a mask; on stage they’re one person, backstage another. I was hurt. That old question rose in me again: why do I always trust too much, why do I always give too much? But what that journey left behind stayed: the seed of the great dream I’m writing down today — to gather a story from every province of this country — was planted in exactly those fifteen cities, in that caravan. The caravan is gone, the journey stayed.

At some point I understood: nothing I had lived through had been in vain.

That "some point" had its own hour, its own place, its own smell. I remember it. Something inside me settled into place quietly, yet with an undeniable certainty — like the bolt of a door sliding home into its socket. Click. Everything that had lain scattered until that moment — all the falls, all the nights, all the "why me?" questions — lined up along a single axis. As if hundreds of needles, strewn across the floor for years, had all turned the same way the instant an invisible magnet was brought near the table. Not one of them had changed. But now they were all facing one direction.

Inner voice: "All this pain, all the falls, all the loneliness — these cannot have happened just to be lived through."

As I formed this sentence in my mind, something strange was happening in my body. In the center of my chest, that tightness I had carried for years — like a fist, a weight lodged behind the bones — was not loosening, no. But it was shifting. It was rising, from the stomach upward, toward the throat; ceasing to be a weight and turning into a pressure, the pressure of a sentence that wanted to be spoken. The thing inside me no longer wanted to collapse. It wanted to speak.

Inner voice: I paused for a moment. "You were trying to heal yourself. When did you start thinking about others?" The other voice answered, calmly: "At the very moment I began to heal. Because to heal means to step out of being imprisoned within the pain and to be able to look at it from the outside. And when I looked from the outside, I saw people other than myself."

I didn't just survive. I saw. I learned. I burned. I fell. I rose again. And now I need to speak. I have a mission. Not money. Not fame. Impact.

As I spoke this word — "impact" — I heard my own voice, as if from the outside. A skeptic inside me jumped in at once, as always, with a shrug.

Inner voice: "Impact, eh? What a grand word. Who do you think you are? A man living in a caravan, two failed marriages behind him, far from his children, and he stands up and says 'impact.'" — Then the other voice, gentler: "That very man. Because that man knows the lie. And he no longer has it in him to lie."

Inner voice: "I will help people. But how? Only as myself. As something real."

I can't save anyone. It took me years to say this — because a rescuer always lived inside me, someone trying to carry everyone, to shoulder everyone's wound. But being a rescuer is a trap; you burn out, while the other person never learns to walk. So now I form a different sentence: I can't save anyone. But I can show the way. My intention is this: that children not be left alone. That people who have suffered bullying find themselves. That neurodivergent people not believe themselves "broken."

Inner voice: "I am here for them. Let my voice be their voice."

Finding the Millions of Tuncays

When I close my eyes, I see them. I truly see them — face by face, classroom by classroom. There are millions of Tuncays in Turkey. The child sitting in silence; at the very back of the row, shoulders caved inward, doodling something in the margin of his notebook, flinching when the teacher reads out his name. The young one daydreaming at the back of the class; eyes on the window, mind three continents away, scolded for "drifting off again." The girl they call "smart but lazy" — she will carry that sentence for years, that sentence stuck to the back of her neck like a brand.

I know the smell of those classrooms. Chalk dust, the metallic breath of an unwarmed radiator, the damp heat of crowded bodies, the cold air slipping in through a window left open and forgotten. I know the creak of those desks, the sound of a pencil scraping the blackboard, the sharp shriek of the bell. Because that child was me. That very back row was me.

I want to find them. Not to judge them — but to guide them rightly, to strengthen them. To say, instead of "There is something wrong with you," "Your system works differently; come, let's get to know it together." Because I know how a single sentence can turn a life around.

Inner voice: "What if someone had said this sentence to me thirty years ago? What if, in that very back row, a teacher had crouched down beside me, looked into my eyes, and said, 'you are not broken, you just work differently'? All those years… maybe they wouldn't have passed so alone." — The counter-voice: "But they did pass. And maybe that's exactly why you can say it now. Because it wasn't said to you, you have a whole lifetime ahead to make sure it gets said to someone else."

For thirty years no one said that sentence to me. I want to say it to them early.

★ More often than not, what saves a person is not some grand miracle but a single right sentence spoken at the right time.

Couples and Relationships

Relationships today are deeply broken. Because no one knows themselves. Two people, each lost within themselves, trying to find each other — like two blind people groping for each other in the dark. They touch, but they cannot recognize each other. Because the one whose hand reaches out does not even know his own hand.

Inner voice: Both my marriages ended. There is still a bitterness as I say this, I won't deny it — a swallow, the eyes slipping aside for a moment. "And you're going to talk about relationships? You tried twice, and twice it fell apart." — But the other voice: "That's exactly why. It's not those who succeed but those who fall and rise who know where the road has its potholes. I know the map of those potholes. Because I fell into every one of them, one by one."

I want to explain people to themselves, to their partners, and to the bond between them. What it means for both people to know their own nervous system; what it means, when one goes on alert, for the other to read it not as an attack but as a cry for help; what it means to see that beneath the sentence "you don't understand me" there usually lies the sentence "I don't understand myself" — this is what I want to explain.

★ A healthy relationship begins with a person knowing themselves.

A Brand Idea: Starting with Clothing

My vision doesn't stay only in words; it turns into something I can hold in my hands too. As I write this, my fingers move involuntarily to the label on the collar of my shirt — that small, stiff scrap of fabric, right in the middle of the back of my neck, that tiny enemy that bothers me all day long. I know in my body what sensory sensitivity means in autism: how a label itches the back of the neck, how a stiff seam occupies the mind all day, how the wrong fabric turns a person into an enemy of their own skin.

Inner voice: For others this is "a small detail." For me it is an alarm that keeps ringing in the background all day long. A corner of my brain is busy all day with that label, that seam; as if someone inside is whispering endlessly, "there's something wrong here, there's something wrong here." No one hears it. But I can't focus.

That's why I'm launching a clothing-design project: garments that don't itch, that are tagless, soft, and allow for stimming (self-soothing movements). Products that truly do neurodivergent people good, that are needed, locally made. My dream is not a small one: to turn this into a global brand that goes out from Turkey to the world. Because awareness spreads not only through talk but also through a fabric that people put on every single day.

Inner voice: "There you go again. In one sentence it's 'a small project,' three sentences later it's 'a global brand.' Take a look at the brakes on that head of yours." — I smile. "Yes. That's me. A browser with ten open tabs. But this time, I'll actually keep one of the tabs open. Just one. For now."

The New Generation and the Vision

New children are coming. Faster. Deeper. Unlike anything before. Watching them, I feel a mixture of two things: admiration on the one hand, and on the other a familiarity that aches inside me. Because I see myself in them — but a sharper, brighter version of myself, one that woke up earlier. But the system isn't ready for them. The system is still built for a single type of child; get in line, be quiet, sit down, walk at the same pace as everyone else. Yet these children vibrate at a different frequency.

Inner voice: "They need space. I want to create that space."

A pioneering role in the field of neurodivergence in Turkey. Artificial intelligence. Science. But always human-centered. Technology, not to change the human being, but to build a bridge to their differently working mind.

This vision didn't come out of nowhere. Before I came to Turkey, I trained as a neurodiversity educator and began life coaching. My company will be based in Germany; I already work internationally — so I gained the toolkit to train both neurotypical and neurodivergent people. Although I myself have ADHD and autism, although I brought my own research and knowledge from around the world, that "was not enough"; out of necessity I also completed the train-the-trainer certification. On that journey through all 81 provinces of Turkey, I additionally trained in the field of ADHD — my certificate is, if desired, visible in the state portal (e-Devlet) too. But I must say this openly: our country's obsession with diplomas often crowds out our own research and our own thinking; it pushes us to walk by rote and to repeat old, wrong information. I collected the diplomas, yes — but I never worshipped them. I have always given priority to researching, questioning, and thinking for myself. Because that is exactly what the title of this book says: I am the question.

Inner voice: "This is my road. I am the bridge. Between Germany and Turkey, between science and spirituality, between pain and meaning." — Then I hesitate for a moment: "Being a bridge sounds nice. But everyone crosses over a bridge, the bridge always stays at the very bottom, it carries the most weight." — The other voice: "So be it. I know how to carry weight. My whole life has been spent carrying weight. At least this time, let the weight I carry serve some purpose."

My Way of Life

When I close my eyes, I see it too — a morning in the future. My dream: a garden where I grow my own food. The wet, deep, living smell of the soil; the damp earth running through my hands, the brown traces left under my nails. Chickens — their flustered, cheerful clucking in the early hours of the morning, scurrying around my feet. By the sea. The sharp taste of salt in the air, the never-silent rhythm of the waves, that first instant on the horizon when the morning light touches the water.

A café — a place where people come to talk, to share, to breathe freely. The steam of brewing tea, the clinking of glasses, someone weeping quietly in a corner and no one judging them. A tiny house: small but free. And a caravan: so that whenever I want, I can simply hit the road.

Inner voice: "I'm not tied down. I'm free." — But right after, quietly, an honest whisper: "Or is this thing I call freedom just a pretty name for running away from attachment? Is the caravan a dream, or an escape hatch?" — A long silence. "Maybe both. Maybe freedom is being able to choose to stay even though you know you could run."

The Children

And then we come to the hardest place. Here the sentences grow heavy, my fingers slow on the keyboard. My greatest dream is to be with them. My four daughters. But I know: they are growing up. And right now I am missing this time.

As I write this sentence, something catches in my throat — a real, physical knot. My eyes burn. Because this is not something I have overcome. This is a place that is still bleeding. When I imagine the garden, the café, the brand, my voice rises with excitement; but when I come here, it falls silent.

Inner voice: "This is a bitter truth. But love still doesn't end." — The counter-voice, harsher, not letting go of me: "Love doesn't end, but time does. Their childhood happens only once, and you are not there. No dream, no brand, no mission can make up for that." — The first voice, broken but standing upright: "I know. I'm not trying to make up for it. I'm only trying to hold what's left to me in the best way I can."

I can't control everything. This sentence is perhaps the most expensively learned sentence of my life. But there is one thing I can do: be a good person.

Inner voice: "That is enough." — And this time, the skeptic inside me fell silent. It did not object. Because it too knew: sometimes this is the only honest sentence we have. And this sentence, small as it may seem, is enough to keep a person standing.

Now I know one thing: my mission is to tell, to share, to help. And to go.

★ A life with purpose gives direction.

Therapist's Note – Post-Traumatic Growth and Finding Meaning

What is happening here: What is described here has a name in the research: post-traumatic growth (Tedeschi & Calhoun, 1996). A person is not always broken forever by a trauma; some, after the upheaval, build a deeper and more meaningful structure of life than before — like a broken bone that becomes stronger at the place where it knits back together. This is exactly what Tuncay is experiencing: accumulated pain, instead of collapsing inward, turns outward into a prosocial energy — the desire to "help others." Here the systems thinking of AuDHD operates not as a flaw but as a strength: where others see a single scattered fate, this mind sees a pattern, a structure, a solvable problem. The scene at the beginning of the chapter, "the needles turning the same way," is exactly this — meaning gives a vector to the pain that arrives in the mind as scattered.

Where it comes from — body and evolution

The "helping" impulse is not a romantic ornament; it is very ancient software etched into our nervous system. The human being achieved survival not alone but within the tribe; the individual who felt another's pain inside and reached out a hand contributed to the survival of the group. The mirror neuron system in our brain (Rizzolatti's discovery, 1990s) allows us to echo another's emotion in our own body — this is the neurological basis of empathy. What Tuncay feels in his body when he says "that child in the very back row was me" is not an abstract memory, but the living operation of this echoing system.

Meaning itself is not a luxury but a biological need. The psychiatrist Viktor Frankl, who survived Auschwitz, observed who stayed alive in the camps and formulated the core of logotherapy: one who has a reason to live (a meaning, a mission, someone they love) can endure almost any how (any pain) (Man's Search for Meaning, 1946). Neuroscience confirms this: a sense of purpose supports the brain's reward and regulation circuits and buffers the destructive effect of stress hormones. And then there is the ADHD side: the dopamine system binds with enthusiasm to new, bright, meaningful goals — which is why the voice rises while telling of the vision, why ten dreams open up at once. This is not a lack of discipline, but the way the brain's reward chemistry works.

If left unaddressed — possible bad outcomes

If trauma is not bound to a meaning, it remains "meaningless pain"; if the brain cannot build a story out of it, what's left is only emptiness and loss of motivation. The person has survived, but the question "for what?" goes unanswered — Frankl calls this the existential vacuum; in an advanced stage it can open onto hopelessness, chronic meaninglessness, and depression. At the other extreme there is also a danger: if meaning is built in an unhealthy way, it turns into the "rescuer trap" — the mission to save everyone, boundless giving, and finally burnout. If the vision is not bound to concrete steps, it stays in the ADHD brain as "ten open tabs in the head": much is started, little is finished, and the inability to finish becomes a new source of guilt. Moreover, it must be said honestly: there are wounds that cannot be "closed" with meaning, like the pain of being far from one's children; trying to force these into meaning can pull a person away from genuine grief and into an unrealistic race to make amends.

Paths of healing — several doors

At this point there are several complementary doors:

• Logotherapy (Viktor Frankl): Focuses not on erasing the pain but on binding it to a meaning and a mission. The question "who could benefit from what I have lived through?" is a therapeutic compass.
• Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT – Steven Hayes): Instead of trying to destroy the pain, taking small concrete steps in the direction of one's values. It reduces a colossal goal like "a global brand" to a step that can actually be taken, like "touching a prototype fabric sample this week" — a life-saving translation for the ADHD brain.
• Narrative Therapy (White & Epston): Rewriting one's own life story from a "broken person" narrative into a "person who carries meaning" narrative. This book itself is, from start to finish, a practice of narrative therapy.
• Internal Family Systems (IFS – Schwartz): The "clash of two voices" in the chapter — the enthusiastic visionary and the sarcastic skeptic — is not a fight that needs to be suppressed; both are protective "parts." IFS teaches one to build a calm center inside that governs these parts.
• Setting boundaries and delegating: The only way not to burn out in helping. Sharing tasks, trusting an assistant, saying "I don't have to carry everything myself" — this is not weakness but the clearest sign of growth (see Chapter 14).

Tuncay's bridge

I felt the sentence "I have a mission" long before I had even read Frankl — but I didn't know how to name it; I even sometimes asked myself, "is this grandiosity?" Then I read Frankl and I felt relieved: so drawing meaning from pain is not arrogance but the healthiest way of surviving. When I learned of Tedeschi and Calhoun's "post-traumatic growth," I understood why all those falls had brought me to this table — light was passing through to others from the places where I had broken. But I have to be honest, because that is the word of this book: the lesson I am still learning is to get the measure right. The rescuer inside me wants to carry everyone at once; my ADHD mind wants to open ten dreams at once. My brakes are still weak. That's why I have now hired an assistant, I share the tasks, I trust others — I learned, with the most expensive lesson, that I don't have to carry control alone. And I also learned this: the pain of being far from my daughters is not a wound to be "closed" by any mission. I'm not trying to turn it into meaning. I'm only learning to stand beside it. Once again I spread science like a map over my own life — but this time the map also has "unfixable" regions, and I am learning to leave them as they are.

Sources: V. Frankl, Man's Search for Meaning (1946); R. Tedeschi & L. Calhoun, Post-Traumatic Growth Theory (1996); S. Hayes, ACT; M. White & D. Epston, Narrative Therapy; R. Schwartz, Internal Family Systems; G. Rizzolatti, mirror neurons.

Where I Stand Today

I found the meaning — the answer to this chapter's question is a clear "yes." Out of "why all this pain?" was born "what is this pain for?", and that "what for" is carrying me. What I am still learning is to get the measure right: not all of it at once, not trying to save everyone, setting boundaries so the fire doesn't burn me up. That is precisely why I now hire an assistant, share the work and the tasks, trust others — I learned that I don't have to carry control alone. Living together with my daughters remains an open pain I cannot control; and let me be honest, I don't gloss over it by "turning it into meaning" — some wounds stay open, and standing honestly beside them is also a kind of maturity. But I have accepted what I have: being a good person and lending my voice to those who don't yet have one. That is enough. That is truly enough.

"He who has a 'why' to live for can bear almost any 'how.'"

— Friedrich Nietzsche (a saying Viktor Frankl often quoted)

I am not complete; I become a little more each day — and this becoming ends only at my last breath.
