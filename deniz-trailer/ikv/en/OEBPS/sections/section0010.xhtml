Chapter 3 – Bullying, Shame, and the First Fracture

School, for me, was not a building. It was a battlefield.

I am not exaggerating when I say that. For most of my childhood, I woke in the mornings with a warm nausea in my stomach. Before I had even opened my eyes, before the daylight had seeped past the edge of the curtain, my body was already preparing for that day's war: shoulders up, jaw locked, breath shallow. For most children, school was a day. For me, it was a front line; and every morning I walked onto that line with a single shelter in my hand — my own silence.

Every year I was the new kid. New school. New class. New people. But the story always stayed the same. My family moved to a different neighborhood almost every year. To this day I still haven't quite figured out why. The suitcases were packed, the boxes were taped shut, a van would arrive, and one room would turn into another. And every time, just like those boxes, I was lifted up and set down somewhere else — but unlike the boxes, what was inside me cracked a little more with each move.

Inner voice: You know how people say "putting down roots"? I never managed to put down roots. I'd barely reach my first thin tendril into the soil before someone would lift the pot and carry it to another window. Picture a plant — constantly uprooted, constantly pressed into new soil. After a while that plant gives up on rooting. "What's the point?" it says. And that's what I did. I learned to grow my roots inward — somewhere no one could tear them out.

The first day… always the same looks. They'd start at my feet and slowly climb upward. "Who's this?" "Where'd he come from?" "Why is he different?" Those looks had a weight to them; a physical weight. The moment I stepped through the classroom door, thirty pairs of eyes would turn to me at once, and in that instant the air seemed to harden. I'd swallow. My step would slow. And that three-second walk — the three seconds from the door to the empty desk — felt to me like three minutes.

The Smell of a Classroom, the Body of a Child

The linoleum floor in the corridor was old and sticky, and it smelled of cleaning supplies. That smell is still in my nose: sharp, chemical, faintly sweet — supposedly clean, but beneath it, years upon years of accumulated shoe leather, sweat, and wet wool. Chalk dust hung in the air, clung to my nose, made my throat itch. The light from the window made that dust visible: millions of tiny particles turning slowly in the air, dancing in a slice of sunlight. Sometimes I'd stare at that dust and lose myself — until a teacher's voice or a burst of laughter pulled me back.

In the classroom, sweat, wet coats, and cheap laundry detergent all mingled together. The radiator clanked, steam rose from the coats hung over it, and condensation settled on the glass. When I sat down, the desk creaked — those wooden desks that had carried the weight of children piled up over years, carved, scratched, filled with names. My body didn't seem to quite fit there. I was fat, and the children noticed it right away. The edge of the desk pressed into my belly; so I'd try to pull myself in, as if by taking up less space I'd be less visible.

Inner voice: Shrinking. That was my one and only skill. I couldn't shrink my body, but I could shrink my presence. Talk less, move less, laugh less, breathe less. As if somewhere there was an equation: the more invisible I am, the safer I am. Years later I learned that the equation was wrong. Being invisible wasn't safe — it was just lonely.

The looks… the laughter… the whispers… But the worst wasn't the things said out loud. The worst was the things only felt. A single glance was enough. A raised eyebrow, a contemptuous curl of the lip — and shame would settle in my stomach like a stone. My cheeks would burn; I still remember that fire, a heat spreading all the way to my earlobes that I could never hide no matter what I did. My ears would ring, loud enough that I could hear my own pulse. My throat would go dry, and I couldn't swallow. As if the whole class were scanning me with X-ray eyes and saying, "Look, there's a part that's wrong."

Inner voice: "I'm ugly." "I'm not enough." "I'm not lovable."

And in time these sentences became my own voice. That was the cruelest part of it. Even when no one said a thing, I was saying it to myself. Every morning in front of the mirror, every night in bed. This is the real insidiousness of bullying: after a while, you don't even need the bullies anymore. You've memorized the voice so well that you keep it going yourself. Even after all the bullies out there had gone home and turned off their lights, that voice in my head kept working its shift. A bully working for free, never tiring, never taking a day off.

Inner voice: What if that voice wasn't right? Back then I didn't even dare to think it. "What if they're all wrong? What if I'm not actually wrong, just in the wrong place?" It took me thirty years to be able to form that sentence. That child couldn't form it. All that child had was accusation, not defense.

"Why Don't You Have Any Friends?"

Being the new kid all the time is one of the hardest things for a child. Never putting down roots. Never being able to build a friendship before the next move. Never being able to settle in before leaving again. Just as I'd grow used to a face, just as I'd memorize a name, just as I'd learn to breathe beside someone — the boxes would be taped shut again. Then you come home, and on top of all that you hear this: "Why don't you have any friends?"

Inner voice: "How could I? I just got here. I don't even know their names properly. You started me from zero every single time." Of course I could never say it out loud. It would turn inside me, turn and turn and fade. Because at that age I'd learned: protesting didn't stop the moving. It only made that evening harder.

That question — "Why don't you have any friends?" — was like a knife, but the blade was turned toward me. As if having no friends were a choice I'd made, a flaw of mine. As if I were deliberately refusing to be warm, to join in the games, to say the right words at the right moment. But I didn't know those words. The social world of an AuDHD child is like a book that others read fluently from birth, while I tried to decipher every page of it separately, by hand, in a language all its own. While everyone spoke fluently, I was still sounding out the letters.

At first I stayed silent. Because I was afraid. Because I didn't know. Because I had learned that silence is the safest path. Be silent, be unseen, don't move, survive. That was the only strategy of those childhood years. But one day something happened. I couldn't take it anymore.

The First Punch – A Door Opening Inside Me

That day the biggest boy in the class threw out another taunt. Like always. I don't remember what he said — the words had long since lost their importance. What mattered was that everything that had been building up until that moment, all those looks, whispers, moves, all those "why don't you have any friends," gathered inside me at once. Picture a dam — one that had filled drop by drop over years, a dam no one had noticed. That day the last drop fell.

But this time something broke inside me. I clenched my fist; I felt my fingers dig into my palm. My heart was pounding, as if it would shatter my chest. I could hear my own pulse in my ears — that old ringing, but this time not from fear, from something else. My face was burning again, but this was a different fire: not the fire of shame, but of reaching the very edge of something. Time slowed. And for the first time, I fought back. I hit him.

Silence. The whole class went quiet. As if time had stopped. Everyone froze. The chalk dust hung in the air, even the clanking of the radiator seemed to cut out. In that moment a door opened inside me. Loud, creaking. A door rusted for years, unaccustomed to opening.

Inner voice: "Whoa… I'm strong." That sentence exploded in my head that day like a clap of thunder. For the first time in my life, I felt that a power outside me was not over me, but that I was over it. It was like a kind of intoxication. And I'll admit it: that intoxication was dangerous. Because what I learned that day both saved me and, for years, pulled me in the wrong direction.

That day I learned that if you don't allow it, they can't keep going. But that was only one side of the story. On the outside, I grew stronger. Inside, I stayed the same child. Ashamed. Afraid. Wanting to be loved. Wearing a mask. My fist was actually a mask — the hardest, the most convincing mask. A child saying "don't touch me" to the outside world, while inside still whispering "please, someone love me."

Inner voice: What if I hadn't hit him that day? Sometimes I think about it. What would have happened if I'd kept staying silent? Maybe I would have been erased entirely. But what if there had been another way to grow strong — like someone telling me "you're not wrong"? Then I would never have needed the fist. That's life's bitter joke: no one said that sentence to me, so I wrote my own sentence with a fist.

Worlds in the Margins of a Notebook

In class I couldn't stay still in my seat. I'd rock back and forth. My body refused to stay fixed in one place — as if there were a current constantly flowing inside me, and the moment I held still, that current would squeeze me. My gaze would drift to the window; a bird outside, a cloud, a branch swaying in the wind — they all called to me louder than the teacher's voice. My notebook filled not with writing, but with drawings. Figures, worlds, complex systems. Boxes connected to one another, arrows, maps, machines I'd invented myself.

Today I know what it was: the ADHD wanted movement, the autism wanted to build systems. When the two worked in the same head, listening to a lesson was impossible, but building a whole universe was possible. Back then I only thought, "There's something wrong with me." I'd look at those drawings in the margins of my notebook and feel ashamed — neat lines in other children's notebooks, fugitive worlds in mine.

Inner voice: If I could look at that notebook now, I'd tell that child this: "You're not broken. You're an engineer, a storyteller, an architect. It's just that no one gave you the right paper." Those fugitive drawings weren't actually an escape — they were an art of survival. My brain was creating, out of a world it couldn't bear, a world it could bear. This wasn't a flaw, it was a gift. But it took thirty years and two diagnoses for me to recognize that gift.

Even from first grade my consciousness was awake. This is hard to explain. But I can put it like this: I didn't just live. I was aware. I watched everything — I watched myself, I watched how others watched me, I watched knowing that I was being watched. My head was like an endless corridor of mirrors. And this awareness hurt. Because not seeing the pain is a mercy; I was deprived of that mercy. I saw everything, and most of what I saw was not beautiful.

Inner voice: "Why me? Why do I feel everything so intensely? The other children laugh it off — why does everything sink into me so deeply?" A glance would touch them and scatter; it would touch me and turn inside me for days. As if everyone else had skin, and I was an open wound. Every touch burned.

This shame, these looks, these whispers — they all stayed in my body. My brain may have forgotten, but my body never did. Sometimes today, as a man of thirty-six, when I hear a sudden loud noise, I still catch the smell of that old classroom. Chalk dust and shame, mingled together, fill my nose all at once. The body knows no calendar; for it, there's no difference between 1998 and today. When that smell comes in, that child is back at the edge of the desk, belly pulled in, trying to be invisible.

But that first punch, that first "no," changed something. It opened a small crack in the darkness. And light began to seep in.
