Chapter 14 – The Bridge: From Your Own Story to Others

On this road there were also friends who made me both laugh and think. With one I’d talk about autism, about ADHD, and we’d say “Now we’re really getting fired up,” and then burst out laughing. Together we came up with ideas: a page, awareness content, questions and answers, soothing things for neurodivergent people — weighted blankets, sensory rooms. Even talking about the most serious subject, we’d end up laughing somewhere. We talked about rejection sensitivity, about meltdowns, about the “fix-it trap in love”; because I was living all of it. One friend laughed at me one day: “You’ve invited people to events, but there’s no event, the page is empty!” He was right — my head full of a thousand ideas, but the details always slipped away somewhere. Today I laugh at it myself. “You just cruise around comfortably in your caravan,” he’d tease. Once I wrote, laughing, “I lost my temper a bit in today’s video.” “Who were you mad at today?” he’d ask, because my anger too came and went like a child’s. One day someone told me, “You made me cry and laugh at once.” That, it turns out, was the best therapy — someone with whom you can cry and laugh at the same time. I had another friend, too, who’d made it his mission to pull me out of that mood: “Just watch how you’ll laugh, I’ll get you out of that mood,” he’d say, and there really were days when I’d say, “You made me laugh first thing in the morning.”

What I had taken for healing, one day, stopped being enough.

I didn't realize it in a single, clear moment. Healing never arrives that way; not with a clap of thunder, but with the slow, patient drip of a faucet. I had come to understand myself — after years of excavation, there was at last a map within me. I had named the weight of the past; once every heavy stone has a name, it presses on you a little less. I was aware of my masks — which one I wore and when, which one had fused to my skin and become part of me. I knew my path, roughly. In a man's life, these are no small things; most people complete their lives without reaching even half of them.

But a restlessness remained inside me. It's hard to describe. Like being full yet still hungry somewhere. Like being tired yet unable to sleep. A quiet, insistent feeling — like someone standing in a corner of the room who won't leave as long as you don't look. It came mostly at night. During the day, amid work and busyness, I could push it down; but once the lights went out, that feeling would quietly sit down on the edge of the bed.

Inner voice: "Only for yourself?"

That question unsettled me. I turned in bed, flipped the pillow, searched for its cool side — but the question stood on both faces of the pillow. Because I knew the answer. I knew it, and that was exactly why I was uneasy. I had never lived only for myself. All my life, even at my lowest, my eye was always on someone else's wound. I had always been the kind of man who stands in the middle of his own fire and smells the neighbor's smoke. I don't say this as a virtue — most of the time it was an escape. But this time the feeling was different. This time it wasn't an escape, but more like a calling.

Inner voice: For a moment I argued with myself. "Enough now. Just once, look only at yourself. From trying to save everyone, you always ended up last." It was true. But another voice inside, calmer, lower: "Maybe this is exactly the way to look after yourself. Maybe you only fill up when you give." The two voices sat down at the table. They argued. And I was between them, not a referee, only a listener.

The Same Faces

After that day the world didn't change — what changed was where I looked. And once I began to look at that place, there was no going back.

Wherever I looked, I began to see familiar eyes. In a café, that child sitting by the window, whose gaze stays fixed on a single point while everyone else is talking. In the supermarket, the little one clinging to its mother's skirt, frightened by the hum of the fluorescent light, covering its ears against the noise of the crowd. On the bus, the young person with a notebook in their lap, drawing tiny patterns in the margins, scolded by the teacher to "pay attention." Quiet children. Young people forever found to be "a touch too much." Too restless, too scattered, too sensitive, too intense — always stopped on the wrong side of that "too."

Sentences that begin with "He has potential, but…" I knew that "but." I knew all the broken hopes, the extinguished lights that lay beneath that "but." Because that "but" was the very ground of my childhood. Everywhere I looked, it was me. The Tuncay of thirty years ago, as if passing from body to body, kept reappearing before me at every corner. But no one noticed it. It was an invisible kinship — a handwriting only someone bearing the same wound could read.

A father, with that helplessness in his eyes, said he didn't understand his child. "I don't know what he wants, nothing I do works." There was no blame in his voice, only exhaustion. A teacher, shoulders slumped, said "I'm doing everything I can" — and truly was, the tools in her hands were simply wrong. A young person, looking away, almost in a whisper, said, "I think there's something wrong with me." There it was. That sentence. When I heard it, something inside me clenched, an old knot retied itself in the pit of my stomach. Because I had said those sentences too. For years. Looking into the mirror, staring at the ceiling at night, in a voice too low for anyone to hear.

Inner voice: I wanted to tell that young person, "No, there's nothing wrong with you." But my tongue froze. Because no one had said it to the seventeen-year-old me either. And for a moment I thought: What if someone had? What if I had heard that sentence, at exactly that age, from the right mouth? Perhaps an entire life would have been built differently. Perhaps I wouldn't have crashed into so many walls, broken so many doors. This "what if" both burned me and, strangely, set me in motion.

It was right there that I understood: there are millions of Tuncays in Turkey. This is no exaggeration, but cold arithmetic. Millions of minds left silent, mislabeled, branded "naughty" or "lazy." Those seated at the back of the class, scolded to "behave," pressured to "try just a little harder." And most of them are waiting to hear that one sentence I myself didn't know for decades:

★ "You are not wrong."

An entire lifetime, sometimes, is spent in the absence of these three words.

It's Not Knowledge, It's Language

At first I thought it was a lack of knowledge. "People don't know," I told myself. "If I explain it, if I teach it, things will be set right." But the more I explained, the more I noticed something else — and this realization went deeper, and was more disquieting.

It wasn't about knowledge. It was about language.

Perhaps people didn't know about neurodivergence — but the real issue was that a language to describe it had never existed at all. If you have no word to name a thing, that thing either ceases to exist for you or is called by the nearest wrong word. And the words at hand were dreadful. They said "broken." "Deficient." "Problematic." "A problem child." "Maladjusted." These words were not neutral labels; they were so many little knives. Tell a child he's "broken" often enough, and that child comes to believe he is not something to be mended, but something to be thrown away.

And I knew this poison very well. It had coursed through my veins for years. I knew, in flesh and bone, how a single word shapes a destiny. They said "lazy," and so I never learned to forgive myself when I grew tired. They said "too sensitive," and so I learned to be ashamed of everything I felt. Words sink into the bone. In that moment a sentence crystallized inside me — the fog lifted, and only one path lay before me:

Inner voice: "I must find another language for this." I must find — I didn't say I can find, I said I must. Because it felt like a necessity, not a choice.

To say something meant to be visible. And being visible had always been risky for me. Whenever I was visible, something had always broken; in my mind, being seen was synonymous with being judged, being loved with being abandoned afterward. Every time I stepped onto a stage, it felt as if, somewhere in the past, it had ended with a stone hurled at me. My body still remembered this: the moment I moved to say something, my throat went dry, my chest tightened, an old alarm began to ring.

But this time, behind the fear, there was something stronger: responsibility. The fear stood in front, yes — but behind it, larger, quieter, more resolute than itself. If I didn't speak, these stories would go on being told with the wrong words. Those children would grow up and go on cutting themselves with the same knives. And

★ wrong words give birth to wrong lives.

I knew this, because part of my own life was the product of wrong words that had come out of other people's mouths.

Inner voice: "What if they laugh? What if they say, 'Who are you to be speaking?' A man twice divorced, who lived in a caravan, whose life is a mess — by what right does he show others the way?" And then a chuckle rose up inside me — that old, dark humor of mine: "For exactly that reason, my friend. These things aren't learned from someone who lived life by the book, but from someone who pulled himself together after falling apart. Not from a clean notebook, but from a page worked over with an eraser."

Opening Up the Story

I began to tell my own story. Unfiltered. Unpolished. Without making myself the hero.

This was harder than I'd thought. Because inside everyone there is an editor who kicks in when they tell their own life — a hand that smooths the corners, cuts out the shameful parts, makes the narrator a little smarter, a little more in the right. I had to hold back that hand. I told it with its pain. Its mess. Its contradictions — admitting, on the one hand, that I'd healed, while on the other confessing I still fell into the same pit. I had understood that as long as I hid my own falls, I could make no room for anyone else's.

And I realized something — simple, but it changed the direction of my life: people don't bond with perfection. They bond with honesty. When I told a polished success story, eyes would drift; but when I said, "that night, shivering in the caravan, I asked myself how much longer," the air in the room would change. A silence would fall — not an empty one, but a full silence. And within that silence, all at once, they saw they were not alone. People would lift their heads and look at one another, as if they'd found, for the first time, someone who spoke the same language.

And I saw it too. Perhaps I saw it most of all. Because when I spoke my own wound aloud, for the first time that wound stopped belonging to me and turned into something shared between us. When you share pain, pain isn't divided — something strange happens, it multiplies yet grows lighter.

★ People respect perfection; but they trust fragility.

At a certain point I understood — and this was a whole new continent on the map spread across my table: what I had lived through was not individual. What I had thought for years was my own flaw was, in fact, the trace of a system. This was a matter of education — a system that tries to force everyone into the same mold, declaring broken whoever doesn't fit. It was a matter of family — homes where even love spoke in the wrong language. It was a matter of culture — a society that hides what is different out of fear of "what will people say." It was about thousands of minds left silent.

My story no longer belonged only to me. This realization didn't frighten me. It made me heavier. Something settled onto my shoulders — but this time, a weight that didn't crush me, that pressed me to the ground, that steadied me. The weight now came from the right place. Rather than being tossed about in the void, to stand beneath a meaningful burden. Only someone who has lived both knows the difference between them.

From Mercedes to the Road

Until I got here, my road was never straight. Looking back, I see not a highway, but a winding footpath, sometimes skirting the edge of a cliff.

In Germany, I had risen to a position as a project manager at Mercedes. A secure seat. A good title. Payday certain, the future calculable, a life that looked "accomplished" from the outside. You don't give up a life like that easily; you cling to it, because security is addictive. I gave it up. Without a safety net, without a timetable, I threw myself onto the road. Most people thought it was madness; on some days I thought so too.

But something strange happened on that road. Out of the project manager came an Artificial Intelligence specialist, someone doing data analysis, a Data Scientist. I learned to speak with numbers, with patterns, with the hidden designs inside the data — and today I use this in my own research on neurodivergence. The mind that had once been thought "scattered" turned out to be a mind that sees patterns; something taken for a defect because it stood in the wrong place, which becomes a talent once set in the right one.

Let me make a distinction clear here, because I loved this work: before I left Germany for Turkey, I worked both as a data analyst and as a data scientist. The part that analyzes the data and draws the conclusions from it is me; the academic paper on it is then usually signed by a professor. But, thank God, I learned the very essence of the work — I became, in a sense, my own professor. I learned to check results, errors, and raw data one by one; and above all, to spot and expose those who pull data to the wrong place, who bend and twist the numbers. The engine of this researching, analytical power is precisely the hyperfocus that my autism and my ADHD give me: once I lock onto a pattern, I can chase the crack others overlook for hours, with stubborn persistence. What was once taken for a "defect" became, at the right table, a talent.

And here one of the greatest false dilemmas of my life was resolved. Science and spirituality. Technique and soul. I had always been told I had to choose one or the other — either cold reason or warm heart. I didn't have to choose between them.

★ The place where the two meet became me.

When the hand that reads the data and the hand that holds the pain are joined in the same person, something else comes into being.

When I set out on the road, the first feeling wasn't freedom. I'd like to say it was — window open, wind in my hair, horizon ahead, that kind of scene. But the truth wasn't like that. The first feeling was unease. An unease close to nausea; a cold hand on the back of my neck saying, "there's no going back now." An unease that had me asking, at night, "what have I done," and in the mornings forcing myself to be persuaded all over again.

But as the wheels turned, something loosened. The fixed points in my mind — "it should be like this," "it should look like that," "what will everyone say" — were pried loose as the road shook. Plans loosened. Control eased. And strangely, my body relaxed; that invisible armor I'd carried on my shoulders for years shed itself, little by little, over the miles. Because

★ the road didn't ask me to be perfect. It only asked me to move.

For the first time in my life, something demanded of me no more than what I was.

Inner voice: The day I gave up that seat, the accountant inside me was in a panic: "Insurance? Pension? What if you get sick? What if the money runs out?" He was right; the questions were valid. But someone else inside me, silenced for years, a child with shining eyes, quietly asked: "And what if you never even try? What happens then?" That question wrecked the whole of the accountant's reckoning. Because some accounts, when left undone, cost far more.

On the road, not everyone tells their story. But everyone has one. This is worth remembering: silence is not the absence of a story, but most often its heaviest form. Some spoke. Some stayed silent. But most met a gaze in which they felt understood for the first time — that moment when the eyes widen for an instant, then soften. Like someone who has searched for something for years finding it where they least expected.

And most of the time, I didn't speak. I listened. This was a revolution for me. Because all my life I was the one waiting to be listened to; always talking, always trying to explain, always straining to be understood. To move to the other side of the table, to fall silent, to give all my attention to the person across from me — this was, in truth, a quiet gift I gave to myself.

★ Listening was the greatest contact no one had given me for years.

Because they didn't give it, I learned to give it.

Where the Road Changed

And one day — I don't quite remember which day, because it didn't happen in a single day — I accepted it.

I am not merely a man trying to heal. I am a bridge.

I stand between two worlds. Between the old language and the new — between the mouth that says "broken" and the mouth that says "different." Between the misunderstood and the finally understood. Between the child inside the fire and the man who managed to get out. To be a bridge is to be trampled a little; you are walked over, footsteps echo on your planks, you are tugged at from both ends. But without a bridge, no one can cross to the other side.

I didn't choose this role. That old, cruel rule of life was at work again: everything I never asked for—the times I broke, the labels placed on me, the loneliness—had shaped me into someone capable of becoming this bridge. A bridge must know both banks. I had lived both banks.

★ I didn't choose this role. But I no longer refuse it.

Inner voice: "What I lived through was not in vain — because it brought me here." For years I tried to say this sentence, but it always rang false in my mouth, like a consolation I used to soothe myself. Now, for the first time, it has a real taste. Because it is no longer a consolation — it's an observation. Looking back, I see that not a single stone moved out of place; every one was set there for exactly this, to build this bridge.

Therapist's Note – Understanding Beyond Survival (Generativity)

What is happening here: In this chapter a very distinct psychological threshold is crossed: the passage from "I want to heal" to "I want to pass something on." The psychologist Erik Erikson, in his theory of lifelong development, called this generativity — the need to go beyond oneself and to create, leave behind, and nurture something for the next generation. Erikson considered this the central task of adulthood; its opposite pole is "stagnation," that is, a person closing in only on themselves. In people who have lived through trauma and a late diagnosis, generativity is often the most powerful form of healing: your own pain turns into a tool for others; the wound, into a door. The chapter's central thesis is also psychologically very apt — the issue is not missing knowledge, but missing language. Words like "broken" or "deficient" are not neutral descriptions; they shape how a person lives themselves. Language creates reality.

Where it comes from — body and evolution

The human drive to "turn one's own pain into benefit for another" is not a romantic ornament; it is something etched into our nervous system. The brain's mirror neuron systems (discovered by Rizzolatti and colleagues in the 1990s) activate similar networks within us when we see another's pain or intention — this is the neurological substrate of empathy. "Recognizing" that expression in the eyes of someone who carries the same wound is not an abstract intuition, but data the body reads directly. Evolutionarily, too, human beings achieved survival not through individual heroics but through bonding and transmission; the groups that carried knowledge, protection, and meaning from generation to generation were the ones that endured. The search for meaning is itself a biological need: Viktor Frankl, in logotherapy, argued that a person's fundamental motivation is not pleasure or power but meaning (Man's Search for Meaning). When a meaning — especially the meaning of "benefit to another" — can be attached to pain, that same pain becomes bearable. To this is added post-traumatic growth (Tedeschi & Calhoun, 1996): some people, after shattering experiences, not only return to their former selves but gain a new meaning and depth. "Bridge" is precisely the name for this.

If left unaddressed — possible bad outcomes

If the drive to reach out to others from one's own pain is not worked through on healthy ground, it can veer in two directions. The first is the wounded helper trap: a person, by constantly tending to others, flees from their own unfinished interior ("I look after everyone, just not myself"). Helping becomes a new and more noble-seeming mask; yet beneath it the same flight continues. The second is burnout and compassion fatigue: the one who gives without limit begins to give from emptiness, until they run dry inside. The body, too, pays its price for this — chronic fatigue, touchiness, resentment, and in the end bitterness and withdrawal. The most ironic ending is this: the person who burns their own wick to the end trying to be a light to others can, in the end, be an example to no one. The text's inner voice already senses this; to name the danger is the first step in guarding against it.

Paths of healing — several doors

There are several doors to staying healthy at this threshold:

• Order: first yourself, then others. That cliché on the airplane is true — you put the mask on yourself first. This is not selfishness, but the only condition of sustainability. The one who is full gives; the one who is empty leaks.
• Internal Family Systems (IFS – Richard Schwartz): The voice that says "I must save everyone" is often a protective "part"; it keeps you constantly useful so as never to feel an old wound again. Rather than declaring that part the enemy, understand its intention and lighten its burden.
• Compassion-Focused Therapy (Paul Gilbert) and self-compassion (Kristin Neff): Learning to set aside a drop of the compassion you show others for yourself. In Neff's words, treating yourself as you would treat a friend.
• Boundary work: Learning to say "no," to place distance between a demand and your own need; this is the antidote to burnout.
• Narrative therapy (White & Epston) and expressive writing (Pennebaker, 1997): Rewriting your own story in the language not of "a broken person" but of "someone who became a bridge." This book is an application of that therapy.
• Meaning-based approaches (logotherapy – Frankl): If the "why" is strong enough, a person can endure almost any "how."

Tuncay's bridge

When I first read the word "generativity," I smiled — because the thing I'd been doing for years without knowing its name had a counterpart in Erikson. Seeing myself in the eyes of those children was, I later learned, the work of mirror neurons; so that feeling of "familiar kinship" was not a fantasy but real data my body was reading. When I read Frankl, I understood why that question in the caravan, "how much longer?", one day turned into the question "for what?": my pain had not lessened, but once tied to a meaning, it had become bearable. And if I'm to be honest — the most real struggle in this chapter is my struggle with the "wounded helper" trap. All my life I was the man who tried to save everyone and put himself last; being a bridge could also be a noble cover cut to fit that old habit. I know this, which is why I had to learn to set boundaries, to say "no," to fill my own cup first — I'm still learning, and I don't manage it every day. What I overcame was the belief that "what I lived through was in vain." What I haven't overcome, what I'm still working on, is my tendency to turn giving into an escape. Being a bridge is beautiful; but a bridge needs maintenance too, or it collapses in the middle.

Where I Stand Today

I am taking this step right now — not "finished," but the very life I am living. Let me say it honestly: being a bridge is not a destination, but a stance I choose anew every day. I traded the secure seat for an insecure meaning, and I'm not sorry; sitting in that seat I wasn't alive, only solid. I know the trap of helping, and I'm careful — I no longer give from emptiness, or I pause twice before giving. The biggest thing I overcame is the fear of being visible: today I can speak openly about things I was ashamed of for decades, and the sky doesn't fall on my head. What I still haven't fully resolved is my tendency to rush to another before filling my own cup; I confess it, because hiding it too would be a mask. And most important of all: I now fully believe the sentence "what I lived through was not in vain." My pain has turned into a language for others. This is not just talk. This mission was the reason I had to walk this whole road.

"I am because we are."

— Ubuntu, African wisdom

As long as I breathe, I become. I am finished only when I no longer do.
