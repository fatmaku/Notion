If I say my father, in truth, carries no blame — because it’s genetic — you’ll be angry with me. But he too learned emotions by rote; he lived with an empathy deficit you could almost call narcissistic. If I really look, it’s as if he never wanted us. He married at sixteen; responsibility felt like a burden to him, and he did everything to escape it. He learned everything by comparison, because he too had never been seen. He tried hard to cover his mistakes, but he often gave in to his impulses; his outbursts of anger also showed the hatred he felt for himself and for his life. He took on responsibility early, was crushed under his own father’s debts; the signatures he was forced into, the burdens loaded onto him, he wanted one day to pass on to me as well.

My mother, on the other hand, was alone. Her parents were dead, she spoke no German, she was at my father’s mercy and she was afraid. For our sake, she gave in to her own fears. She didn’t give up — but maybe giving up would have been her salvation, and ours.

Today I’m not angry at either of them. Because understanding is stronger than forgiving without understanding. When you understand, forgiveness comes on its own; only one choice remains: I allow that person to be in my life, or I don’t. That’s all.

 

My father and my mother, in their youth. From them I learned both fear and love.
