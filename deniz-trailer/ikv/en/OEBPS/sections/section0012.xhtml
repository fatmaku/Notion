Therapist's Note – Chronic Bullying and Body Shame

What is happening here: Here, three things are colliding at once, and each one alone is heavy enough. One: an attachment wound born of constant moving, a wound of never being able to put down roots — every friendship severed before it can even sprout. Two: an AuDHD brain that reads social signals differently, and therefore always seems "half a beat off." Three: a child of immigrant background, already standing between two worlds. When these three overlap, what emerges is a chronic sense of "I don't belong here." Shame here is not "thought about," it is recorded in the body — stomach a stone, cheeks aflame, ears ringing, throat dry. This is a classic freeze or fight response coming not from the thinking part of the brain, but from its oldest part. And the masking begins: looking strong on the outside, breaking on the inside. The fist and the silence are, in fact, two faces of the same mask.

Where it comes from — body and evolution

That a child should be shaken so deeply in the face of "not being accepted" is not a weakness but an evolutionary inheritance. The human infant is one of nature's most helpless offspring; it cannot survive on its own. That is why our brain reads exclusion just like a physical danger. Naomi Eisenberger's brain-imaging studies showed that social rejection activates the brain regions that process bodily pain (the anterior cingulate cortex) (Eisenberger, 2003) — meaning "my heart is broken" is not just a metaphor, but a state the brain actually records as pain. For our ancestors, being cast out of the group meant death; that's why the alarm of "everyone is looking at me, I'm wrong" is a survival circuit.

That the body holds onto this memory is no coincidence either. The very title of Bessel van der Kolk's book says it: The Body Keeps the Score (van der Kolk, 2014). When a traumatic moment is lived, the amygdala — in van der Kolk's words, "the brain's smoke detector" — records the sensory traces of that moment (smell, sound, touch) with a danger label. Years later, when a similar smell or a sudden loud noise arrives, the body mistakes the time and teleports you back into that classroom; this is called a trauma trigger. What you call "catching the smell of chalk at a loud noise" is, scientifically, exactly this. Stephen Porges's Polyvagal Theory completes the picture: our nervous system, without our being aware of it, scans the environment at every moment for "am I safe?" — he calls this "neuroception" (Porges, 2011). In a classroom where you are always on guard, this scanning turns into chronic alarm.

And then there is attachment. John Bowlby and Mary Ainsworth's attachment theory shows that a child needs a stable, predictable bond in order to develop securely. In a child uprooted every year and pressed into new soil, the belief "there's no point in forming a bond, it'll break anyway" can take hold; this turns into a pattern that repeats later in adult relationships. Your sentence, "I grew my roots inward," is in fact the extremely clever but lonely response a child gives to instability.

If left unaddressed — possible bad outcomes

Shame recorded in the body, if left unaddressed, hardens into the life-feeling of "Being different = Being wrong." A possible course: lasting low self-worth, pervasive social anxiety, a chronic fear of "giving oneself away," and an unspoken reservoir of anger — a reservoir that later either spills outward (outbursts, conflicts, a tendency toward violence) or inward (complete withdrawal, depression, identity collapse). This is very common in neurodivergent adults diagnosed late in life. The voice of "I'm wrong" internalized in childhood steps onto the stage again and again in adulthood — in relationships, at work, in parenting. The first punch feels like salvation — but let's be honest, it's only the second-best solution: it stops the threat, but it doesn't dress the wound. The best comes later.

Paths of healing — several doors

There is no single way to heal this wound; there are several doors, and all of them are built on "bringing shame out of silence and into the light":

• Somatic approaches (Peter Levine – Somatic Experiencing): Since the body holds the trauma, healing too passes through the body. Grounding exercises — feet planted firmly on the ground, breath felt, the vagus nerve stimulated by long exhalations — pull the nervous system out of alarm and into "safe" mode (consistent with Polyvagal Theory).
• EMDR: A method with strong evidence for trauma, which allows those old, frozen memories (the desk, the look, the first punch) to be reprocessed in the brain and filed away on the "past" shelf.
• Compassion-Focused Therapy (Paul Gilbert) and self-compassion (Kristin Neff): Learning to respond to that bully working for free in your head not as an enemy, but as if speaking to a frightened child. Turning the sentence "I'm wrong" into "I was a child doing the best he could in a hard situation."
• Schema therapy / CBT: Re-examining, with today's adult mind, the "defectiveness/shame schema" that took hold in childhood; updating that program left over from 1998.
• Narrative therapy and expressive writing (Pennebaker): Shame cannot withstand a story that is told. Writing down what you've lived through — just like this book — helps the brain reframe that memory; Pennebaker's research shows that writing improves both mood and immune function (Pennebaker, 1997).
• Reframing: Shame is not my flaw, it is other people's mirror — they were reflecting onto me their fear of the unfamiliar. And the drawings in the margins of the notebook, the thing I once thought was an escape, are now a recognized strength: someone who builds whole worlds and systems is not "broken," but creative.

Tuncay's bridge

For years I thought the child in that classroom was the one who was wrong. When I read Eisenberger's finding that "social rejection is processed in the same place as physical pain," I understood why that stone in my stomach was as heavy as a real stone — so I wasn't exaggerating after all, my brain really had recorded it as pain. Van der Kolk's sentence "the body keeps the score" explained why I still catch the smell of chalk together with a sudden noise; that smell isn't an illusion, it's a recording. When I read Bowlby, I found the answer to the question "why did I never put down roots": a child whose roots are torn out learns to give up rooting, because that's the path that hurts least. I lived through all of this for years without knowing the names; then, as I read, I saw that each one had a name, an explanation, and I felt a strange relief. I now remember that first punch not with shame, but with compassion: it was the only tool that child had in his hand. Today I have words in my hand; that day he had a fist. Both were saying the same thing: "I am here, and I refuse to disappear."

Where I Stand Today

I've largely managed this — but it took time, and it's still not entirely finished. I no longer need a fist to say "no"; I have my words, and most of the time they're enough. When the old shame rises, I now recognize it for what it is: a program left over from 1998, one that should long since have been updated; not a threat, but a visitor. What remains is the body's memory — the smell of that classroom at loud noises. Perhaps I'll never fully get over it, and I've come to accept that. But it no longer runs me. Sometimes it just knocks at the door; and I can say "I see you've come" and close the door again. And most importantly: that child who scribbled worlds in the margins of his notebook became a man who builds worlds — only now those worlds help others. I didn't erase the ashamed child; I took him along with me.

"Shame cannot survive a story told out loud in a safe place."

— inspired by Brené Brown

Finished is not for me; I become, every day anew, to the very end.
