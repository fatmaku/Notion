Therapist's Note – Hyper-Attachment and Separation Trauma

What is happening here: Here, something beautiful and something fragile are happening at the same time. The beautiful part: a man who has never experienced safe closeness is living it for the first time with his children. Touch without alarm. Unconditional love. That state described on the night walks — "I relaxed along with them" — is, neurobiologically, a real thing: a parent and a baby in calm contact regulate one another; this is called co-regulation. And the dangerous part is right here too: precisely because his own childhood was so empty, the bond formed with his children grows excessively strong. A projective repair is at work: "By giving them what was missing for me, I am healing my own wound." The deep empathy and sensory bonding capacity of AuDHD intensify this even further. The attachment grows very strong — and this makes the separation far more devastating. There is also a very healthy, conscious effort here to break the cycle: "I will not do to them what was done to me." Recognizing one's own neurodifference in one's children, and framing it not as a flaw but as a gift, is precisely the right move to stop the transmission of intergenerational trauma.

Where it comes from — body and evolution

What happens in the body when you take a baby into your arms is a program millions of years old. According to John Bowlby's Attachment Theory, the infant's clinging to its caregiver and the caregiver's bonding to the infant is a biological system that ensures the survival of the species (Bowlby, Attachment, 1969); the ancestor who did not keep a defenseless young one close could not carry on their line. Mary Ainsworth's work showed that this bond takes secure, anxious, or avoidant forms (Ainsworth, 1978). A child's first word being "Daddy" and reaching into the arms with surrender is a classic picture of secure attachment — and for a parent who often lived through their own insecure childhood, this is, in the fullest sense of the word, restorative.

That "my hands stopped trembling" is no coincidence: calm, loving contact releases oxytocin; this is the hormone of bonding and of calming. At the same time, in Stephen Porges's Polyvagal Theory, the "vagal brake" engages — the body's shift from threat mode into "safe social bonding" mode (Porges, 2011). As Bessel van der Kolk says, the body keeps the score; but the body keeps the score of healing contact too (The Body Keeps the Score, 2014). The sentence "for the first time, closeness was safe" is the account of a nervous system being retrained. The capacity to recognize oneself in one's child has to do with mirror neurons and high empathy: the brain echoes another's emotion within; this intense echo is often stronger in neurodivergent individuals. And, after the separation, smelling their pillows and sinking into rumination for hours is the grieving state of the brain's default mode network.

If left unaddressed — possible bad outcomes

If the bond becomes the sole source of a person's self-worth, the algorithm "Love = Completeness" is born. If separation shatters that algorithm, a deep loss of identity follows: severe depressive episodes, constant self-blame ("I couldn't be a good enough father"), withdrawal from life, extreme anxiety in new relationships or, conversely, complete avoidance of closeness. To lose the child then becomes not just the loss of the child, but the loss of oneself. Unprocessed grief of separation can also settle into the body — sleep disturbances, chronic tension — and most insidiously of all, with the decision "I will never love this much again," it can shut a person down emotionally; and this, in turn, produces within oneself a likeness of the very emptiness one was trying to repair.

Paths of healing — several doors

The key sentence: Hold the bond, but do not grip it tight. Love does not vanish with distance. There are a few doors that work:

• Self-compassion (Kristin Neff) and Compassion Focused Therapy (Paul Gilbert): answering the voice that says "I couldn't be good enough" as you would speak to a friend: "You did what you could; what was missing was not love."
• Internal Family Systems (IFS – Richard Schwartz): recognizing and soothing, separately, that fearful part asking "Will they hate me?" and the protective part saying "I will break the cycle"; both are different voices of the same love.
• Somatic approaches (Peter Levine – Somatic Experiencing): releasing the part of separation grief that accumulates in the body — the knot in the throat, the tearing in the chest — not with logic but with slow breath and bodily awareness (a long exhale stimulates the vagus).
• Expressive writing for grief (James Pennebaker): putting the nights of farewell, the longing, into writing; this book itself is a product of that writing.
• Giving the bond a new form: a message, a photo, a heartfelt "I love you." The bond shifting from "possession" to "guidance" — the practice of Kahlil Gibran's words, "they belong not to you." The old sentence "If I lose them I am finished" slowly turns into "I love them and this love is still within me."
• Maskless parenting: a child's nervous system internalizes not acted-out peace, but real peace. That is why the healthiest healing is the parent's own genuine healing.

Tuncay's bridge

I learned the names of these concepts only later — while living them, I only felt them. On the night walks, when I noticed that as I soothed my child I too was being soothed, I didn't know it had a name; later I read of something called "co-regulation" and said, "so that's what it was." That my hands stopped trembling in the delivery room had seemed like a miracle to me — then I learned of oxytocin and the vagal brake, the miracle turned into science, but it lost nothing of its beauty. The sentence "the one thing you cannot give your child is the thing you do not have within yourself" — I learned it not from books, but from that night of handing them over, from that question at the threshold, "Daddy, are you leaving?" And let me be honest: I cannot say I have fully overcome the wound of separation. Every night of handing them back still gently reopens that first night. But there is one thing I overcame — I learned not to turn that wound into self-hatred. I took the science and spread it over my own life like a map; that map turned the sentence "I was a bad father" into the sentence "I was a wounded man and I did the best I could." And this note in this book, too, is an attempt to hold that map out to you.

Where I Stand Today

Becoming a father became both my wound and my healing. I kept the healing: I now know I am able to give safe closeness — the promise in Chapter 4 came true, not "click" but "Daddy." The wound of separation I still carry. But I learned not to turn it into self-hatred. The love I feel for my daughters is not a possession that can be lost. No matter how great the distance grows, it is a piece that remains within me. And most important of all: I am determined to show them my real face, to genuinely heal rather than act out happiness. Because the only way they don't learn the mask is for me to truly be myself. Breaking the cycle is not a sentence — it is a decision I make anew every single day.

"Your children are not your children. They come through you but not from you, and though they are with you, yet they belong not to you."

— Kahlil Gibran, "The Prophet"

I live in becoming, not in being finished. And that becoming stays with me to my last breath.
