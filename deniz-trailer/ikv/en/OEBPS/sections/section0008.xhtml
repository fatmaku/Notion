Therapist's Note – Childhood Emotional Neglect

What is happening here: This is a textbook example of something rarely noticed, because it is so invisible: emotional neglect. Careful — this does not mean "bad parent." It means this: bodily needs (food, warmth, a roof) are met, but the basic emotional need — "I see you, I ask after you, your inner world matters to me" — is left wanting (Jonice Webb, Running on Empty, 2012). A child who is well cared for but not emotionally seen learns this insidious sentence: "I'm here, my stomach is full, I'm warm — I have no right to complain. So the problem must be me." That contradiction between the warm house and the chill inside is exactly this: the plate gets filled, but the "I am being heard" bowl stays forever empty.

Where it comes from — body and evolution

The human infant is one of nature's most helpless young; for years it cannot survive on its own. That is why evolution has wired us with a survival cable: the attachment system. John Bowlby and Mary Ainsworth showed that a child clings to the caregiver not only for food but as a "secure base" — when frightened, it seeks a face to turn back to for shelter, a face to look to in order to be seen (Bowlby, Attachment, 1969; Ainsworth's "strange situation" experiment, 1978). When the baby cries, the softening of the caregiver's face, the voice turning toward it — this is called emotional "attunement." When this attunement is missing, the child survives but learns something: "There is no answer to the world inside me." Stephen Porges's Polyvagal Theory gives this a biological foundation: our nervous system constantly reads, from another person's face, tone of voice, and gaze, the signal "am I safe and am I seen?" — he calls this "neuroception" (Porges, 2011). If the mother's voice is constantly flowing toward someone else, the child's nervous system can never quite receive that soothing signal; it stays full but on alert.

And when an AuDHD brain is layered on top of this, the matter compounds. The autistic nervous system experiences sensory input more intensely (a television that is always on becomes a real physical burden), while the ADHD side hungers for dopamine — that is, for attention, novelty, reward. Decoding social signals (how to enter a conversation, the timing of a joke) is not an automatic, built-in program; it is a puzzle that must be solved by hand; "laughing a second too late" is the classic sign of this. The child's response is clever, even ingenious: it seeks warmth in the concrete (feet on the radiator — the body trying to compensate through skin for what the soul cannot find), and flees into a world built in the mind — its name: maladaptive daydreaming. Imagination becomes the safest place, because it is the only place where control belongs entirely to the child. His attachment to Goku and Simba is no coincidence either: the mirror neuron system in the brain makes us live the emotion of a character we watch as if it were our own; while no one in real life attuned to him, a screen character taught him empathy.

If left unaddressed — possible bad outcomes

The child internalizes the "I'm not wanted / I'm not seen" algorithm and carries it into adulthood unchanged. Possible course: difficulty recognizing and naming one's own emotions (alexithymia — "I'm feeling something, but I don't know its name"); a chronic loneliness that chafes even in full rooms, even among those who love you; in relationships, either over-giving or withdrawing on the expectation that "I won't be seen anyway"; confusing love with food, success, or approval; recurring depressive episodes and low self-worth. The most insidious of all: the person cannot find the cause of their unhappiness, because "nothing bad even happened" — the pain of neglect is the pain not of something that happened but of something that didn't happen, and it is very hard to put your finger on something that isn't there.

Paths of healing — several doors

The direction is clear: the inner world must now come out, and this time someone must attune to that child — and if need be, that "someone" must be the grown-up self.

• Expressive writing (Pennebaker): Pouring the inner world onto paper. James Pennebaker's research shows that writing about emotions several days in a row improves mood and even immunity (1997). Answering the question "what really happened inside me today?" in writing is asking yourself the question no one asked. This very book is proof of that.
• Internal Family Systems (IFS – Richard Schwartz): Approaching that unseen child within you as an "exile," and having the grown, calm "Self" turn toward it and attune. That is, stopping waiting for your mother and instead sitting down at that table yourself and asking the child, "How was your day?"
• Compassion Focused Therapy (Paul Gilbert) and Self-compassion (Kristin Neff): Responding gently to the "the problem is me" voice, as if speaking to a child you love. Placing a hand on the heart (the "radiator mode" in the afterthought) activates precisely the soothing system Gilbert worked on.
• Somatic approaches (Peter Levine – Somatic Experiencing) and body awareness: That "chill inside" and "knot in the belly" live in the body and cannot be undone with words. A slow, long exhale stimulates the vagus nerve and pulls the system into "safe" mode (Polyvagal Theory).
• Reparenting and forming a bond: Experiencing attunement, late though it may be, within a safe relationship — therapist, friend, partner, or a community. The brain is plastic; the experience of "being seen" can lay down a new cable even in adulthood.
• Turning imagination into a resource: That inner world that was once an escape — that safe room where control belonged to the child — turns in adulthood into story, into painting, into a book. The place you fled to becomes the place you create.

Tuncay's bridge

For years I had labeled those scenes "a lovely childhood I have no right to complain about" and shelved them. Because truly nothing bad "had happened" — the house was warm, the table was full. The day I read Webb's concept of "emotional neglect," something inside me clicked into place: so pain doesn't necessarily have to come from a slap; sometimes pain comes from a question that never came, from a gaze that never turned back. That was when I understood why I stayed hungry when I rose from that table — it wasn't the stomach being fed, it was the hunger to be seen. When I read Porges, I saw why my nervous system could never quite say "safe" while my mother's voice always flowed toward someone else; this relieved me, because I wasn't an "ungrateful child," just a system that hadn't received the signal. And the best part: I stopped being angry at those screens, at Goku, at Simba. They were my first mirror neuron exercises; while no one in real life attuned to me, they were the ones who taught me to feel. Today I am the one who attunes to that unseen child — I am the one who sits at the table, I am the one who asks, "how was your day?" It would be a lie to say I have completely "filled" that emptiness I couldn't fill for years; but now I can look at it not with panic, but with compassion. And that too is a kind of healing.

Where I Stand Today

I still recognize that emptiness from back then. But I have learned to answer it now not with panic, but with compassion. When the old "no one sees me" feeling comes, I place my hand on my heart and — yes, a little cliché — I say: "Radiator mode is on, but this time I warm myself." And so today I am the one who asks after the child no one asked after. I have forgiven my mother too; because now I see that she too, somewhere, was trying to tell her own pain to someone at the end of a phone, with the same hunger to be seen. No one was bad; it was just that no one had been able to ask after anyone. This is what I have truly left behind: in order to exist, I no longer wait for someone else to see me. And to my four daughters — I am grateful that I can give them that sentence I never knew by heart, the one I learned only later: sitting at the table and asking, "how was your day?"

"Children are educated by what the grown-up is and not by his talk."

I am not yet complete. I complete myself daily — until my last breath.
