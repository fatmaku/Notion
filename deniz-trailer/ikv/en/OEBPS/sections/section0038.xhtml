Chapter 13 – Acceptance and the New Tuncay

Then, in the least expected moment, my heart rang again. I found her as someone who heals; and me, someone trying to heal. Two wounded healers found each other. Even our first messages did me good: I’d say “Don’t forget to eat, don’t wear yourself out, my lovely blue-eyed one”; she’d say “You’re so sweet, but.” “Sometimes there’ll be four or five hours when you can’t reach me,” she said, because we both had work, a worry, a world. Like every relationship, this one has its tides too, small fights, tangled matters that need explaining. But this time it’s different — now I know myself. When I say “I’m going to lose it,” I know I actually mean “I’m afraid the bond will break.” One night she wrote “Goodnight, my love.” A simple sentence. But for someone like me, who felt invisible for years, “goodnight” means “you are seen.” I always wanted to be seen in order to exist; and this was exactly that.

My blood ties are quiet too. My relationship with my siblings is built more on presence than on words. One says “You made me laugh first thing in the morning,” another sends a prayer: “My Lord, give me patience and protect me, ease my heart.” Not big sentences, but big love. When my mother didn’t come home one day, didn’t answer the phone, I wrote to my brother in a panic: “Mum hasn’t come home, do you know anything, why isn’t she picking up?” In that moment I understood that no matter how far away I am, that house, those people, are still my anchor. We grew up in the same house, but everyone saw the world from a different window; even so, we’re tied to the same root. In a family it’s like that — different branches of the same river.

One day I noticed it. It wasn't really a single moment; there was never one of those thunderclap moments, with the lights flashing on. It happened quietly, almost under the radar. It was morning, I think. I was standing in the kitchen, waiting for the water to heat — that ordinary, indescribable kind of waiting. Outside, a neighbor slammed a door, a plate clattered somewhere, someone's car backed out of the street before it had warmed up. Once, each of these sounds would have set off a tiny alarm inside me: my shoulder would tense, my teeth would clench, and some part of me would leap up demanding "who, what, why." But that morning… nothing leapt up. The sounds came, passed through me, and went. I stayed where I was.

And it was in that emptiness, in that alarm that didn't ring, that I understood: I am no longer the old me. The same street, the same sounds, the same world — the same events, the same people. But I respond differently. Or rather, most of the time I don't even respond; I simply notice. I listened to the water bubbling, picked up the cup, and my palm filled with warmth. And I felt a strange gratitude — to whom, for what, I don't know. Perhaps to that old man who, for all those years, lived so on edge that he couldn't even notice this warmth.

Inner voice: "What changed?" — I asked myself this question, and the answer came before I could wait for it: I changed. The world stayed the same; I shifted to a different frequency. "But when?" asked the accountant inside me. Give me a date, give me a moment, give me a reason. But I couldn't. Because transformation, it turns out, isn't something that comes with an invoice. One day you look, and the thing that used to flatten you is now just a passing spell of weather.

Anger

Let me describe anger, because we shared the same house for many long years. Once, anger would stay inside me for hours, sometimes days. I know centimeter by centimeter where it settles in my body: first a tightening in the middle of my chest — as if someone had placed a fist there and was slowly pressing down. Then the stomach; it clenches, turns to stone, and no food will go in. My jaw locks, my shoulders climb toward my ears. And sleep… there is no sleep. On my back in bed, eyes on the ceiling, the same scene playing over and over in my head. I rehearse the same sentence a hundred times: "If I'd said this, if he'd done that." I set up a courtroom in my mind, where I am the prosecutor, the defendant, and the judge all at once — and the trial never ends.

And now? Anger is brief. It comes — yes, it still comes, because I didn't tear it out and throw it away, nor did I want to. It rises for a few minutes. A shadow of that old tightening appears in my chest, and I say to it, "Hello, so you're still here." And then it passes. Like a cloud. The sky was always there; I just used to mistake the cloud for the sky.

Inner voice: "It's not worth it." — This sentence used to be a suppression, a lie I spoke through clenched teeth; in truth it was worth it, I was burning inside. Now it really isn't worth it. Because afterward I realized this: This anger doesn't belong to me. It's only the echo of old wounds. When a room is empty and you shout, the sound comes back, doesn't it — well, this anger is that kind of echo. The one who shouted left long ago; but the walls still hold the sound.

Once I watched myself, almost from the outside. Someone had wronged me; with the old reflex my chest swelled, and I thought, "Okay, here we go." But this time there was a gap — a thin gap that hadn't been there before. Between the trigger and my reaction, a breath's worth of space. And in that space, a choice appeared. There used to be no such thing as choice; there was a trigger, an explosion, regret. Now a door had slipped in between.

★ Freedom lives in that one-second gap between trigger and reaction.

Empathy

Empathy is a strange story for me. Most people assume empathy is innate, easy, flowing. For me it wasn't. Once, understanding people was genuinely hard for me. I couldn't figure out why someone behaved a certain way — and when I couldn't, I'd get angry. "Why is he doing this? Why is he saying something so absurd? Why can't he see something this obvious?" There was a logic machine in my head; I'd feed people into that machine, the machine couldn't process them, and I'd output anger as an error code.

Now it's the opposite. Most of the time I understand far too much — sometimes so much that I can't protect myself. I hear the smallest crack in a person's tone of voice. When someone says "How are you, I'm fine," I catch that half-step dip in their "I'm fine." I see a muscle in their face tense and release for an instant, their eyes flicker away for a second. Once these were noise, meaningless signals; now they've become sentences.

And it passes through me: "Why is this person behaving this way?" The answer no longer comes as judgment, the way it used to. Right after, it comes in a gentle voice: "Because they too are wounded." If the person facing me is aggressive, it's because they're afraid. If they're cold, it's because they were once badly burned. If they're silent, it's because they learned their words held no value. Everyone has, somewhere unseen inside them, a child who never healed.

Inner voice: "Everyone has their own story. Just like me." — And sometimes, to be honest, I add this too: "Come on, Tuncay, don't go understanding everyone this much. Sometimes people are just being unkind, and you don't have to look for a wound underneath everything." I laugh at this. Because even if this inner voice is right, I'm no longer that man. Understanding isn't a weakness for me; it's a muscle I earned the long way.

The Masks Are Dissolving

For years I wore masks. If I tried to count them, they'd fill a chest. The worker mask — the man who never tires, never complains, always says "I'll handle it." The agreeable mask — the one who becomes whoever everyone wants, who doesn't know how to say no, who tries to blend into the crowd and vanish. The strong mask — the one who never breaks, never cries, says "I'm fine," while inside there's only wreckage. These masks stayed on my face so long that at some point even I forgot which was the mask and which was the face.

The awakening didn't tear them off my face in one stroke. If it had, maybe I couldn't have borne it; a face left bare can't step out into the open air before it's ready. It dissolved them quietly. Like taking the end of a knot and slowly loosening it. Today, when I smile, I know why I'm smiling — politeness, real joy, or a reflex to soothe a threat. When I fall silent, I can tell whether I'm silent out of fear or out of choice. This distinction seems small, but it changes an entire life.

This awareness leaves a person both naked and free. Naked, because there's no longer any cloth to hide behind; people see you, the real you — which can sometimes be frightening. Free, because each of those masks you carried had a weight, and for years you didn't even realize you were carrying that weight on your shoulders. I can't tell you how my back straightens once I set it down.

Inner voice: "For the first time, I'm not trying to 'fix' myself." — All my life I was a repair project in my own eyes. A fault list: fix this, suppress that, be ashamed of the other. Now there's no list. I don't fix myself — I read myself. Page by page, without judgment, like reading a book with curiosity, asking "why was this part written this way."

Peace with the Inner Polyphony

The voices inside me didn't fall silent. Let me say this, because those who think "I healed" and expect the voices to go quiet will be disappointed. The voices are still there: the analytical one, endlessly calculating; the creative one, likening everything to something else; the angry one, the old watchman; the frightened one, the smallest child. They're all there. But they no longer quarrel.

My head used to be a marketplace — everyone shouting, no one listening. The analytical one would belittle the creative one as an "idle dreamer." The angry one would shame the frightened child. Now something strange has happened: they've all sat down at the same table. The angry one began to protect the others — turns out that was always its whole concern, it just didn't know the language. The analytical one learned to take the creative one's ideas seriously. This isn't a balance; balance is fragile, it tips over in the slightest wind. This is an agreement.

When a thought comes, I no longer suppress it. I watch it. I sit on the shore like a fisherman, a thought comes, and I look at it: "Who are you? Which one are you from?" Which one truly belongs to me, which is a recording left over from the past, which speaks out of fear — I can tell them apart. When a thought like "Nobody loves me" comes, I used to take it for the truth; now I say, "Ah, that's the eight-year-old child's sentence, I've heard this one before," I pat its head, and I move on.

Inner voice: "This was a mental revolution." — And the quietest of revolutions. There were no banners, no slogans. Just one day, I took my place at the head of the table. That crowd was still talking, but now it was I who chaired the meeting. "All right," I said, "you can all speak. But one at a time. And I make the decision."

Redefining Time

Time was never a straight line for me. People describe time like a train — from station to station, regular, predictable. My time was always otherwise. Sometimes it flowed, hours passing like minutes; I'd lose myself in some task, and when I stepped outside the day would be over. Sometimes it stood still, a single minute stretching as long as a year. Sometimes it overlapped — yesterday would surface in the middle of today, a moment from ten years ago standing as vivid as the present.

For years I took this for a flaw. Everyone's clock ticked away neatly; mine had gone mad. Society imposed a rhythm — this in the morning, that in the evening, this much per week, this much in a lifetime — and I never settled into that rhythm, and the more I tried, the more I tore myself apart.

The awakening taught me to take my own time seriously. When I sped up — when that wave of hyperfocus came, when my brain locked onto something and forgot the world — to make myself stop. When I stopped, in those empty moments, not to blame myself. To silence that old voice that said, "Why are you stopping while everyone else is running?" Society's rhythm doesn't have to be my rhythm. I'm a musician who plays to my own metronome; the more I tried to fit into the orchestra, the more I played out of tune.

Inner voice: "But what if I'd always thought this way?" — For a moment I see that counter-life: a Tuncay who accepted his own tempo years ago, who stopped blaming himself. How much energy would have been left over, who knows. Then I smile: "Maybe then you couldn't have gone deep enough to write this. Understanding late is also a kind of understanding." And for the first time, this acceptance relaxed my body — my shoulders dropped, my jaw loosened, my breath fell all the way down to my belly.

Boundaries, the Past, Identity

I no longer say "yes" to everything. This sentence sounds simple, but for me it was as vast as discovering a continent. All my life I said "yes" — yes when I was tired, yes when I didn't want to, yes even when everything inside me screamed no. Because in my old equation, saying "no" meant being unloved; it meant being abandoned, left outside.

Now I say "no." And this word protects me. It's as if a shield has formed inside me — invisible but solid. The first time I said it, my hands trembled, my voice cracked, guilt came in waves. Now "no" isn't a drama for me, it's a complete sentence. I don't add an explanation, I don't apologize, I don't put a paragraph of justification behind it. Just: "No." Period.

Once I lived in the past. When I got into bed at night, a cinema would open in my head — always the same scenes, in an endless loop. That word, that look, that door, that farewell. Every night I'd watch it over and over, as if this time the ending would change. It never changed. I'd wake exhausted in the morning, as if I'd been fighting all night — because I had been fighting, with ghosts.

Now I see them, I remember them — but I no longer get stuck. The past has become a museum. I can walk through it, I can say "look, this happened here," I can even sometimes pause for a moment to look. But I can close the door behind me and walk out. I used to live in the museum; now I visit.

Inner voice: "It happened. It's over. I learned. Thank you, past. Now I'm looking ahead." — I sometimes say these four sentences like a prayer. Especially the "thank you" part — saying that took me years. Because giving thanks, it turns out, is the secret door to forgiveness.

I am no longer a victim, no longer a loss, no longer something lacking. For years I told myself these things: I'm a victim, I'm a loss, I'm lacking. I carried them like an identity, because it was a familiar pain, and being familiar made it safe. But one day I let it go. I am not a victim. I am my experience. The sum of everything that happened to me, and the person who emerged from that sum.

Inner voice: "I am a walking story. Still being written." — And the most beautiful part is this: the pen is in my hand. For a long time I thought others were writing my story. As it turned out, the page was always open, the pen was always in my hand, I was only afraid to write.

Do you know what strength is? Not muscle. Not the thickness of your wrist. Not money, not the zeros in your bank account. Certainly not the loudness of your voice, the thunder of the fist you bang on the table. Strength is the ability to keep going. To rise one more time after falling. On a night with no witness, when no one will applaud, to take one more breath all the same.

★ This is psychological resilience.

The old Tuncay died. I say this not with sorrow but with respect. The new Tuncay is calmer, more conscious, stronger. But don't get me wrong — this doesn't mean I built walls, that I hardened. Quite the opposite. At the same time: more sensitive, more profound, more real. I learned that being strong and being soft can live in the same person. As it turns out, the strongest people are the softest ones.

Inner voice: "I no longer run away. I stand and face it. And this is freedom." — All my life I ran. From pain, from conflict, from myself. Running, it turns out, is exhausting; standing still is not. When I stopped, the thing I feared didn't swallow me; it just flowed past me and was gone.

After Having Tasted Everything

I understood something only after years, and this understanding was perhaps the most painful of them all. I was someone who thought of everything, who ran every possibility through his mind, who talked to himself without pause. While drinking a single tea, I'd plan three different futures, rewrite two pasts, and listen to the present conversation as well — all at the same time. That mind was an engine, never stopping, overheating, sometimes burning. And that mind slowly grew calm. Not by being shouted at — no one told it to "be quiet," and that wouldn't have worked anyway. By maturing. My thoughts grew up along with me.

Because life has a strange law, and I wrote this law in the first chapter too, and here it came before me once more: whatever I ran from, whatever I feared, whatever I pushed away, life brought it back to me as an even harder test for a while. The more I pushed, the closer it came. The more I resisted, the heavier it grew. You know how you hold a door shut, pressing "don't come in," and the more you push, the more it's pushed back from the other side — it's just like that.

And one day, when I was spent — truly spent, when I had no strength left to push — I tried something else: accepting. Trying to understand. I stopped holding the door. And that was when the knot came undone. Not resisting, but accepting, was the better solvent. For years I'd used the wrong tool; I'd tried to open the knot with a hammer, when all it needed was a loosened hand.

And the strangest part is this: I could only build real empathy after having tasted everything. Without living through what I condemned, without passing through it, I couldn't truly understand those people. I did the things I once said "I'd never do." Life sat me down in those very seats. I fell into the mistakes I'd judged — and into precisely the ones I'd judged most. And for exactly this reason, I can no longer judge anyone from above. Because I, too, sat in that seat. I, too, lay at that bottom. I, too, walked through that darkness, groping with my hands.

Living, existing, turned out to be an inevitable consequence — and the person who fully takes it on the chin softens in the end. Iron passed through the fire of pain either breaks or bends; I chose to bend. What remains is not hardness but understanding. The hard shell always belongs to those who are afraid; whoever learns not to fear sets the shell down.

Inner voice: "So real compassion comes from the other end of pain. Had I not lived what I condemned, I'd have stayed someone who judges rather than understands." — And sometimes, with a strange gratitude, I even thank the worst things that happened to me. Without them, I wouldn't have had so soft a heart. Maybe I'd have stayed clean — but I'd have stayed blind too.

I broke this cycle. Because I saw early what was done to my father. So as not to carry on the same thing, I never took on debt, didn’t want it, didn’t allow it. Not once did a letter or a phone call come to my house saying “you haven’t paid this.” If I borrowed, I paid it back. Not because I was fleeing responsibility — I didn’t want to become my father; never.

I had four daughters, but also a son I had to raise inside me: the Tuncay within. While I tried to take good care of him, I lost myself. One solution gave birth to the next problem; I wrestled with my life, I fought.

In my second separation I grew up, I matured; I didn’t make the same mistakes. I didn’t panic, didn’t attack, I accepted. I understood my children’s mother and what she had been through. Today she can calmly leave the girls with me when they come for the holidays — because there’s no panic anymore.

Today my eldest daughter doesn’t speak to me. Because I projected my fears onto her too. And yet I love her so much. But for a time I didn’t look at things from her side. That child only wanted a loving father; one who wants to be loved even if her parents separate, a father without a mask, who is truly happy, who plays happily. I couldn’t see that at the time — and it became one of the greatest lessons of my life.

Therapist's Note – Self-Acceptance and Trauma Integration

What is happening here: This chapter is, in the textbook sense, a portrait of trauma integration and self-acceptance — and importantly, it shows this not in the abstract but in concrete, measurable changes. One: episodes of anger drop from days to minutes; this is one of the clearest signs of improved emotion regulation. Two: a "gap" appears between trigger and reaction — this is the brain's shift from reactivity to responsiveness. Three: empathy that once overwhelmed turns into a conscious capacity. Four: the body holds less tension; shoulders, jaw, breath relax. But the real change is linguistically tiny, spiritually enormous: the sentence "I am wrong" gives way to the sentence "This is who I am." This is not cheap positive thinking; it is the result of long and genuine labor — the brain slowly rewired its old protective loop.

Where it comes from — body and evolution

When you said that morning in the kitchen that the sounds "flowed through and didn't leap up," you were actually describing a very concrete neurobiological change. The amygdala, what Bessel van der Kolk calls "the brain's smoke detector," becomes hypersensitive in trauma; it treats the smallest crackle as a threat (The Body Keeps the Score, 2014). With healing, the regulatory areas of the forebrain (the prefrontal cortex) become able to brake this alarm again — the alarm is still there, but it no longer mistakes every sound for a fire. Stephen Porges's Polyvagal Theory makes this even clearer: without our awareness, our body is constantly scanning the environment for "am I safe?" — he calls this "neuroception" (Porges, 2011). That stillness you called "didn't leap up" is your nervous system beginning to settle into the safe mode (the ventral vagal state). When the body feels safe, the shoulders drop, the jaw loosens, the breath falls to the belly — what you described is exactly this.

Your saying that empathy "opened up" is no coincidence either. The brain's mirror neuron systems and emotion-reading circuits are freed to read another's inner state once a person lowers their own threat level; while constantly on alert, there are no resources left to spare for empathy. Your observation, "first my own alarm went quiet, then I could see the other person," is, neurologically, the correct sequence. And anger "dropping from days to minutes" is the loosening of rumination — the brain's default mode network replaying the same scene over and over (Nolen-Hoeksema, 1991). And your sentence "I couldn't understand without living what I condemned" is the essence of the concept of post-traumatic growth: Tedeschi and Calhoun show that, in some people, pain can produce, alongside ruin, deepening, compassion, and meaning (1996). Viktor Frankl's logotherapy says this too: if pain is inevitable, attaching a meaning to it transforms it.

If left unaddressed — possible bad outcomes

This acceptance is not a permanent possession but a garden that requires tending. The old patterns don't vanish forever; their voices are merely turned down. Under stress, sleeplessness, a new loss, or an old trigger, the "I am wrong" voice, the flight response, and the victim role can all sound again at any moment. A person who entirely abandons the practice of self-compassion can quietly slide back into the old loops — and most insidious of all, miss new opportunities without noticing, because they're viewing them through old glasses. The masks can also be recalled; the "strong mask" in particular is the habit that returns fastest in hard moments. If left unaddressed, that "gap" that was gained narrows again, the door between trigger and reaction closes again, and the person finds themselves in the trap of self-blame, asking "I had healed, why did I go back?" — when in fact this is not a failure but a garden whose tending has lapsed.

Paths of healing — several doors

At this point there are a few paths that help; all are built on protecting and deepening what has been gained:

• Self-compassion (Kristin Neff) and Compassion-Focused Therapy (Paul Gilbert): A small daily practice — "Today I can be kind to myself." To answer the critical voice inside you as you would speak to a friend. This is the most basic daily care that protects what has been gained.
• Internal Family Systems (IFS – Richard Schwartz): What you called "they all sat down at the same table, and I took my place at the head" is the very heart of IFS. To see the parts (the angry protector, the frightened child, the analytical manager) not as enemies but all as members trying to protect you; with a calm, compassionate "Self" standing at the head of the table.
• Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT): Your sentence "not resisting, but accepting, was the better solvent" is the essence of ACT. Instead of suppressing a thought, making room for it, watching it, and acting according to your values.
• Boundary work: Exercises that turn "no" from a drama into a complete sentence — without explanation, without apology. Each small "no" strengthens the shield.
• Expressive writing (Pennebaker): This book itself is a product of this therapy. Putting emotion into writing improves mood and even immunity (Pennebaker, 1997).
• Body-based regulation: A long exhale stimulates the vagus nerve and draws the system into the safe mode. The calm you've gained is consolidated not with the mind but with the body.

Tuncay's bridge

I did most of these without knowing their names — then, as I read, I'd say once again, "so this had a name." When I first read that paradox of Carl Rogers's, I stopped: "When I accept myself as I am, only then can I change." Because all my life I'd tried the exact opposite — I'd assumed I would first tear myself apart and fix myself, and then accept myself. It turned out the order is reversed. When I learned ACT, I saw that my intuition that "accepting was a better solvent than resisting" was an entire school of therapy; I wasn't alone. IFS told me that the crowd inside me was not a madness but a family — and I had already taken my place at the head of the table, I just didn't know its name. And when I read the literature on post-traumatic growth, I felt both relieved and shaken: so that painful thing I called "I couldn't understand without living what I condemned," that fire that deepens a person, was a real path recognized by science. All I did throughout this whole book was actually this: I took science and spread it out like a map of my own life. In this chapter, that map showed me, for the first time, not "the end of the road" but "solid ground." And let me be honest — this ground wants to be chosen again every day. Some mornings the old Tuncay knocks at the door. I don't open it. But I know he's there, and I no longer resent him. Once, it was he who kept me standing.

Where I Stand Today

Here I can say without hesitation: this has been achieved — not as a "final state," but as a reliable baseline stance. Anger that once stayed for days now passes within minutes. "No" is my shield; I can say it without trembling. I visit the past but no longer sit there, I can leave the museum. Empathy no longer drowns me, it's a capacity I use with awareness. And the sentence "This is who I am, and it's enough" is no longer a wish — it's something I genuinely mean when I look in the mirror in the morning. The old Tuncay really did die. I don't miss him, but I thank him; without his stubborn resistance, there would be no ground for today's calm. My one honest note is this: this calm doesn't come for free. Every day it asks for a little tending, a little compassion, sometimes a renewed choice. But now I know what to do — and knowing that is, in itself, a peace.

"The curious paradox is that when I accept myself just as I am, then I can change."

— Carl Rogers

I remain a draft that writes itself daily — until my last breath.
