Chapter 5 – Escape, Growing Up Too Soon, and the First Rebellion

Then I grew up. Early. I’d make a decision and dive into it headfirst, thinking about the consequences afterward. To those who asked “What’s the plan?” I had no plan to give; an “I have to” would explode inside me, and the rest came along the way. Sometimes it led me to the edge of a cliff, sometimes to the most beautiful story of my life. If they’d told me “Sit down and think first,” I couldn’t have done a thing. Good thing I jumped without thinking. The most beautiful things always began, for me, with a crazy idea.

I was fourteen. I had finished school. Here's the strange part: I had graduated top of my class in the final exams. And as the only "foreigner," no less.

I can still picture that classroom. The smell of chalk dust, the heavy, woolen odor of wet coats drying on the radiator, the shuffle of feet from the hallway. The desks would creak. When the teacher wrote something on the board, that thin scratch of chalk would send a shiver up my spine. I sat somewhere in the very back — close to the wall, close to the door, always close to an exit. My grades were well above average; the teachers liked me.

But the diploma and the certificate never made it into my hands. Because we moved again. We were in Ingolstadt, then we moved on to Stuttgart. And there the next hurdle appeared: the qualifying lower-secondary diploma (qualifizierender Hauptschulabschluss) I had earned in Bavaria did not count in Baden-Württemberg. So at fifteen I continued at the Realschule, made up the intermediate level (mittlere Reife) and earned my Realschule diploma (Realschulabschluss). At sixteen I moved into my own apartment and began my apprenticeship as an industrial mechanic.

"Again." That word. The most faithful word of my life. Every time I started to truly put down roots somewhere, they pulled the ground out from under me. I would begin to recognize a teacher's face, to grow used to the creak of a desk, to memorize a child's name — and then the suitcases. Always the suitcases.

My family had already left before the exams. Throughout the exams I lived nearly a month alone in a boarding house. Just imagine: a fourteen-year-old boy, all alone in a strange room, getting up in the morning to sit his exams, passing top of the class — and in the evening sharing it with no one.

I remember that boarding-house room. A narrow bed, with a gray-blue cover frayed at the edges. The faucet on the sink dripped; in the silence of the night that drip ticked like a giant clock: tick… tick… tick. The wallpaper had curled away from one corner and yellowed. The light of a gas station fell through the window; a bluish, cold glow that drew a perfectly straight rectangle on the ceiling. In the mornings I would wake in that light, get dressed, and go to the exam. My stomach clenched — not from the exam, but from something else: from the emptiness of a morning where no one said "good luck."

The certificate came by mail. Inside a small envelope. I opened the door, took the envelope, set it on the table. For a while I just looked at it. The paper of the envelope was thin, slightly rough; it left a formal, cold feeling between my fingers. Inside was my first place. And around me, there was not a single face to tell.

Inner voice: "Congratulations, Tuncay. You did it. But no one saw." — And another voice answers from within: "So what if they didn't see? You know." But that night, even that answer wasn't enough. Because a fourteen-year-old child doesn't want what he knows; he wants what someone else sees. Not applause — just a glance. A "well done." A hand to be laid on his shoulder.

Eight Weeks of Waiting

Summer vacation began. Eight weeks. I was home. Nothing to do. No plan. There was only one promise: "You'll go to the Turkish youth camp." Every week it was postponed. "We're going this week." "Next week for sure." And then? Canceled.

For eight weeks I went around and around inside the house. The walls were closing in on me — really, physically. I would walk from one room to another for no reason. Kitchen. Living room. Hallway. Kitchen again. I opened and closed the refrigerator, not out of hunger, but to touch something with my hand. Time grew heavy. That hot, stagnant air of summer afternoons hung suspended in the room; the curtains never stirred, and the air left a stale taste in my mouth. The hours simply would not pass. The tick-tock of the clock on the wall seemed to slow down more and more; the gap between two seconds stretched longer and longer.

I would look out the window and see children passing on bicycles. That faint whir of tires on asphalt, someone's burst of laughter, the double click-clack of a bell. Life was on the other side of the glass. I sat inside and waited. The glass was that thin, invisible wall separating me from life — I could see everything, I could touch nothing.

Inner voice: "Why is my whole life just waiting? Other kids are on vacation, going places… Why am I always stuck in 'later'?" — And right behind it, that sly thought: "Maybe 'later' will never come. Maybe 'later' is their way of saying 'never.'"

I was suffocating. Really. Something inside me clenched, like a fist pressing down on the center of my chest, and it never let up. I would breathe, but the air seemed to reach only halfway into my lungs and snag there. My jaw clenched without my noticing. Then my head would ache. Fourteen — the age when the body begins to scream what it cannot say.

Inner voice: "What if this camp were real? What if, just once, a single time, a promise were kept? I would get on the bus, with other kids, kids who spoke my own language… Maybe one of them would ask, 'Who are you?' and I would tell them." Then my mind shuts that imagined scene down — because imagining something that won't happen too beautifully hurts twice as much when it never comes.

Work as a Door to Escape

Then came work — and with it, the first real door opening to the outside. Because working was the most legitimate way to escape the house. If a person is working, no one can blame them. A payslip is an alibi. A shift schedule is the polite way of saying "leave me alone."

My father rented me a basement place, a single room, an hour away from home. It was cold. The kitchen was overrun with mice. At night I would hear those tiny scratching sounds inside the wall — scratch, scratch, then silence, then again. The smell of damp, that moist, mineral odor of concrete, was always in the air. There was a single bulb, bare, hanging from the ceiling; its light was yellowish and merciless, never fully lighting any corner, splitting them all with shadow. It wasn't presented as a kind of freedom. No one said, "here, this is your own home." But for me, that's exactly what it was. A freedom with mice, with cold, with a bare bulb. And I preferred it to a warm but suffocating home.

Inner voice: "This place is terrible. This place is mine. For the first time there's a door I can close behind me, and no one will open and close it for me. The mice? Even they aren't telling me I'm a burden here."

Here I have to add something that has stayed inside me to this day. I had been accepted into an art school — my father swept it aside with the back of his hand: "What will you do there, it's pointless." That sentence. That "pointless." With a single word, the curtain is drawn over a child's courage to show something inside himself. Later I won a place in Radiology, in the field of medicine. I was one of six taken from a thousand applicants. Six in a thousand. Just think — six in a thousand. But the program had tuition, and my father wasn't keen. I didn't want to be a burden to them, so I didn't accept. I was tired of being a burden. Tired to the bone.

Inner voice: "Maybe I'm not worth someone investing in me. Maybe it's easier to want nothing at all." — Because wanting was dangerous. If you wanted, you could be refused. If you never wanted, no one could say no.

★ If a child has learned to want nothing, it is not because he doesn't want, but because he is afraid of wanting too much.

Even today, I still open that radiology letter in my mind. And my mind, for years, simply will not close that scene; each time it replays it with a different ending.

Inner voice: "What if I had accepted? Would I now be a man in a white coat, walking hospital corridors? What if my father hadn't said 'tuition,' what if I had, just once, found the courage to say 'I want this'? Would I have been alone in that life too, or would someone there have seen me? Or would that road have broken me even more?" These questions don't want answers. They just turn, and turn — like the ghosts of a life that never happened. In the end I always arrive at the same place: I didn't choose that door, but the door I didn't choose brought me to exactly here, to the man writing these pages.

Talking to Machines

My first real job was in a factory. We made gearboxes. The smell of iron, oily floors, vibrating machines. When I walked through that door in the morning, the way the air grew heavy — the mingled smell of cold metal, cutting oil, warmed-up electric motors filled my nose. The floor was coated with a film of oil; as I stepped, the sole of my shoe would stick slightly, then release. The fluorescent lights on the ceiling hissed. And the machines — that constant, deep hum came up through the soles of my feet into my bones. People got tired. I listened.

When a machine's sound changed, I noticed it immediately. The others lost it inside that hum; for me, the hum was an orchestra, and when a violin slipped out of tune within it, I heard it. The vibration, the rhythm, the brief hesitation — all of it told me something. One machine would say "I'm fine," another would say "something inside me is wearing out." No one had taught me this. It came on its own. I would lay my palm on the metal and read the vibration in my hand. The machine spoke to me, and I listened.

I didn't know it then, but I got along with systems far better than with people. Because machines don't shout. They aren't capricious. They don't say "yes" one day and "no" the next. There is a cause-and-effect relationship. If you press here, something happens there — every time. Same cause, same result. That gave me trust. People were a riddle to me; machines were an answer. I trained as an industrial mechanic.

Inner voice: "Finally, a place where rules carry meaning. 'Do this' — I do it. 'You did it wrong' — I can ask why. At home that was never possible. At home, 'wrong' meant 'be quiet.' Here, 'wrong' is a door opening onto the next right thing."

But the system had a price: it only saw the worker in me. I had ideas. I noticed mistakes — an unnecessary step in a process, an illogical flow in a layout. I thought of other solutions, rebuilding the whole line inside my head. No one cared. "Just do your job." That sentence was familiar. It was my father's "pointless" dressed in a work apron. Only the voice had changed; the content was the same: "You're not meant to see."

I learned incredibly fast, and got bored just as fast. Once I understood something, repeating it felt like torture; my brain wanted a new stimulus, a new riddle, a new leap. Responsibility was handed out according to a plan and a clock — but I was timeless, learning as an autodidact, in leaps, at my own rhythm. I might learn a subject in one breath at three in the morning and then not show up for the class the next morning. They couldn't cope with this. Everything had to march like soldiers: many rules, strict order, fixed hours. I couldn't do it. It seemed illogical to me. When I asked, "Why exactly at nine? Why in this order?" the answer was "because that's how it is" — and "because that's how it is" is the one food my brain cannot digest. I took a lot of sick leave right up to the exam.

Inner voice: "The problem isn't me, the problem is this mold. They're trying to fit me into a box, but I'm not box-shaped. Their mistake isn't thinking the box is wrong — it's thinking I'm the one who's wrong."

And the funniest part: I was supposed to attend school for three and a half years. I went for one year and skipped the rest. Even so, I sat the exam. And I graduated top of the class. The teachers were left speechless. I won't forget the expression on one of their faces: half astonished, half angry, a look that said "how is this possible?" The AuDHD brain sometimes works like this: fails attendance, wins the exam. Doesn't fit the system's form, but answers the system's question better than anyone.

Like an Adult at Sixteen

The money I earned was mine. This — at sixteen — was dizzying. To hold in my palm the paper of money I had earned myself was a strange, intimate kind of power. I bought my own shoes. My own food. My own silence. Even silence was something that could be bought now — a room where no one said "do this, don't do that," with my own money.

But I didn't get my childhood back. I had already let it go. While my peers were playing games, I was calculating shifts. While they talked about the score of a match, I was working out the end-of-month accounts. This, too, became a mask: "the hardworking boy," "matured early." People praised it. "Look how grown up he is, bless him." But no one asked: why did this child have to escape so early? A child growing up too soon is not an achievement; more often it is a wound — just a wound wrapped in a pretty word.

Inner voice: "They think I'm 'mature.' I'm not mature. I'm just afraid, and fear silences a person early. Maturity is something you choose; mine is a garment forced onto me — too big for my body, too tight for my soul."

I drank alcohol for the first time. At a friend's house. The bottle was cold in my hand — that sweat-like dampness of the glass on my palm. The first sip burned my throat; bitter, sharp, a taste I wanted to recoil from. With the second, the world softened a little — the corners blurred just slightly, that fist in my chest loosened a little. With the third I thought, "I don't care." And there it was — that "I don't care" — the first place a child who until that moment had cared about everything far too much finally took a breath.

Inner voice: "So this is all it takes. So that thing that's always clenched can loosen, just for a moment. This is a dangerous discovery — because once you learn there's a button for loosening, you have to find a reason not to press it."

Inner voice: "Rules, waiting, 'later'… enough. From now on I set my own rules."

My father's rules were clear and rigid. But I no longer bowed to them. I raised my voice — that rising tone of my own voice was foreign to me; it both frightened me and gave me strength. I slammed doors. That slam exploded inside the house like a gunshot; the silence that followed was even louder. For the first time I said "Enough." And when I said that word, a door locked inside me for years cracked open with a single creak.

Inner voice: "If I stay here, I'll be destroyed. This house is killing me slowly." — I'm not saying this for the sake of drama. A fourteen-year-old body really does feel it: a slow drowning, an invisible pressure. And when a body feels this, escaping is not a rebellion, it's a survival instinct.

At night I would lie in bed and stare at the ceiling. From outside came the sound of cars, the whistle of a distant train — that whistle, drawn out and far away, a sound saying "there are other places." The sound of a train is the sound of everything that can leave. "One day I'll go. Far from this house, these rules, this endless waiting," I would think. On the darkness of the ceiling I drew the map of that departure — not knowing where; but far away, definitely far away. I never really lived a childhood. I went straight to surviving. Some children grow up; some learn straight away to endure.

Inner voice: "Am I a child, or already an adult? I'm fourteen but I feel like thirty. Or the exact opposite: at thirty I'm still fourteen. Both are inside me — one a child who never played, one a man who tired far too soon. The two are constantly talking to each other, and neither ever fully falls silent."

A decision ripened inside me: "I will be myself." The only problem was this: I didn't know who I was. I had set a direction but had no compass. I was walking toward a "myself," toward someone — but I had never seen that someone's face.

Inner voice: "I don't know, but I'll find out. I won't stop until I do. I won't give up. Like Goku: even if I'm lying on the ground, I get back up."

That summer, those eight weeks, that repeated school year, my first room with mice, the first sip that burned my throat, the first "Enough" — all of it was preparing me for something. For escape. For rebellion. And, most of all: for the search for myself. I didn't know it then, but all of this was not a loss, it was a beginning. It's just that the face of beginnings is never beautiful at the moment they begin.

Therapist's Note – Premature Independence and Identity Fragmentation

What is happening here: A child who cannot find a stable, safe environment shifts early into survival mode. When constant relocations, a lack of emotional support, and an AuDHD brain that already struggles to hold direction and motivation come together, forced premature maturation is the result. In developmental psychology this is called a state close to "parentification": the child is forced to take on a level of responsibility and self-sufficiency far beyond his years — but he pays the price for it inside, as a childhood never lived. Work becomes the door to escape, alcohol the first valve, and a distorted sense of time ("waiting" grows heavy in the body) becomes a permanent condition. Important: this is not a "difficult adolescent." This is a nervous system doing what it must to get by. Always sitting near the door in class, pacing from room to room at home — these are not laziness or moodiness, but a body on high alert searching for a way out.

The relationship formed with machines is also striking. A child being able to "read" machines better than people is no coincidence; it is a classic feature of the autistic side: systems are predictable, people are not. The talent that will later turn into a profession appears here early.

Where it comes from — body and evolution

That "fist pressing down on the center of the chest," that feeling of suffocation, is not random. A nervous system that is constantly relocated, that can never get a clear answer to the question "when am I safe?", remains chronically on alert. In Stephen Porges's Polyvagal Theory, this is called impaired "neuroception": the body, without our awareness, constantly scans the environment and asks, "can I put down roots here?" (Porges, 2011). When the answer is always "maybe, but soon you'll leave again," the system never learns to rest. In Bessel van der Kolk's words, "the body keeps the score": what cannot be spoken is recorded in the muscles, in the clench of the stomach, in the tightened jaw (The Body Keeps the Score, 2014). Your jaw clenching at fourteen, your headaches, are precisely this record.

The "heaviness" of waiting is also a real neurological phenomenon. In the ADHD brain, the perception of time (linked to "temporal discounting" and low dopaminergic stimulation) works differently: time without stimulation, without reward, stretches out in a way that is physically painful (Barkley's work on ADHD and executive functions). So those eight weeks "simply not passing" was not an exaggeration; it was the direct experience of the brain's hunger for stimulation. Turning to machines is the other side of this: the autistic brain's tendency toward "systemizing" (Simon Baron-Cohen's "empathizing–systemizing" framework) finds a deep sense of safety in a predictable world of cause and effect. That a machine doesn't shout, isn't capricious — for a child coming from a chaotic home, this is, in the fullest sense, a refuge.

Learning to refuse, to want nothing, is connected to attachment and emotional neglect. As John Bowlby and Mary Ainsworth showed, if a child cannot develop trust that his needs will be met, he learns to shut down his needs in the name of self-protection. Jonice Webb calls this Childhood Emotional Neglect (Running on Empty, 2012): the equation "if I don't want, I won't be refused." Saying "no" to the radiology letter was not a logical error — it was a protective reflex a child had learned over years.

If left unaddressed — possible bad outcomes

The real risk is identity fragmentation: the brain turns "I can't put down roots anywhere" into "I don't know who I am," and carries this into adulthood as a chronic feeling of "Where am I? What am I doing?" On top of this comes an increased risk of addiction — the discovery of the first loosening button is rarely the last escape route; once you can say "I don't care," becoming attached to a substance, to an escape, becomes easy. Self-harming tendencies and a chronic belief in one's own worthlessness can be added to this. The rejected Radiology is symptomatic here: someone who has learned "I am a burden" says no even to his own chances in order to protect himself — and years later the regret of "what if I had done it?" settles into the mind as rumination (replaying the same scene again and again with different endings). If left unaddressed, the stubbornness of "I will be myself" cannot find a direction and can turn into drifting, into constantly starting over, into being unable to complete anything.

Paths of healing — several doors

At this point there are a few doors that help:

• Narrative therapy: Rewriting the story of "the child who had to grow up too soon, whom no one saw." You can't change the events, but you can rewrite their meaning: instead of "I was a burden," "I was a clever child who shut down his needs in order to protect himself." This book itself is a practice of narrative therapy.
• Internal Family Systems (IFS – Richard Schwartz): The dialogue between the "child who never played" and the "man who tired far too soon" inside you is exactly IFS's field of work. Neither is the enemy; one is a wounded "exile," one a "manager" protecting him. The goal is for a compassionate "Self" to take charge of both.
• Expressive writing (Pennebaker): Putting into words those eight weeks, that boarding-house room, that "no one saw" moment. Pennebaker's research shows that writing about emotion improves mood and immunity (1997).
• Somatic approaches (Peter Levine – Somatic Experiencing) and body awareness: The "fist in the chest" cannot be solved with logic; it loosens only with the body. A long exhale, feeling your feet on the ground, consciously relaxing the muscles — by stimulating the vagus nerve, this draws the system into "safe" mode.
• Self-compassion (Kristin Neff) and Compassion Focused Therapy (Paul Gilbert): Being able, today, as an adult, to say "good luck" to that fourteen-year-old child, to lay that hand on his shoulder. Saying to yourself what no one said.
• Identity work within Erikson's framework: Erik Erikson showed that identity is not found but built — piece by piece, by trying, by saying "this is me / this is not me." The stubbornness here — "I will be myself" — is not a problem, it is raw material; it just needs to find a direction.

Tuncay's bridge

Years later, when I read Porges and van der Kolk, I understood why those eight weeks simply would not pass — I hadn't lost my mind, my nervous system had just been writhing from a hunger for reward. That was also when I figured out why I felt so close to machines: they didn't betray me, because they weren't capricious. This wasn't an escape, it was an attunement; it was my autistic brain finding a language that felt safe to it. And today I can finally smile at the AuDHD brain's story of "failing attendance and winning the exam" — because it was not a broken student, but a right mind forced into the wrong mold. The hardest thing was making peace with the Radiology I had refused: for a long time I kept replaying that scene with different endings, asking "what if I had done it?" Then I understood that the child, with the only information he had, had made the most logical decision to protect himself. I stopped being angry at him. I began to understand him. That was exactly the bridge: taking science and laying it over that fourteen-year-old child like a blanket — saying, "you were not wrong; no one ever gave you the right environment."

Where I Stand Today

I truly lived out the sentence "I will be myself, but I don't know who I am" all the way to the end — it just took twenty years. The child who understood machines later became a project manager, then an Artificial Intelligence specialist and Data Scientist. The talent was there from the start; it just waited a long time for an environment that didn't tell it "just do your job." What I have overcome is this: I no longer take refuge in work to escape feeling; working has become not an escape but a choice. That mouse-ridden basement, and that "no one saw" night, I now remember not with shame but with respect — that child endured. And what I carry with me, and have held on to, is this: stubbornness. "Even if I'm lying on the ground, I get back up" did not remain a child's saying. It became my operating system.

"What does not kill me makes me stronger."

— Friedrich Nietzsche

I am not the end, I am the way to it. A step each day, until my last breath.
