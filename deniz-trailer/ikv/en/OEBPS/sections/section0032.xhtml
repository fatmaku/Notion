Chapter 11 – Rock Bottom and the Diagnosis

I often set off at three in the morning. “I’ll leave at three,” I’d say, and I would; because standing still in my head at night was harder than being on the road. Movement did me good. If the phone lost signal for a moment, I’d say “I’m going to lose it.” A small thing, I know. But with me small things turn into big waves; when a connection breaks, it’s as if the world breaks. For years I couldn’t explain this to anyone. Turns out it had a name.

The night I hit rock bottom, I was in Istanbul. In my father’s caravan, in Üsküdar, at night, alone. It was getting on toward three o’clock. Outside, a single street lamp was burning; an orange, flickering light seeped through the fogged glass and made a pale stain on the ceiling. I stared at that stain. The cold was merciless, it seemed to come from within, cutting from skin to bone. The metal walls of the caravan trembled softly in the wind, without pause; as if someone were running their fingertips over the metal and saying, “You’re here, you’re still here.” And right at that moment, I laughed at myself. “The man’s hit rock bottom and he’s still writing a poem about the light stain on the ceiling.” That very laugh saved me.

And one day, after so much searching, after so many question marks, I found a room inside me that had a name. The diagnosis. AuDHD. Both autism and ADHD, together, two worlds constantly at war inside me. One part of me wanted order, routine, silence; the other wanted excitement, novelty, movement. Two drives fought each other, and I tore apart in the middle. “Are you scattered or obsessive, which is it?” they’d say. Both. When I first heard that word, I cried — not from sadness, but from the relief of “so I’m not lazy.” That feeling I’d carried my whole life, “something’s wrong with me,” turned all at once into an explanation. It wasn’t me who was wrong; it was a world that didn’t understand me. My inner war was, in truth, a search for balance. I’m not between two worlds — I’m the bridge between two worlds. And the view from this bridge is beautiful.

There came a moment when everything fell silent. Not outside. Within me.

It was a strange kind of silence. The world's sounds were all in their place — the hum of the refrigerator, the tires of a passing car hissing over the asphalt outside, a muffled laugh leaking through from the neighbor's television — but inside, right in the middle of my head, that engine which had not gone quiet for years had suddenly stopped. Not the thinking engine; that was still running. What had stopped was hope. That thin, stubborn murmur of "maybe tomorrow will be a little better." Once it was cut off, all that remained was a ticking.

There was no place left for me to escape to. Not a relationship, not work, not the body — nothing filled the emptiness anymore. There had always been a door before: a new love, a new project, a new city, a new decision. I would fill the emptiness inside me by running toward one of those doors. But that night I realized I had tried every door, one by one, and behind each of them was the same room. The same emptiness. The same me.

At night I would sit alone at home. Most of the time I wouldn't turn on the light; the dark was less tiring than too much brightness. The walls would close in on me — really, like a physical sensation, I would feel the room shrinking centimeter by centimeter. The clock would tick, but time seemed to have stopped. Tick. Tick. Tick. Each tick fell into the same emptiness, arriving nowhere. I was breathing, but I was not living. My chest rose and fell; a reflex. My body was still programmed to survive, while the me inside had long since let go.

Inner voice: "What now? How much longer will it go on like this? Is it me who's wrong, or is the system wrong?"

I withdrew from people. Family: very far away. Friends: almost none. I didn't want to talk to anyone. A name would flash on my phone screen, my finger would hover over it, then pull back. Talking took energy; all I had left was just enough to stay silent. But here was the bitter part: I wasn't talking to the outside, and inside I never fell silent. There was a relentless crowd inside my head — one accusing, one defending, one wanting to get up and leave, one saying "hold on a little longer." At night I would stare at the ceiling in bed. Hours would pass, sometimes until morning. My eyes open, my body still, my mind at full throttle. This is the cruelest kind of exhaustion: your body is spent, but your brain won't shut down.

Inner voice: "Why am I like this? Why is life so hard? How do other people live through all of this so easily?"

Inner voice: "Or maybe they don't live it easily, and I just can't see their mask? No… that can't be. They can laugh. They can get up in the morning and go somewhere. They have something I don't. Or I have some flaw they don't."

One day everything stopped. Truly stopped. My second divorce was predictable — even being able to say that was a kind of pain, because I was like someone who watches the oncoming train and still cannot pull himself off the tracks. I saw it, I knew it, but I couldn't move. I can't do it. I can't get up. I had sat on the edge of the bed, and standing up felt like the most impossible movement in the world. My body was made of lead. I'm breathing, but somewhere inside me something has died. I can describe it: in the middle of the chest, just above the heart, where there should be warmth, a cold stone.

Inner voice: "Should I keep going? Is this enough already? Or should I give up?"

In that moment I was at absolute rock bottom. In the darkest place of the darkness. No light was visible, no exit. And the most frightening thing about the bottom wasn't the pain — I had grown used to the pain. The most frightening thing was the indifference. I had even stopped suffering. To want something to end, you have to care about it; and I was no longer in any state to care about anything. That numbness is the quietest danger of all.

The Doctor

Then I went to a doctor. I don't fully remember how I got there — I must have dragged myself, because in those days every step was a decision and every decision was an exhaustion. I remember sitting in the waiting room: the sound of the clock on the wall, others' whispers, an antiseptic smell, the rustle of a paper tissue. Inside, I expected nothing. "He'll give a prescription, it'll pass," I told myself. "Or he won't, doesn't matter."

For the first time, someone truly listened to me. Even writing this, my throat tightens, because how many times in my life had I been listened to? Being listened to is one thing — being heard is another. People often heard me, waited for me to finish speaking, then gave their own answer. This doctor did not do that. He looked long into my eyes — he didn't look away, didn't glance at his phone, didn't abandon me by bowing his head to take notes. He just looked. And under that gaze, something I had carried for years grew lighter.

He said: "Tuncay… get away from all this. You need love. You need care. Leave everything behind. But don't do anything to yourself. You are very intelligent. You are aware of everything. This may be hard."

Silence. In that moment I froze. The words echoed in my head, but one of them rose above the others, like a gong:

★ You are very intelligent.

I couldn't digest that sentence. My brain tried to reject it — like a body rejecting the wrong blood type.

Inner voice: "Me, intelligent? Me? All my life I thought myself stupid… inadequate… wrong… This man must be mistaken. He doesn't know me. If he saw me for two hours, he'd understand."

Inner voice: "But… wait. He said, 'You are aware of everything, this may be hard.' He spoke of the awareness as a difficulty. As if he knew that seeing too much has a price. No one had ever said it that way before."

Why the sentence "don't do anything to yourself" was spoken on that exact day, in that exact order, I understood only later. That man knew who he was looking at. He had gently taken the hand of someone at the edge of a cliff — without shouting, without creating panic, just by saying "I'm here, don't jump." Sometimes a single sentence does the work of an entire therapy.

Then tests were done. An intelligence test. While answering the questions, I was mocking myself inwardly — "let's see how you embarrass yourself now," I said, because my expectation was always the worst; expecting the worst was an old armor I had developed against disappointment. The result: high intelligence.

Inner voice: "How can that be? For years I thought myself stupid. At school, at work, in relationships — I always thought 'I'm the one lacking.' All those 'you can't do it' looks, those 'why can't you be like everyone else' questions… were they all wrong? Or is the paper wrong?"

There was a strange feeling inside me: neither joy nor pride. More like a confusion. As if for years they had called me by the wrong name, and I had taken that wrong name to be my own; now someone had come and spoken my true name, and I had become a stranger to my own name.

The Diagnosis

Then it came: ADHD. And then: autism spectrum, high-functioning. That is, AuDHD. Silence. That silence again — but this time there was a hum inside it, the sound of something falling into place.

In that moment all the pieces of my life came together. As if someone had poured a puzzle onto the table and all the pieces suddenly fit. All those pieces that had lain scattered on the table for years, none of which I thought matched any other — why I remembered people's faces only hours later, why I instantly sensed the smallest change in a setting, why I would get stuck on a subject and forget the world, why I was drained in crowds, why I reacted to injustice far more fiercely than others — they all locked together. The picture appeared. And the picture was me.

Inner voice: "This is why I think this way. This is why I feel this way. This is why I live this way. So the problem wasn't me — my system was just different. For thirty-some years I had been trying to run a correct machine with the wrong user's manual."

At first I didn't believe it. Of course I didn't — believing was always the hardest thing for me. I researched. I read day and night, articles, criteria, other people's accounts. As I saw myself in every line, a strange thing happened: I felt both relieved and afraid. I had the tests repeated. A third time. Three times the same diagnosis. I was so meticulous about not trusting myself that I had to test even my own explanation three times.

Inner voice: "Look again. Maybe I answered wrong the first time. Maybe I wasn't feeling well that day, and the result was inflated. Maybe this time it won't come out the same."

Looking back, this very behavior of testing three times was a very autistic behavior — the search for certainty, the intolerance of uncertainty, the inability to accept a pattern without confirming it. In other words, even the way I rejected the diagnosis proved the diagnosis. When I realized this, I laughed inwardly; it was one of the rare moments in those days when I could laugh.

Inner voice: "So it's real. I'm not wrong. I'm different."

But right beside the relief, that familiar mind kicked in again — it began to replay all the scenes of the past through this new knowledge. Like a film projectionist, I took the reels of my life down from the shelf and ran them through the projector again with a new lens.

Inner voice: "What if I had known this at fourteen? What if, in that rat-infested basement room, someone had told me, 'You're not broken, your brain just works differently'? What if my teachers, my father, the kids in that classroom had known? Would I have turned down radiology then? Those two marriages, those betrayals, that night I gave up my daughters — which of them would have ended differently?"

I reconstructed an entire life through new glasses. Every scene hurt, because in every scene I saw that mapless child — angry at himself, apologizing to everyone, not knowing why he didn't fit. It hurt. But this time, at the end of the reconstruction, there was not sorrow but tenderness: I couldn't be angry at that child. No one had given him the map. To blame him for going the wrong way was like scolding someone who'd never been handed a compass with "why are you lost?"

Inner voice: "What if it had been like this?" I kept asking that for years. Then I learned: that question is a trap. You can't change the past; you can only exhaust your present self with it. Instead of "what if I had known?" I tried saying "I know now, what will I do?" For the first time, the question pushed me forward instead of spinning me in circles.

Until that moment I had thought "I am wrong." From that moment on, I knew "I am different." And this changed everything — not overnight, but from the foundation. I no longer blamed myself. I no longer tried to explain myself to everyone. How tiring that effort of explaining had been, I understood only once I stopped. I had only been trying to reach the right place.

Inner voice: "This is who I am. And this is enough."

Therapist's Note – Existential Collapse and Late Diagnosis

What is happening here: The collapse here is not an end but, paradoxically, a turning point. A decades-long definition of "I am wrong," layered over a never-recognized AuDHD, creates a brain stuck in the "Why me?" loop. The autistic side endlessly scans for threat (sounds, faces, the smallest change in the environment, the "what ifs"), the ADHD side endlessly seeks stimulation and reward — and when enough unprocessed trauma accumulates, the system overheats and crashes. What Tuncay describes is clinically familiar: immobility, a body like lead, the stopping of time, the "cold stone" in the middle of the chest, and the most dangerous of all, that numbness — pain being replaced by indifference. This is not simple sadness; it is the nervous system shifting into "shutdown" after prolonged overload.

Where it comes from — body and evolution

That numbness at rock bottom is not random; it is very ancient survival software. Stephen Porges's Polyvagal Theory describes how, when a threat becomes unbearable, the nervous system goes beyond even "fight or flight" and drops into the most primitive brake of all, dorsal vagal shutdown (freezing, collapse, numbing) (Porges, 2011). Just as an animal in nature "plays dead" in a predator's jaws, when the body calculates that it can no longer fight, it shuts the system down. Tuncay's state of "I can't get up, I'm breathing but not living" is precisely the human counterpart of this primitive brake. This is why Bessel van der Kolk says that trauma is a record first of the body, not the mind — "the body keeps the score" (The Body Keeps the Score, 2014); that cold stone in his chest is the score the body keeps.

The question "How does everyone else live so easily?" is also not an innocent comparison; it is the product of an overactive default mode network. This network kicks in when we are not occupied with the outside world, replaying and reconstructing the past and the future over and over; when it overworks, this is called rumination (Nolen-Hoeksema, 1991). Tuncay replaying his life with "what if I had known at fourteen?" is this network's classic seizure after a late diagnosis — the brain reopens all the old files with the new information. In late-diagnosed adults, this phase of "rewriting the past" is almost the rule, and it contains both grief and healing. Jonice Webb's concept of Childhood Emotional Neglect also comes into play here: because no one gave that child the map, the child wrote the deficiency into his own character — reading "there was no map" as "I am broken."

If left unaddressed — possible bad outcomes

If it is left without explanation and without help, the existential collapse worsens: an entrenched belief that "there is something fundamentally wrong with me," recurring and deepening depressive episodes, total social withdrawal, the collapse of sleep and eating rhythms, and — it must be said plainly — suicidal thoughts. The most insidious form of the danger is the very numbness Tuncay noticed: a person is most fragile not when they stop suffering, but when they stop caring. This is exactly why the doctor's sentence — "don't do anything to yourself" — is vital. The most dangerous belief at this point is not "Life is hard," but "There is something fundamentally wrong with me"; because the first is aimed at the world, the second directly at the person's right to exist.

Paths of healing — several doors

At this threshold there are a few doors that work, and all of them are built on "turning judgment into explanation":

• Safety first, insight second: A system that has gone into shutdown wants safety before it wants persuasion by logic. Somatic Experiencing (Peter Levine) and body-based approaches aim to slowly thaw the frozen energy; planting the feet on the ground, a long exhale (which stimulates the vagus nerve and pulls the system into "safe" mode — Polyvagal Theory), simple rhythmic movement. The mind only listens after the body has relaxed.
• Reframing the diagnosis (CBT / schema therapy): Turning the question "What's wrong with me?" into "How does my system work?" — what Tuncay did spontaneously is, in fact, the very heart of cognitive reframing.
• Internal Family Systems (IFS – Richard Schwartz): That crowd in the head (the accusing, defending, fleeing, "hold on a little longer" voices) are not enemies to be suppressed; they are all protective "parts." Tuncay's "inner voice" dialogue is exactly IFS's field of work.
• Compassion Focused Therapy (Paul Gilbert) and self-compassion (Kristin Neff): Feeling compassion for that mapless child instead of anger — Tuncay's shift from sorrow to tenderness while replaying the past is, clinically, a sign of healing.
• Narrative therapy and expressive writing (Pennebaker): Rewriting life from the story of "I am broken" to the story of "I am an explainable system." This very book passes through this door (Pennebaker, 1997).
• Connection: Reading the accounts of other neurodivergent people. There is no medicine that dissolves the belief "there is something fundamentally wrong with me" as quickly as the sentence "I have the very same thing" — this is why Tuncay seeing himself in those lines as he read was healing.

Tuncay's bridge

I could have taken this diagnosis like a brand and gone home — many people do exactly that, put the paper in a drawer, and carry on with their lives under the same accusation. Without realizing it, I did something else: I read the paper like a user's manual. When I read Porges, I understood that my "can't get up" state was not laziness or weakness, but the most primitive brake my system pressed to protect me; for the first time I could remember that day without being angry at myself. Van der Kolk's sentence, "the body keeps the score," told me that the cold stone in my chest was not imaginary but recorded. And the moment I realized that taking the test three times was "an autistic behavior," that even my rejection confirmed the diagnosis made me laugh — and that laugh was the first tiny door out of the bottom. Today I can honestly say: I crossed this threshold. I do not belittle rock bottom; looking back, I still shudder; but the "I am wrong" operating system no longer runs in me. Over it has been written, "I am different, and different is an explainable system." Every therapist's note in this book is an attempt to hand you that paper not as a brand, but as a map.

Where I Stand Today

This is the moment when I can most clearly say "yes, I made it through." Not because what followed was easy, but because the fundamental equation changed. "I am wrong" had been my operating system for over thirty years; I woke up every morning with that sentence and closed every night with it. The diagnosis overwrote it with "I am different — and different is an explainable system, not an illness." Looking back today, I see that rock bottom was not a punishment but a threshold: because everything fell silent, I could hear my own voice for the first time. I lived through rock bottom, and this is not something to be taken lightly, I know that. If these lines are reaching you and you are sitting in the dark right now: please, don't do anything to yourself. Talk — to a doctor, to someone close to you, to a helpline. The darkest place of the night is most often just before the moment when the pieces are about to come together.

"I am different, not less."

— Temple Grandin

“Finished” is for things, not for people. I am becoming — to the end.
