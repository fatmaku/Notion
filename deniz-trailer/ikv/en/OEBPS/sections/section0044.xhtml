Chapter 16 – The Road Goes On

Where am I now? Right here. I’m still scattered, I still drift off, I still sometimes speak first and think later. I still find my keys in the unlikeliest places, I still forget to eat when I sink into a subject, I still invite people without putting an event on the page. I still carry a heart split between two homes. But I no longer get angry at these things — I laugh at them. I lost a marriage, but I didn’t lose my fatherhood. I lost a traveling companion, but not the dream. My money ran out, a debt-enforcement notice arrived at the house, I was ashamed — and still I got up. I lost years, but I found myself.

I never forgot my mother’s sentence: a sun that has set does not stay set. I too went under, again and again. But every time, I rose anew. I was angry, I missed, I was afraid, I was hurt, I was ashamed — and still I laughed. All of it was real. All of it was me.

Istanbul. This time it wasn't an escape. It was a decision.

The difference began in my body. In the old days, every time I came to this city there would be that familiar tightening in my chest — as if someone had wound a thin wire around my lungs and was slowly pulling it taut. Someone who arrives while fleeing looks not at the place he has reached but at the place he has fled; over his shoulder, always backward. But this time I did not turn my shoulder. I stood on the deck of the ferry, the salty, faintly cold wind of the Marmara striking my face, the gulls hovering as if suspended at the level of my head, and for the first time I looked ahead. The gray-blue water of the Bosphorus, the hazy silhouette of the far shore, the minarets stitching the sky in place like seams. There was no nausea. No tightening. There was only a calm, dignified sentence inside me, planting its feet on the ground like a decision.

Inner voice: "No more hiding. No more concealing. I am going to show myself exactly as I truly am."

Inner voice: "Would you look at that. With all the energy I spent over thirty-some years trying to look 'normal,' I could probably have lit up an entire country. Now I'm letting go of the mask — and the strange thing is, I don't feel naked, I feel light."

What I had learned in Germany was no longer enough. Because knowing is one thing — sharing is something else entirely. Knowledge can sit inside a person like books lined up on shelves; orderly, clean, but never touching anyone. For years I was the keeper of those shelves. I read, I researched, I took notes, I understood. But understanding alone is a single room. Sharing is opening the door of that room. And now I had to share — this was not a wish, it was almost a physical pressure; like an unspoken sentence building up in the chest and squeezing the breath.

Social Media, Podcasts, Television

I opened the first page. Without expectation. Without a plan. Only the truth. HappyTuncay.

I remember that moment: phone in my hand, the cold light of the screen falling on my face, my finger hovering one extra second above the "publish" button. In that single second a little courtroom convenes inside me. One voice says, "What if they laugh?" Another says, "What if no one sees it — that's worse." A third, more weary voice simply says, "Enough already." And I press. The button answers with a vibration — tiny, mechanical, innocent. As if nothing had happened. And yet inside me a door had slammed shut: the door behind me.

Within a week: 10,000 people. Silence. I froze where I stood, staring at the screen. My fingers were trembling slightly. The numbers kept changing on the screen while I sat motionless; somewhere inside me, a very old child — the one who raised his hand at the back of the class and no one ever looked at him — was whispering for the first time, "So I am being seen after all."

Inner voice: "So… people were ready. There are people who feel the way I do."

Inner voice: "Ten thousand people. Ten thousand. That's ten thousand objections to every 'you are alone' lie I ever told myself. For thirty years I'd been adding up the wrong number."

I was invited onto two different podcasts. At first my voice trembled a little. I kept looking at the foam cover on the microphone, and that soft thing suddenly seemed to me like a witness; one that would swallow and record every syllable, an irreversible witness. At the first question my throat went dry, I swallowed, I said "may I have some water" — but what I really wanted wasn't the water, it was one extra second of time. But then I opened up. That wire inside me loosened. I spoke about autism, ADHD, AuDHD, and everything I had lived through. For the first time I told it — without hiding, without softening, without "but anyway." And people listened. Being listened to… I had forgotten what that even was. Someone's eyes resting on yours, not fleeing.

Then something big happened: state television. A program about autism and ADHD. I remember the strange world of that studio — the white, eye-stinging light of the spotlights on the ceiling, the dusty feel of makeup left on my face, the warmth in my ear, the hollow that opened in my stomach when the red light on the camera came on. Sweat on the back of my neck despite the air conditioning. As I sat in the studio, this is what went through my mind:

Inner voice: "The road that began in Germany is now being spoken of in Istanbul. How did I get here?"

Inner voice: "What if someone had come to me at three in the morning in that caravan and said, 'One day you'll talk about autism on state television'? I'd probably have laughed. Bitterly. I'd have said, 'First just make it to morning.' But here I am. The camera is red, and I am calm. Truly calm."

The story resonated deeply in Turkey as well as in Germany. Turkish and German, two worlds side by side. The same wound in the same words in two languages — as if, after a lifetime, a plank had finally been thrown across a river whose two banks had never met, and I was the one walking across it.

Inner voice: "I am the bridge. Between two cultures, two ways of thinking, between pain and hope."

Inner voice: "I know the price of being a bridge, too: everyone crosses over you, and no one asks whether the bridge is tired. But this time it's different — I wanted this bridge. Every foot that crosses me carries some once-lost 'me' to the other side."

Kadıköy: Learning to Stop

And then I did something I had never managed in my whole life: I stopped.

I settled in Kadıköy. I rented an apartment. I cannot describe the moment I first put the key into the lock — a small click, then the bolt settling into its seat, that dignified "clack." Opening a door in my own name. Instead of the wheels of a caravan, I had an address. This is no small thing for me — for someone who never put down roots, who was tossed somewhere new every year, an address is almost a revolution. As I was writing my own address on an envelope, my hand paused for an instant; it felt as if I were writing a lie, and then I understood: no, for the first time I am writing a truth.

Here I am learning to stop. I devoted six months to my book and my social project — but for the first time I also gave time to myself. Without constantly moving, I am trying simply to be in one place, to see the same light through the same window each morning. That light comes through the glass a little differently every morning: some days like milk, some days like gold dust, some days leaden. But always through the same window. And I stay in the same place long enough to be able to notice it. That is the revolution: stopping long enough to be able to see the change.

Inner voice: "Stopping. Even the word used to frighten me. Because stopping meant letting the past catch up with you. I always ran so the memories couldn't run after me. Now I'm standing still — and look, they catch up. But they don't attack. They just sit down beside me."

And a strange thing happened: once I stopped, I became reachable to people. Someone always on the move can never fully reach anyone — a hand always going is like a hand always left out in the open, never finding the chance to be held. Once I stopped in one place, people could reach me — and I could reach them. When a message came, I no longer said, "which city am I going to be in tomorrow?"; I could say, "I'm here, come." I no longer merely think about what I've learned, I put it into action. Knowledge doesn't rot inside me; it goes out and finds someone. The shelves were opened. The room was aired out.

Happyiz and the Butterfly

An idea was born inside me: Happyiz. Not just a name. A system. A space. A community. Not a load I would carry alone — that too I learned, and the hard way. Happyiz means bringing together experts and entrepreneurs, big and small, who are on the path of awareness.

Now I hire an assistant, I share out tasks, I trust. This sentence slips by in a single paragraph, but there is a whole war inside it. Because trusting had always been a risk for me — trust felt to me like the down payment on a betrayal. Whenever I left a task to someone, a worm would gnaw at me through the night: what if they don't do it, what if they do it wrong, what if they leave me stranded halfway? My hands were programmed to grab, to take back, to say "I'll do it better myself." Now I am learning: a bridge is not built from a single stone. A single stone makes no bridge — at most it makes a gravestone. The first time I was able to give a task to someone and turn my back, it was as if a clenched muscle let go; somewhere in my spine, a muscle held tense for years, took a breath for the first time.

And our symbol: the butterfly. Because it represents change. Transformation. Emerging from the cocoon. And a truth few people ever mention: becoming a butterfly is not a caterpillar becoming "a slightly better caterpillar"; it is the caterpillar completely melting, dissolving, turning into liquid within itself. Transformation is, first of all, a dissolution. I melted too. In the caravans, in the partings, in the diagnoses, in those three-in-the-mornings — I melted. And now I am solidifying again, this time with wings.

Inner voice: "This is me too. The cocoon has torn open. My wings are unfolding."

A new relationship. A new balance. But this time without losing myself. In the old days, to love was to tear off a piece of myself and offer it to the other person; in the end I'd be left with a handful of an incomplete 'me.' This time I love without putting myself on the table, while sitting at the table.

Inner voice: "I no longer give myself up."

What the Road Gave Me

I became vegan. In spirituality I made a quantum leap. I traveled the world on foot, with my backpack, and in doing so I became grounded. I met cultures and took my place within them. Walking is a strange kind of therapy: every step leaves the previous thought one step behind. The foot meeting the ground, the hardness of the earth, the sound of gravel beneath it, the sweat running down your back — when the body speaks, the head is forced to fall silent. I became most grounded while walking; I came to rest most fully while moving.

This world tour was not only a journey; it was at the same time a research and data-gathering expedition. Australia, Indonesia, Japan, Europe, England, America, New Zealand… At every stop I observed, took notes, compared how neurodivergence is seen across different cultures. I have traveled almost every continent; one remained: Africa. I will go there too, God willing. After Turkey I will continue the Europe and world tour. But first I carried everything I had gathered — both the tangible (methods, data, sources) and the intangible (perspectives, intuitions, the silent wisdom of cultures) — into my own country, and I began to develop it here. Because a bridge is only a bridge if it carries something to both shores.

In New Zealand, I finally found a name for what I am: Takiwātanga — a word the Māori use for autism. Its meaning is this: "in his own time and his own space." Not "broken," not "lacking." His own time, his own space. When I heard it, something that had wandered homeless inside me for decades settled into place — with an audible "clack," just like that lock in Kadıköy. A word. A single word silenced a lifelong "there's something wrong with me" sentence.

Inner voice: "So the problem was never in my pace, but in everyone who set me to someone else's clock. I wasn't late. I was in my own time."

And I learned one more thing: what I couldn't find in Mecca, I found on the road. Yes — I completed my recitation of the Qur'an, I visited the Kaaba, and I did far more than that. Even in the midst of that crowd, circling that black covering, it was not an answer that turned inside me but a question. But I met my faith not in a single sacred place, but in every person who became a mirror to me on the road. In a stranger offering me tea, in a podcast listener who heard out my troubles, in a mother who told me of her own wound. My faith is no longer hard, but clear. Not a faith fed by fear, but a surrender born of compassion. God is no longer a threat to me. A relationship. And in every person there is a face of His.

Inner voice: "Every person I meet brings me closer to God — and brings me closer to myself."

There is one more thing I learned on the road: shamanism is not a religion. Among the Māori I came across the word Takiwātanga — 'in one's own time and space' — coined for autism by language scholar Keri Opai in 2017. I like to imagine that people like us were once the shamans of their communities: far-seeing, intuiting, analyzing…

And I understood: the healers, such as Mevlana, were in my eyes in truth shamans. A person can be a shamanic Muslim, even if that name is no longer spoken; today we simply call him a “healer” (şifacı). Every time I read the Qur'an, it was as if I were reading a new book: I became anew, understood anew, learned anew. Because to enter that depth, one must live, one must grow. (These are my personal belief and interpretation.)

I Am Not Your Healer

And here I must make something clear: I am not your healer. I am not even at ease with the word, because it is God who gives healing — and He needs no intermediary. At most I hold up a light; I illuminate the path, but I cannot walk it for you.

Even when I could do little, I have felt deep empathy for many wounded countries and peoples. Yet I told no one, “Do this, don't do that.” Because I learned that I must first value myself, must respect myself. Where I see injustice, I am bound to do what I can — I lead by example, but I force no one.

That is why I have cut certain brands out of my life. You will not see me beside certain well-known drinks or inside certain stores; there I will not buy or pay for anything — not even for my daughters. If I did so unconsciously, let the pain and the sin remain mine. If I did it consciously — everyone will one day weigh themselves; the mind knows its sin as well as its good deed. I have judged no one; my intention, as far as I know, is clean.

I hate money, because it divides people; it sorts them into categories, levels, ranks. Yet to me everyone is of equal worth — until I see their disrespect, until I glimpse behind their mask. I have come to know thousands of people and hundreds of cultures.

To be someone's mirror is to be water: you see their beauty, but also their ugliness and their darkness. There are those who do not want to see it; and those who know, yet do not want to know. Because of my honesty I have often been very alone — those who could not face themselves drifted away from me after a while.

I perceive, I take in; I can no longer stay silent, no longer play games, no longer wear a mask. I found my own essence and began to live it — that was my most precious medicine. Now I live my essence, understand myself, and am full.

In that fullness I also learned to understand you. I feel with you, I see your inner world; whatever you cast onto me, I cast back, and I cannot stop it. I trust my feelings now: mind, heart, and soul — that is, my feelings — work as one. It would not be wrong to say I live the power by which pure white light splits into its colors through a prism.

I found healing — but I had to walk the path myself; the healing itself was given by God. You, too, will have to walk the path yourselves. However much you flee, hide, or push it away, it only makes you lonelier and more ill, and then it meets you stronger than before. You cannot escape your trials, nor what you are meant to learn.

I believe that here we judge and are judged; and afterward only light awaits you. Then you will understand — here, perhaps, you cannot. Ask, but do not expect every answer here; some answers you will receive not here, but there. (This is my personal belief.)

So be in the now, live the moment. Grow, accept, see both your good and your bad side — and once you have accepted, become the best version of yourself you can be.

The Universe and Movement

"I'm staying," I said. In Turkey. I gave my father's old caravan back, and he gave me a new one as a gift. Small, but great in meaning. Perhaps there had always been a sentence between my father and me that could never be spoken — and that sentence, years later, came into my hand in the shape of a caravan key. Some loves arrive not by word but by wheel. The familiar smell of the old caravan — old engine oil, rusted metal, damp fabric — gave way to the different, raw smell of the new one, a smell with no memory yet attached to it. It was strange that my nose noticed that change: an era had closed, and even its scent had changed.

But something curious happened: the harder I tried to settle, the harder life pushed me onto the road. As if the universe were stubbornly repeating the same lesson to me: "You were not made to stay. You exist to pass through."

Inner voice: "Don't stop. Keep going."

Inner voice: "Wait a second — is this 'keep going' voice the same as my old voice of escape, or is it different? Hold on, let me see. … No. Different. The voice of escape watched my back; this voice points the way ahead. The same words, the opposite direction."

And I understood: my road is not a fixed place. It is movement itself.

★ For some people, putting down roots = moving.

But the story isn't over yet. Because here I am, on the road again. Right now I'm in Germany — to pick up the new caravan. The familiar cool air of the place where I was born—pine and wet asphalt—brushes my face. A new road. A new movement. And this time I am leaving behind not a bridge burning behind me, but a bridge I have built.

Inner voice: "This is only the beginning. The road goes on."
