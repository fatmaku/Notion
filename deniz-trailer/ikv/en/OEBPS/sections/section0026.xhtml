Chapter 8 – Bodily Transformation and the Inner Void

But I wasn’t yet at peace with myself. Before I’d dressed my own wounds, I was running to other people’s. One day I wrote to my mother, because I saw the same thing in her: “Even if you made mistakes, most of them came from your traumas and your loneliness. You tried to bind others’ wounds before you could bind your own. Set free those eyes that haven’t cried in years.” In that moment I understood that this road isn’t only mine — it’s the wound of a generation, maybe of several.

After the first separation, I really looked at myself. For the first time. For years I had been slipping past mirrors with my eyes averted — when I shaved, I looked only at my chin; when I brushed my teeth, only at my mouth; I wanted the rest to stay blurred. That day it was different. I closed the bathroom door, leaned my back against it, and stepped in front of the mirror.

The light was dim. A single bulb in the ceiling; a yellowed, tired light. There was a lime stain in one corner of the mirror, fogged over with steam. It was warm inside; humid, heavy air, the smell of soap and old towels. From outside came the muffled sound of a television — no words I could make out, just that monotone droning.

I looked at the man inside the mirror. I weighed 137 kilos. Heavy. Tired. I could no longer carry myself — and I don't mean that as a metaphor; in the fullest, most physical sense, carrying my own weight was a labor.

My belly sagged, my shirt strained; the fabric between the buttons gaped open and shut with every breath. My shoulders had dropped forward, as if my body were trying to fold in on itself. Sometimes breathing grew hard — especially when I bent over, tying my shoelaces, that invisible stone sitting on my chest. Climbing the stairs, my heart pounded in my ears. At night I couldn't lie on my back; not snoring, but as if something inside were pressing on my windpipe. I looked at myself in the mirror, really looked, and for the first time I didn't lie: "I am not well."

Inner voice: "This isn't me. This body doesn't belong to me. I'm trapped somewhere, somewhere beneath this flesh. Who's going to get me out of here?"

Inner voice (the other voice, harsher): "Nobody's going to get you out. You did this to yourself. A little more with every bite. And now you stand here staring into the mirror, surprised. Bravo."

Two voices. Always two voices. One trying to tear down, one trying to lift up. The one that won that night was, strangely, a third voice — tired but clear: "Alright. Enough. I'm going to do something."

Renovating the Façade

I had the surgery. My stomach was made smaller. Then I began to lose weight. From 137 down to 82. Fifty-five kilos — almost a whole person; as if for years I had carried another body on my back, and one day I set it down on the ground. In those first weeks my body felt foreign to me. My trousers grew loose, I had new holes punched in my belt, and then I changed even that belt. Sitting on the edge of the bed in the mornings, I noticed my spine bending more easily. Climbing the stairs one day, I stopped and was astonished: I wasn't out of breath.

And the world changed. Or rather — the way the world treated me changed. Glances grew different. The attention increased. "Wow, how you've changed," they said; I heard the same sentence dozens of times, always with the same admiration, always with the same little smirk. Some women who once wouldn't have turned to look now smiled at me. At the supermarket, in the elevator, in a café window — eyes lingered on me a second longer now.

Inner voice: "So now I have worth? At 82 kilos? Was I worthless at 137? I'm the same person. The same mind, the same heart, the same past. Only the shell got thinner. So all these smiles — are they for me, or for the shell?"

Inner voice (with bitter humor): "Congratulations, Tuncay. So a person's worth is measured in kilograms after all. I can't understand how science missed that one."

Then I had my teeth done. I learned for the first time what it means not to be ashamed of smiling — I used to laugh with my hand over my mouth, like a reflex. I had a hair transplant. The first time it didn't take; I looked in the mirror and my heart sank — "nothing has changed," I told myself, and even, "it failed again, it's just me again." But I kept fighting. The second time it took.

I went to the gym. I still remember that smell: sweat, iron, rubber flooring, someone in a corner having sprayed deodorant. The clatter of the plates against each other, that dull metal sound of barbells dropped to the floor, the muffled grunt of someone straining on a final rep. Mirrors — mirrors everywhere; wherever I looked, my reflection. The more I pulled against the iron, the more chalk dust on my palms, the burning in my forearms, the ache in my legs the next day as I went down the stairs.

I built muscle. My shoulders broadened, my arms filled out. I learned discipline: getting up every morning and going, fighting the alarm and winning, keeping the promise I'd made to myself. Somewhere inside me a voice said, "see, you can do this after all" — and it was true.

Inner voice: "Now I'm strong. From the outside I look strong." But right after, in a fainter voice: "…look. I only look it. I got stuck again on that thin line between looking and being."

The Silence Within

There was a void inside me. It never left. I had thought I could defeat that too — the way I'd defeated everything else: the weight, the shortness of breath, the alarm. But the void wouldn't step on the scale, couldn't be measured, couldn't be hammered out with iron.

After training I would stand under the shower. The water was hot, running over my worked body, from my shoulders down my back; muscles taut, veins swollen, my body exactly as I'd wanted it. Steam would rise, the glass panes sweating like mirrors. That sweet heaviness of fatigue in my arms. And still — right there, beneath the sound of the water, when I closed my eyes — the void was there. As if right in the center of my chest, a little to the left of my heart, a small black hole. No matter how much muscle I built, I couldn't fill it. And here's the strange thing: the fuller I looked, the more distinct that hole felt to me. Because now I had no excuse left. "It'll get better once I lose the weight," I had said. I lost it. It didn't get better.

I had carried out the world's most expensive renovation. New façade, new roof, fresh paint, a gleaming display window. The neighbors stopped to look, said, "how lovely it's turned out." And I was proud — for a while. But when evening came and the lights went out, when I stepped inside the house and closed the door, I could still hear the water pooled in the basement. That dripping. Drip. Drip. Drip. There for years. No coat of paint silences that sound.

Inner voice: "Why do I still feel like something's missing? Everything looks good from the outside — so why is the void inside the same? Am I really broken? Maybe some people get repaired, and I'm one of the ones who don't."

Inner voice (counterfactual, "what if it had been like this"): "Say I never had the surgery, never lost the weight, I'm still 137. Then would this void not exist? …It would. The very same. Which means the void had nothing to do with the weight at all. I forced the wrong door — and for years, with all my strength."

That thought — the cold, clear awareness of having forced the wrong door for years — was what shook me most. Because the effort hadn't gone to waste, no; but the effort had always flowed outward. I had become a master at repairing the outside. I had never learned how to go inside. I couldn't even find the door handle.

Food Was Once My Enemy

For me, food had once been bound up with fear. Not with love, not with satisfaction — with fear. Because as a child there was a sentence at the table, hanging in the air at every meal: "If you don't finish your plate, your father will kill you." That sentence stayed inside me for years, worked itself into my flesh. While eating I always felt a weight on my shoulders; even raising the fork to my mouth, a state of being on alert. You didn't get up before the plate was empty. Being hungry, being full, wanting, not wanting — none of it counted. A rule was a rule. The body was a debtor, and the plate was the bill.

Years later I understood that I had eaten not from hunger but from fear. My body had never learned to say "enough," because to say "enough" was dangerous. I had no idea where to find a feeling called fullness.

After the surgery, for the first time I was able to leave the plate half-finished. I set down the fork, there was food on the plate, and I stood up. I waited a second — as if an alarm would sound, a voice would shout. Nothing happened. The kitchen was silent. And inside me, for the first time, instead of guilt there was astonishment: so it's possible after all. So the world doesn't fall apart.

Inner voice: "Food is no longer my enemy. Not anymore. And that voice at the table… that voice was never mine. For years I carried someone else's fear in my own stomach."

But the real problem was never the body. It never had been. I had changed — but not really. The façade had changed, the load-bearing columns were the same. Because the problem had never been the body; the body was only the visible translation of something within.

Inner voice: "I never loved myself. That's the truth. No matter how strong I look from the outside, inside there's still that fat, shy, lonely child. Sitting at the edge of the table, praying his plate would never empty. I built him a huge body — but I never once held him."

Outer worth arrived. People looked. They spoke to me. They smiled. But the inner worth was missing, sitting in its old place, unmoving. From the outside: strong, fit, disciplined, "a man who's pulled himself together." From the inside: emptiness, searching, lack, and still that dripping sound from the basement.

Inner voice: "There's more. This isn't all of it. The outer shell changed but the child inside still stands in the same place. And one day I'll have no choice but to sit down beside that child. It won't be the mirror I have to face — but him."

Therapist's Note – Outer Transformation, Inner Void

What is happening here: This is one of the most honest lessons in the book — and really, a lesson that concerns an entire culture. What is being lived here has a clinical name: self-worth tied to external sources (contingent self-worth). A missing inner worth is being papered over with outer change. The body transforms radically, but the mind goes on carrying its old traumas just as they were: that fear at the table, the shame of "finish your plate" carved into the body, and the belief that "I am not enough." The body sheds 55 kilos; that belief doesn't shed a single gram. Because that belief won't step on the scale. In AuDHD, a neurobiological accelerant is added to this: every external success — one kilo less, one muscle more, a compliment, a smiling glance — releases a small hit of dopamine. It feels wonderful for a short while, then drops; the person becomes dependent on the next bit of approval. Meanwhile the hole stays exactly where it was. Researchers call this the "hedonic treadmill": you run and run, but you're always in the same place.

Where it comes from — body and evolution

First, the trace that childhood table left in the body. The "if you don't finish your plate…" threat pairs a small child's most vital need — being fed — with a danger. In Bessel van der Kolk's famous phrase, "the body keeps the score" (The Body Keeps the Score, 2014): years may pass, but the stomach, the shoulders, the breath remember that fear; the tensing of your shoulder as you raise the fork to your mouth isn't "weakness of character," it's an old alarm written into the body. Stephen Porges's Polyvagal Theory explains this: the nervous system, without our awareness, scans the surroundings at every moment for "am I safe?" — this is called neuroception (Porges, 2011). If the table wasn't a safe place in childhood, the body will go on perceiving the table as a battlefield in adulthood too.

So why is the illusion of "I became worthy once I lost the weight" so powerful? Because for tens of thousands of years, the human being survived in the eyes of the group. For our ancestors, being cast out of the tribe meant death; that's why our brain processes the question "how are others looking at me?" as if it were a matter of survival. When we receive social acceptance, dopamine rises in the brain's reward system — this is an ancient wiring that kept our ancestors close to the tribe. In an AuDHD dopamine system this swing is sharper: it spikes high in the moment of a compliment, then drops fast, and the need for a new compliment arises. So the feeling of "a woman smiled, which means I have worth" is not a flaw of character, but the voice of a very old biology.

And then there are mirror neurons and "seeing yourself through the other's eyes." A human being largely constructs the self through the eyes of others; the child "reads" itself in the caregiver's face (Winnicott's "mirroring function"). If you saw constant criticism, fear, or disappointment in that face, the mirror that settles inside you is cracked: whatever the outside says, you always see yourself as "not enough." Even at 82 kilos, that cracked mirror shows the old child.

If left unaddressed — possible bad outcomes

If the belief "Worth = Appearance" is left unworked, a chronic dissatisfaction settles in despite every visible success. The likely course: compulsive exercise edging into addiction, a never-ending spiral of cosmetic interventions ("if I fix just one more thing it'll be complete"), distortions of body image and the re-disordering of eating patterns, a relational ground built anxiously on "am I attractive enough?" Approval dependency: a person enters an endless chase after a feeling that, in principle, cannot be bought from outside — the feeling of "I have worth." In its heaviest form, in the midst of all the external successes, a deep meaninglessness sets in, a collapse into "I put in all this effort, and still I'm empty." Because no matter how hard the wrong door is forced, it won't open.

Paths of healing — several doors

The direction must turn 180 degrees: from outside to inside. There isn't a single way to do this, there are several doors:

• Self-compassion (Kristin Neff): The question, "how would I speak to a good friend today?" Looking at the man in the mirror as you would look at a friend, not an enemy. Neff's research shows that self-compassion is different from, and sturdier than, self-esteem: self-esteem depends on success, while self-compassion stays with you in failure too.
• Compassion Focused Therapy (CFT – Paul Gilbert): Building a "compassionate voice" inside you to counter that harsh, critical voice ("you did this to yourself"). This isn't about fighting, it's about shifting the balance.
• Internal Family Systems (IFS – Richard Schwartz): That "fat, shy, lonely child" is not a source of shame but a part to be protected. IFS teaches you to sit down beside that child, to ask him what he needs. You built up a body; now it's time to hold that child.
• Schema therapy / CBT: Making deep beliefs — schemas — such as "Worth = Weight" and "Food = Danger" visible on paper and rewriting them. A belief can't change until it becomes visible.
• Somatic approaches (Peter Levine – Somatic Experiencing): If that old alarm from the table lives in the body, the solution is in the body too: slow breathing, exercises for noticing fullness, learning where the feeling of "enough" settles in the body. A long exhale stimulates the vagus nerve and pulls the system into "safe" mode (Polyvagal Theory).
• Expressive writing (Pennebaker): Writing a letter to that child. Pennebaker's research shows that putting emotion into writing heals both mood and body (Pennebaker, 1997).
One sentence runs beneath them all: "My worth is independent of my appearance." Once inner worth begins to grow, the outer chase slows of its own accord.

Tuncay's bridge

I took all that surgery, all that iron, all that discipline to be a kind of heroism — and in a way it was; I don't regret it. But years later, reading these concepts, I saw something bitter: I was actually in flight. "The body is broken, if I repair it I'll be fixed," I said, because repairing the body had a prescription — calories, reps, plates. The child inside had no prescription, and what has no prescription is what I always feared. Reading van der Kolk, I understood why that shoulder-tensing at the table was still there — the body had kept the score; I had forgotten, but it hadn't. When I read Neff's self-compassion, I realized I had never been a friend to myself; I'd been my own trainer, my own critic, even my own jailer — but never my own friend. And that question, "if I'd stayed at 137, would the void still be there?" — that counterfactual thought — was a door for me: it would, yes it would, which means the hole had always been inside. In that moment I stopped repairing the outside and, for the first time, dared to look within. Have I fully overcome it? No, not entirely. That child is still inside me. But I no longer run from him; sometimes I sit at the same table with him and leave my plate, without fear, half-finished.

Sources: B. van der Kolk, The Body Keeps the Score (2014); S. Porges, The Polyvagal Theory (2011); K. Neff, Self-Compassion; P. Gilbert, Compassion Focused Therapy; R. Schwartz, Internal Family Systems; J. Pennebaker, research on expressive writing; P. Brickman & D. T. Campbell, studies on hedonic adaptation (the “hedonic treadmill,” 1971).

Where I Stand Today

I've made peace with food — it's no longer my enemy, and I can leave a plate half-finished without my father's voice shouting. I changed my body, and I don't regret it; that journey taught me discipline, keeping my word, living again in my own body. But the real lesson of this chapter I only grasped later, much later, and truly took in: you cannot repair on the outside what is broken on the inside. Even the most expensive façade won't dry the water in the basement. Today I no longer tie my worth to the scale, or the mirror, or to someone looking at me a second too long. That fat, shy child from back then still lives inside me — but I'm no longer ashamed of him. I hold him. The difference between a gleaming, renovated building and a home where someone truly lives, where it's warm, where it's loved — this is exactly that. I spent years renovating the building; now I'm learning to move in.

I do not stop becoming. Every day. Until there is no breath left.
