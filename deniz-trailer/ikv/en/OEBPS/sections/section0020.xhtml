Chapter 6 – First Love, Marriage and Guilt

Love was always a big thing for me. Not something ordinary or everyday, not a "comes and goes" sort of thing — but big, heavy, almost sacred. Because as a child I received so little of it that, when it came, I had no idea how to behave. If you set a plate in front of a child who has gone hungry for years, that child won't just eat from it; he'll guard it, hide it, hunch over it so no one can take it away. I was like that too. When love came, I would cling to it with all my strength — not so much to love, as to keep from losing it. There was a constant voice inside me: "Hold on tight. If you let go, it leaves. Like everything else, it leaves."

Inner voice: Back then I didn't know that loving out of the fear of losing something isn't really loving at all. It's clinging. Like a drowning person grabbing a life buoy. And no life buoy can carry a whole person's weight forever.

The First Bond

I was eighteen. My first serious bond. I got engaged.

I remember that moment — or rather, I remember the warmth it left inside me. For the first time, someone had said "yes" to me. To me alone. For someone who had spent his whole life sitting in the back rows, always found a little too much, always a little wrong, always a little "hard to understand," that "yes" was no simple word. It was a door. I felt something somewhere in my chest loosen; as though I could finally exhale a breath I'd been holding in for years. My shoulders dropped. My jaw unclenched. For the first time, it felt as though I had a place in the world.

Inner voice: "At last I'm not alone. There is someone. Someone sees me. Me — that me inside, the one no one ever saw — she sees it."

But before long I learned: I had been betrayed.

Even writing that sentence, nearly twenty years later, makes the same spot in my chest ache. The outside world hadn't changed at all that day. The sun had still risen, the streets were still crowded, the buses still passed by. Nothing, absolutely nothing, betrayed from the outside what was happening within me. A person's world collapsing while the world couldn't care less — that is the strange loneliness. A heavy, cold stone settled right in the middle of my chest. Not an imaginary stone; a real weight, one that took up space and wouldn't let me breathe. Swallowing became difficult. A knot in my throat, an emptiness in my stomach, a strange trembling in my hands.

At night I would lie in bed staring at the ceiling, and the hours would pass. In the dark you couldn't even see the ceiling; still, I would stare — because the moment I closed my eyes, the same film would start over from the beginning. My heart truly hurt. People say "heartache," and most think it's a metaphor. It isn't. It's a real, physical pain. Behind the breastbone, a concrete pain that tightens, that closes in, one you could point to with a finger. My body was shouting at me a truth my mind couldn't yet accept: You've been left again.

Inner voice: "Wasn't I enough? Wasn't I good enough? Why didn't she choose me? What was lacking in me — what was too much?" And the most poisonous, the most insidious was this question, the one that always returned to the same place: "Or maybe the problem is… me? Is it always me?"

That night, for the first time, I clearly realized: this pain was familiar. It wasn't new. It only had a new face. In truth I had formed this sentence — the "so there must be something wrong with me" sentence — much earlier, when I was much smaller. The betrayal had only confirmed it one more time.

★ The trauma of abandonment razes a person's self-worth to the ground.

Inner voice: What if that "yes" had been real? What if someone, once, just once, had stayed all the way to the end? Maybe then I would never have formed this sentence. Maybe I would never have written this book. Strange — my deepest wounds turned out to be the places where I learned the most. But that night I couldn't have known this. That night I was just eighteen years old, a child staring at the ceiling, carrying a stone in his chest.

Marriage

I married at nineteen. Why? I asked myself this question for years, and each time a different answer came up. But the most honest answer was this: because I wanted to leave. Because I didn't want to sin. Because I wanted to "do the right thing."

Inner voice: "This must be the right way. If I marry, everything will fall into place. I'll have a home, I'll have order, this feeling of being chased inside me will end. Marriage will make me safe."

How young I was. How… hopeful. Inside me a child was preparing to play the heavy role the world had cast for him, the "proper man" — but there had been no rehearsal, the lines were borrowed from others, and the stage was far too big. I didn't marry out of maturity. I married out of two needs to escape at once: away from home and away from guilt. For me, marriage was not a meeting but an exit. Not a door, but that green light under the emergency-exit sign.

Inner voice: Looking back now, I want to laugh — but it's a bitter laugh. Marrying in order to leave home… It's like running from a fire and shutting yourself in another room. You close the door behind you, you say "I'm saved," and then you realize: the smoke came in with you. Because it wasn't the house that was burning. It was you.

A marriage built as an emergency exit is rarely a good idea. One always understands this afterward — long after passing through that door. I approached marriage as if it were a solution, but marriage isn't a solution; it's a mirror. Whatever you carry into it, it magnifies. And I carried into that home an unresolved guilt, an unfilled emptiness, an unnamed loneliness. And that home — the home to which I was faithful, which I never once betrayed — reflected all of it back into my face, growing a little larger each day.

Closeness That Felt Like Danger

Closeness always came to me in two languages. One language was warm, inviting, full of promise; it said, "come, it will be good here." The other was sharp, cautionary, harsh; it said, "step back, this place is dangerous." And the worst part — the two spoke at the same time. When someone drew near me, a door would open inside, light would come in, I would warm up; and at that very moment, somewhere else, deep down, an alarm would sound. This alarm was loud, it came from within, it had no off switch: Stop. Stop. You've come too close. Closeness was not a safe word in the dictionary of my childhood. The people I loved were also the people who could hurt me most — I had learned this not in words but with my body, at a very early age.

Touch, too, was no light thing in my body. For others, when a hand rests on a shoulder, perhaps it's only a hand; for me it was a question mark. When a hand touched my shoulder, sometimes I would fill with love, sometimes with defense — and I couldn't know in advance which would come. My body decided before my mind: it would pull back, tense up, brace itself. My back would stiffen, my shoulder would rise, my breath would stop for an instant. I bore no ill will toward anyone. The person across from me had, most of the time, only wanted to show their love. But my body took every possibility seriously. Because my body had never once been joking. My body still remembered the things my mind wanted to believe it had long forgotten.

Inner voice: Two voices were always fighting inside me. One would say: "Come closer, life is here. Don't be afraid. This time it's different." The other would answer at once: "If you come closer, you'll get hurt. Like always. Have you forgotten?" And I would stay in the middle — one foot inside, one foot ready to flee. And each time I would notice the same thing: loneliness was familiar, closeness uncertain. A person more often prefers the familiar pain to the unknown healing. Because familiar pain is, at least, a pain whose outcome you know.

★ Loneliness was familiar, closeness uncertain — and fear, more often than not, chooses the known evil over the unknown good.

Sexuality and Guilt

My first sexual experience gave rise to deep feelings of guilt. That moment, which for someone else might have been a discovery, a coming together, even a joy, set up a tribunal in me. Because what I had learned was this: "Not before marriage." "It's shameful." "It's a sin." These sentences hadn't been said to me just once; over years, through veiled glances, through timid silences, through all the capillaries of a devout upbringing, they had worked their way into me. At the same time I was curious. My body wanted — because the body is designed to want. My mind said "wrong." There was a war inside me, and both sides were me.

Inner voice: "Is this wrong? Or is it normal? Is everyone like this, or am I the only one who's broken? Why am I fighting myself so much? Why does a punishment come right after a pleasure — who set down this punishment, and when, and how did I get bound to a contract I never signed?"

Even masturbation was something I hid as a child, something I was ashamed of. At night, in my room, silently, in the dark. And right after — almost at the very same moment — the guilt would come. As if there were an eye in the ceiling, as if someone were watching me, as if even in my most private moment I was not alone but being judged. That invisible gaze didn't let go of me for years. I had mistranslated my own body, the way one mistranslates a sentence: Desire = danger. Pleasure = sin. Closeness = loss. I didn't write this equation; I was taught it, and I was not old enough to object. Yet the body was never the enemy. It was only speaking a language I had been forbidden to hear — and I had learned to treat my own mother tongue as a crime.

Inner voice: What if they had told me, "the body is beautiful, desire is human, pleasure is not a sin"? What if that invisible eye had never existed at all? Maybe then getting into bed would have been a relief, not a tribunal. But my own parents had only taught me what they themselves had been taught — they too were a link in a chain. I no longer blame anyone. I only see the chain. And seeing is the first step to breaking it.

Years later, I had a wife. I was faithful — I never cheated, not even once. This wasn't a matter of pride for me but a matter of identity; betrayal was the name of my deepest wound, and I had forbidden myself to open that wound in anyone. But between us there was an emotional indifference. In the same house, the same bed, at the same table — and still, miles of distance between us. A great emptiness was growing inside me. Sometimes it felt as though someone were punching me right in the middle of my stomach; that real, physical sense of emptiness, a hunger that resembled hunger but couldn't be filled by food.

Inner voice: "Am I the one who's wrong? Why isn't it enough? Others are happy with this much, so why can't I be? Why do I always want more — something deeper, more real, more naked?"

And, as always, I would look for the way out in the same place: in myself, as the guilty one. "I want too much." "I'm the one who's wrong." "I'm insatiable." "I don't deserve to be loved." I blamed myself so much that blame turned into a habit, almost a comfort. Blaming myself gave me, at least, the illusion that the situation was under my control: if I was the problem, then I was also the solution. But I wasn't the problem. The problem was the sentence I had never been taught: To need is not a deficiency.

Surrender, Tantra and the Wrong Target

I didn't merely love. I surrendered myself. There are mountains of difference between these two, but learning that took years of my life. Watching the other person, reading the smallest shadow on their face, making them happy felt good to me — or rather, it felt as though I were filling my own emptiness with their happiness. Their smile was my oxygen. If they were in good spirits, it meant I was "good enough."

Inner voice: "Just looking at them is enough. As long as they're happy. What I want doesn't matter — I don't even fully know what I want anyway. I want their happiness. Yes. That's enough. That must be it."

But the truth was this, and it's hard to admit: I was living the other person, not myself. I had a life, but I wasn't in it; I was the spectator, the servant, the décor of someone else's life. At some point a doubt fell into me — sharp, unsettling, unrelenting: "Could it be that I want too much sex? Could the problem be in my desire?" This question wouldn't leave me in peace.

I searched for an answer. And I searched in my own particular way — the way few others would, by opening files, researching, traveling to three different countries. After my second divorce I took Tantra trainings in three different countries. Love, bond, energy, touch — I went to learn what these were not from books but from the body. And I learned something surprising, one of the great turning points of my life: it was never about sex at all.

Inner voice: "Is it really this simple? For years I judged myself as 'insatiable,' 'wanting too much,' 'wrong' — and the thing I wanted wasn't even sex? I only wanted to be seen and to become whole. Not with another body inside a body — but with another soul beside a soul."

I wanted to unite. To truly merge — body and soul at once, the two never separating. I didn't want short things: a one-night consolation, a momentary proof. I wanted depth. I wanted continuity. Not half, but whole. I wanted to be able to exist with a person without losing myself. But this was the mistake I made for years, and Tantra showed it to me with a bitter clarity:

★ I was trying to live the right things with the wrong people.

With the right thirst, but always running to dry wells.

Lovebombing and a Theory of Narcissism

Later I learned a word: lovebombing. The moment you name it, what you've been living turns from a riddle into a pattern — and seeing the pattern is the beginning of getting out of it. First comes an intense love; so intense that everything you've hungered for your whole life pours over you at once, like a flood. Attention, admiration, "you're special," "I've always been looking for you." An addictive beginning. Then — suddenly, for no reason, without explanation — a withdrawal. Coldness. Distance. And you, having once tasted that flood, what do you do? You give even more. You try even harder. You make yourself even smaller for them to come back.

Inner voice: "Maybe they'll come back… Maybe this time if I give enough, they'll stay. Maybe the fault is mine, maybe if I fix it… One more chance. One more chance." And that "one more chance" sentence devoured years of my life.

Inner voice: Now I understand that the urge to "give even more" was no coincidence. It was a reflex learned in childhood: "If love is withdrawing, then I wasn't good enough — I must try harder." Lovebombing touched precisely this old wound, with a surgeon's precision. As if there were a lock inside me and they knew the key. Most of the time they weren't even conscious of it — they too were speaking the language of their own wounds.

And here, after years of observation, something I came to call my own theory formed inside me. Not an expert's laboratory theory — a theory a life tested in its own flesh: some narcissists are not born without empathy. The empathy either never developed, or it was once there but closed off by a trauma — just as a hand pulls back when it touches fire, that person too, somewhere, having once been burned too badly, locked the capacity to feel from the inside.

★ My personal interpretation: in my own relationships, trauma, neurodivergence, and protective defenses sometimes formed patterns that could look narcissistic or emotionally closed. This is not a diagnosis of neurodivergent people or anyone else; it is simply how I make sense of my own experience.

Inner voice: In saying this I'm not letting them off the hook — what they put me through was real, the pain was real. But understanding is a different thing from forgiving. I wanted to understand, because understanding lifted me out of victimhood. The question "why did they do this to me?" diminishes a person; the question "what door inside them closed?" sets a person back on the path of seeing again. And seeing is the only way not to fall into the trap again.

The outcome was the same every time, repeating like a refrain: I always gave. I opened my palms, opened my insides, opened my door. But in return I received almost nothing. And the more I gave, the emptier I grew, and the emptier I grew, the more I gave — because I thought I could fill the emptiness by giving. Yet I was carrying water to a leaking bucket.

Inner voice: "Do I not deserve to be loved? Or is there no such thing as love at all — is everyone really just trading something, and I was the last to notice? Or… or is the problem that I always knock on the wrong door? The right door exists, but because I'm always so hungry, I run to the first one that opens."

Therapist's Note – Trauma Bonding, Sexual Guilt and Dependent Love

What is happening here: In this chapter three materials combine to form a stubborn pattern that works almost like a law of nature. One: early emotional neglect — a childhood in which love came scarcely and unpredictably. Two: a religious-restrictive sexual upbringing that ties pleasure to sin and desire to guilt. Three: the intense impulsivity, hunger for depth and "all or nothing" perception of AuDHD. Out of these three the brain produces an unquestionable equation: Love = Pain + Guilt. And here's the insidious part: lovebombing cycles don't break this pattern, on the contrary, they reinforce it; because familiar pain feels "safe" to the nervous system. The system confuses familiarity with peace — what we take for calm is, most of the time, merely the familiar.

Where it comes from — body and evolution

The answer to the question "why do we attach so strongly to familiar pain?" lies not in logic but in the depths of the nervous system. John Bowlby and Mary Ainsworth's attachment theory (Bowlby, Attachment, 1969; Ainsworth's "Strange Situation" experiments, 1978) shows that for an infant, staying close to the caregiver is a matter of life and death; that is why the infant attaches to the caregiver even if that caregiver is inconsistent or hurtful — because not attaching is, in evolutionary terms, death. A child who grows up in an environment where love comes with pain cannot separate the two; as an adult, he recognizes the "warmth + danger" mixture as "home." This is called insecure/anxious attachment, and it explains exactly why lovebombing is so effective: that cycle replays, beat for beat, the "come close–go away" rhythm of childhood.

The tensing of the body in closeness, the rising of the shoulder, the freezing at a touch — these are not weakness of character but what Stephen Porges, in his Polyvagal Theory, calls "neuroception" (Porges, The Polyvagal Theory, 2011): the body, before we are even aware of it, scans every contact for "is this safe, or dangerous?" In a nervous system that has lived through trauma, this scanning is hypersensitive; even a loving hand can trigger the alarm. In Bessel van der Kolk's famous phrase, "the body keeps the score" (The Body Keeps the Score, 2014) — the body still remembers and guards what the mind believes it has long forgiven.

Sexual guilt, too, has an address. The shame that kicks in at the moment of pleasure fires the brain's threat and punishment system (the amygdala and related circuits); when pleasure and punishment signals arrive at once, the brain pairs desire with danger. This is a merciless form of classical conditioning — like Pavlov's bell, except this time the bell is your own body. Add to that the dopamine system of AuDHD: the search for depth and intensity is not "insatiability" but the reward system seeking a stronger stimulus; what you call "I want too much" is, most of the time, not a flaw but a neurological signature.

If left unaddressed — possible bad outcomes

If this pattern is not recognized and worked on, it repeats itself for a lifetime: always the same type of wrong partner choice (because healthy love feels "boring/foreign," while chaotic love feels "passionate/familiar"). A complete loss of self in relationships — where you end and the other begins becomes blurred. Relationship addiction (codependency). Recurring, chronic depressive episodes. Burnout in the perpetual "giver" role, and a silent anger building up beneath that burnout. A person becomes a master at filling other people's emptiness, a novice at feeling his own — and the day he is left alone with his own emptiness, he won't know whom he'll find inside. The question "do I deserve love?" turns into an open wound that won't close, that festers; every new "wrong person" can press a finger into it.

Paths of healing — several doors

There is no single prescription; but there are several doors that complement one another:

• Naming the needs (linguistic clarity): The first liberation is linguistic. "Sex is not the same thing as emotional merging." Separating the two needs and calling them by name maps the chaos within. What has a name becomes manageable.
• Internal Family Systems (IFS – Richard Schwartz): Your "inner voices" — the one that wants to come close, the one that wants to pull back, the one that blames, the one that protects — are not enemies; each is a "part" that once protected you. Rather than banishing the part that says "give more," talking with it: "Why are you trying to protect me?"
• Somatic Experiencing (Peter Levine) and body-based work: Trauma bonds and the touch alarm cannot be solved with logic; they can only be retrained with the body. The slow re-teaching of safe contact to the nervous system, in small doses.
• EMDR and trauma-focused therapies: Tracing ingrained beliefs like "no one chooses me / I don't deserve it" back to the moments they were first wounded, and reprocessing them.
• Compassion Focused Therapy (Paul Gilbert) and self-compassion (Kristin Neff): "I'm not wrong, I'm just wounded." Learning to answer your inner critic the way you'd speak to a friend.
• The practice of setting boundaries: For someone who has learned to dissolve himself, saying "no" is a training program in itself. Every small "no" is a signature of writing yourself back onto the list.
• Expressive writing (James Pennebaker): Pouring guilt and shame onto paper instead of turning them over in your head; research shows this improves mood and even immunity. This book, too, is in a way the fruit of that therapy.

The shared aim of all these doors is the same: not to love less — but at last to add yourself to the list of the ones you love.

Tuncay's bridge

I lived this pattern for years without knowing its name — then, the more I read, the more trainings I attended, I said, "so this thing has a name," and each name let me breathe a little more. When I understood Bowlby, I grasped why I always attached most strongly to the people who hurt me most; this wasn't stupidity, it was the survival strategy of an eighteen-month-old infant still running in my adult self. When I learned the word lovebombing, a pattern in my life suddenly lit up — and I have to be honest: learning that word gave me faster relief than years of therapy had, because now I was dealing not with a riddle but with a mechanism. The Tantra trainings I took in three countries showed me something I could never have learned from books: what I was searching for wasn't sex but union — and once I knew this difference, I acquitted myself of that "insatiability" verdict I'd condemned myself with for years. As for sexual guilt — I couldn't solve it with logic; that guilt didn't sit in the mind but in the body, and I could only soothe it with the body, slowly, patiently. What I did was always the same: take the science and spread it over my own life like a map. That map turned the sentence "I'm insatiable, I'm wrong, I'm broken" into the sentence "I am an explainable system, and systems can relearn."

Where I Stand Today

This is the part of my life I've worked on the longest — and let me say it honestly: it hasn't fully closed, but it has been understood. I now recognize lovebombing not ten years later but as it's happening; when that flood pours over me, a small voice inside says, "slow down — we've seen this before." When familiar pain feels "like home," I know it's lying; that house burned down, I got out, I don't have to go back there. I didn't only learn the sentence "sex ≠ union" — I lived it, in three countries, in my own body. What I've overcome is this: I no longer put everything I have, myself included, on the table to prove I deserve love. What I'm still working on: being able to stay there when closeness is good — instead of pulling back because the good thing feels unfamiliar. This is the hardest lesson of my life; because they programmed me not to stay in goodness but to flee from harm. But I'm on the way. And this time, most importantly: I'm taking myself along with me. There's one more name on the list now — mine.

"Your task is not to seek for love, but merely to seek and find all the barriers within yourself that you have built against it."

— Rumi

I will never be complete, only a little more myself each day — to the end.
