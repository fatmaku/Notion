Chapter 10 – Loss: What Remains When Something Is Gone

Then the second marriage ended too. A separation doesn’t happen in a single day, no one tells you that; it happens again every day. Peace in one message, war in the next. “You unsettle me; instead of looking after them, I argue with you and my nerves are shot,” she said. And I wrote, “Why am I covering your faults if I won’t get to see my children anyway,” and then the same sentence again, and again — that’s how it is when I fixate, I send the same pain over and over. Most of all, one fear burned in me: not being able to see my children. That’s a father’s greatest fear — becoming a visitor in your child’s life, someone unseen.

Those days were bilingual. A sentence would begin in Turkish and end in German. “Du bist der Vertragspartner, deshalb müsstest du anrufen.” “I made the children a dentist appointment.” “Don’t forget their medication time.” Being a father from a distance means chasing every small detail from behind a screen. She told me, “All three of us are sick, I deal with everyone, and you wander around without a care in the world”; “stop putting these burdens on me, so I can be a healthy mother.” Maybe she was right. Once she said, “I don’t want my children to live through what you lived through in your childhood” — that sentence was both true and went straight into my heart. All I could say was, “May God grant healing, I miss my lambs.”

And yet I laughed. Even in the most tense moment I’d find a laugh. In the middle of a crisis I’d see some gesture of my daughter’s and laugh. Even she, one day, in the middle of the harshest argument, wrote: “Just kidding, so funny”; for a moment we both laughed. Because if you can still laugh, you’re not finished yet. Humor wasn’t an escape for me; for years it was the only tool that kept one of us — me and my own head — from slamming the door and walking out. When I read the sentence “The children played happily today,” all the tension would pause for a second. Because it was always the same question: are they happy? On the day I wrote “My honey girl has grown so much,” behind that sentence stood all the days a father had missed. Time always flows strangely with me; either very slowly or all at once. Yesterday she was in my arms, today she’s a big girl. No medical term eases the pain of the time a father has missed.

There is a kind of loss that is louder than anything else — and it makes no sound at all.

It was morning. One of those mornings. The light slipping through the gap in the curtain had fallen into the middle of the room like a thin, slanted blade; dust motes turned slowly inside that light, motes in no hurry, with nowhere to go. I opened my eyes and saw the ceiling. Then the wall. Then the sound of my own breathing. Everything was in its place. And yet something was missing — an absence I couldn't name, but that my body had noticed before I did.

For a long time I couldn't name what I had lost. It wasn't just a person. Nor an object. And it certainly wasn't a dream. It was more like… a possibility of continuing. After the second separation I would wake in the morning and life was still in the room — but it no longer looked at me. I was there, but life had stepped one pace back. No one had died. And yet something inside me no longer wanted to live.

In the middle of my chest, just beneath the ribs, there was a weight like stone. It didn't hurt — I wish it had, for pain at least has a location, you can press your finger to it and say "right here." This was not like that. This was like the silence inside a room: wherever you look, there it is, but you cannot hold it. I put the kettle on. I listened to the water come to a boil. That thin whistle rose, reached its peak, then slowly faded as I turned off the stove. And there, in the middle of the kitchen, cup in hand, I felt that whistle die out inside myself.

Inner voice: Come on. No one died. Pull yourself together. Millions of people are going through worse than this. — Yes, I know. But saying "there's worse out there" doesn't mean this isn't happening. A bigger wound beside yours doesn't heal yours. It just becomes an excuse for your silence.

Silent Grief

People think grieving means crying. I fell silent. My smiles grew thinner. My sentences grew shorter. My shoulders sank a little further inward. No one noticed this. Because I was already cast in the role of the one "who copes."

For me, grief came not as sound but as a loss of warmth. It was as if I dropped by one degree — too little to be seen from the outside, but as clear as every one of my cells knew it to be. You pick up your phone, you're about to write to someone, the screen lights up, your finger hovers over the keyboard — and you can't find what to say. "How are you?" they ask. "I'm fine," you say. Two words a lie, the rest exhaustion. And the strangest part: you say that "I'm fine" so many times that after a while you start to believe it yourself. Or to pretend to believe it. You're too tired to tell the difference between the two.

Inner voice: "Handle this one too. Carry this one alone too."

Inner voice: When did I take on this role? At what age did someone tell me "you're strong, you can manage" so that I never once let go of that sentence again? Because at first that sentence felt like a medal. Then I realized it was a burden. Now I understand — it was a suit of armor bolted onto me. Protecting me, but never letting me breathe.

Every loss piled onto the one before. Like stones stacked one atop another without ever touching. Silent. Heavy. One more stone, one more stone. None of them a mountain on its own. But all of them together, a mountain sitting on your chest the morning you wake.

As If Cast Out of My Own Home

For me, loss was not the ending of something. It was like being cast out from somewhere. As if the key to a room I had been living in had been taken from me, and I was still waiting in front of the door. Knowing full well it would never open.

I could see that door before my eyes. Wooden, old, with a knocker I too had touched. In my dream I reach out my hand — and the lock won't turn. I turn it and turn it, metal grinding against metal, that muffled "click" comes but it doesn't open. Inside, the light is on. Inside, there is life. Out here, in the cold, I am a stranger on the threshold of my own home. It was in that moment that I thought, for the first time: "Maybe some people just don't belong anywhere."

Inner voice: What if that sentence is true? What if I really am someone who can never belong anywhere? — Stop. That's exhaustion talking. Saying "I don't belong" is just "right now this hurts so much" in disguise. Pain always whispers the worst sentences as if they were the truest. You don't have to believe it.

But in that moment I believed it. Because pain speaks louder than reason. And waiting outside, on the threshold, I was doing a small reckoning with myself: "What if I had acted differently? What if I hadn't said that sentence, what if I had been there that day, what if I hadn't gone silent, what if I hadn't spoken…" Dozens of "what ifs." Each one like trying a different key in the lock of a door that will never open. And none of them open it. Because that door now opened onto someone else's home.

Crashing Into Yourself

When a person loses, they don't immediately look to someone else. First they crash into themselves. I crashed too. And at full speed, like a bird hitting a windshield — it was an invisible glass; I didn't know it existed until I struck it.

Inner voice: "Was I lacking? Was I too much? Did I stand in the wrong place? Or was I always wrong?"

These questions came mostly at night. When I turned off the light and lay down in bed, just as my body was getting ready to rest, my mind began its shift. It would replay the same scene — like a tape, rewinding it over and over. The same moment, the same look, the same closing door. Each time I begged the scene, "End it differently this time." It didn't end. It always stopped at the same place.

These questions no longer wanted answers. They just wandered inside me — like children who have lost their way inside a house. Going from door to door, unable to get out of any of them. And for the first time I understood: some losses cannot be passed through without facing yourself.

★ When a door closes, the first wall you crash into is always yourself.

Being Near and Unable to Touch

And then there is a kind of loss that happens not by leaving, but by staying. Sometimes you stay, but you can't keep up. You hear their voices, you know their laughter, but you remain outside the rhythm. That's how it was between my daughters and me. My losing came not with a departure but with a slowing. As life sped up three steps, I stopped somewhere.

I felt this most in the small things. In that emptiness where one day you notice they've grown, but you couldn't watch them grow. Yesterday they pronounced a word wrong, today they say it right — and I wasn't on the bridge between that "yesterday" and "today." For a parent, the most ordinary thing: some silly school story a child tells over dinner. For me, a treasure. And most of the time I heard that treasure secondhand, delayed, told by someone else.

Guilt doesn't shout. It whispers: "You could have done more." "You should have been there more." "This is your fault." I had known these sentences since childhood. But this time the target was clear. To be a father was to feel every moment you lost twice over.

Inner voice: What if I had never set out? What if I had stayed, gritted my teeth, been there in spite of everything? — Maybe I would have been there. But what kind of "there"? A broken, burned-out, hollowed-out there. A child also senses a father who is physically present but spiritually gone. Maybe this is the hardest truth: sometimes "staying" is a loss too, just one without a name.

When I was far from them, my mind was always with them. Which game they loved, which words they had just learned, which fears they were growing up with. It hurt that they were growing up without me beside them. But there was something I didn't know: the further I moved away from them, the more room they took up inside me. Every day they weren't before my eyes, they opened one more room in my heart. Distance didn't lessen the love — it concentrated it, distilled it, turned it into something sharper and more aching.

Inner voice: "I'm not only a father. I have to remain a human being too. If I use myself up completely, I can't be a good example to anyone."

This realization was painful. But it was steadying. Not everything gets completed. Some stories stay unfinished. But being unfinished is not the same as being a lie. I grieved the time I couldn't give them. I am grieving it. But there's something I keep giving them: reality. Without deceiving them, without denying myself, without paying out love like a debt.

★ The greatest gift you can give a child is not a perfect father, but a real one.

What the Emptiness Taught

After the loss, something changed. I no longer wanted to cling. I no longer wanted to force. I no longer wanted to carry. The emptiness, painful as it was, was honest.

At first I fled from that emptiness. I tried to fill it — with noise, with work, with the phone screen, with whatever I could find. Silence felt like an accusation to me; every hush rang in my ear, "see, there you are, alone again." But one day, somehow, I grew tired of running. I sat down. I sat down right in the middle of the emptiness and looked it in the eye. And something strange happened: it didn't swallow me. It just stood there, with me. For the first time I heard clearly:

★ Some things do not choose you. This is not your worthlessness.

And that sentence settled into a small but solid place inside me. What remains after losing? It's different for everyone. For me, this remained: slowing down. Less explaining. More feeling. And one piece of knowledge: losing sometimes severs the last bond that keeps you from yourself. It severed mine. And right there, something I never expected happened: silence, for the first time, was not my enemy.

That first night, I breathed inside the silence. I truly breathed — long, deep, all the way to the end. And as I let the breath out, I felt that stone, that stone on my chest, grow lighter by a single gram. Just one gram. But even one gram is a beginning, for someone being crushed beneath a mountain.

But my bond with my children didn't break. It only changed shape. In a glance, in a message, in a reunion one day. I don't know when, or how. But this I know: some bonds aren't measured by distance. And this story is still being written.

Therapist's Note – Loss, Silent Grief, and Unspoken Guilt

What is happening here: Not every grief looks like grief. There's the loud, weeping kind — and there's the silent kind, the one that shows itself through silence: the thinning smile, the shortening sentences, the shoulders sinking inward. In people who took on the role of "the one who copes" early in life, it is almost always the silent kind at work, because collapsing was never a permitted option. On top of this comes a particularly stubborn form: an "ambiguous" grief for children — they are alive, they are reachable, and yet they are absent. There is no farewell, no ritual, no clear point of ending. That is why this grief does not end on its own. In psychology this is also called a quiet, unrecognized loss: there is no funeral, no grave, no socially recognized loss; so those around you look on and ask "what is there to be sad about?" and the grieving person is forced to hide their pain. This is called disenfranchised grief (Kenneth Doka, 1989), and it is one of the loneliest kinds of grief there is.

Where it comes from — body and evolution

There is a very old answer to why grief settles in the body, in the chest, in the stomach. The human being is a creature whose species' survival depends on forming bonds. John Bowlby's attachment theory (Attachment, 1969) describes exactly this: the infant cries, searches, and clings in order to stay physically close to its caregiver — because in our ancestors' world, closeness meant survival, and separation meant the risk of death. That is why, when a bond breaks, the brain reads it not as simple sadness but as danger. In Mary Ainsworth's "strange situation" experiments, the panic response in the body of an infant separated from its mother is the raw form of our adult grief. It's no coincidence that you feel "as if cast out of your own home" — for your brain, breaking from a bond truly is being torn from your homeland.

The bodily weight of grief is real too. As Bessel van der Kolk put it, "the body keeps the score" (The Body Keeps the Score, 2014): unprocessed loss accumulates in the shoulders, the jaw, the chest; because the brain remembers threat not with logic but with the body. According to Stephen Porges's polyvagal theory, when we break from someone we love, our nervous system can drop out of "safe bond" mode and into a kind of freeze/withdrawal mode (Porges, 2011) — what you call "dropping by one degree," "a loss of warmth," is the very withdrawal of the vagus nerve. And that scene replaying over and over at night: the overactivity of the brain's default mode network, that is, rumination (Nolen-Hoeksema, 1991) — the mind, trying to solve a loss it cannot solve, looping the same tape endlessly.

The whisper of guilt has a root too. The voice saying "you could have done more" is, more often than not, an inner-voice pattern learned in childhood. In people who experienced emotional neglect (Jonice Webb, Running on Empty, 2012), the finger automatically turns inward at the moment of loss — because the message "you're always the one responsible" was internalized early in life.

If left unaddressed — possible bad outcomes

Stones stacked one atop another without ever touching: when losses are merely "handled" instead of grieved, at some point they turn into a baseline weight that surfaces as burnout, loss of meaning, or depression. What grief researchers call complicated (prolonged) grief can develop: months chase years but the pain doesn't soften, it freezes. The whispering guilt ("you could have done more") becomes a constant background hum, gnawing at self-worth from the inside. The sentence "maybe I don't belong anywhere" can harden into an isolating way of moving through life — social withdrawal, the expectation in relationships that "I'll be abandoned anyway," disrupted sleep and appetite. In the most severe course, unresolved grief can lead all the way to a sense of meaninglessness and the dimming of the will to live. That is why finding a witness to your grief is not a luxury but a need.

Paths of healing — not a single road, but several doors

There is no single right way to grieve, but there are several doors that work:

• Expressive writing (James Pennebaker): Grief needs a witness — and that witness can even be a journal. Pennebaker's research shows that putting a loss into writing for 15–20 minutes a day improves mood and even immunity (1997). The whispering guilt must be said aloud or written on paper, because once spoken it loses its power.
• Internal Family Systems (IFS – Richard Schwartz): Seeing that voice asking "was I lacking, was I too much?" not as an enemy but as a wounded "part" trying to protect you. Instead of getting angry at that part, sitting beside it and asking "what are you afraid of?"
• Somatic Experiencing (Peter Levine) and body work: That stone sitting in the chest is dissolved not by logic but only by the body. Planting your feet on the ground, long exhalations (which stimulate the vagus nerve and draw the system into "safe" mode), allowing bodily releases like trembling or rocking.
• EMDR and narrative therapy: Reprocessing that scene that plays over and over and turning the story of the loss from a "I was inadequate" narrative into a "I did what I could under hard conditions" narrative.
• Compassion-Focused Therapy (Paul Gilbert) and self-compassion (Kristin Neff): Answering the judging voice inside you with compassion, as if speaking to your own child.
• The ambiguous loss framework (Pauline Boss): Boss's most powerful suggestion is this — stop seeking a clear closure, learn to live with the reality of "both present and absent." Keeping the bond open despite the distance is more healing than forcing a closure.

Tuncay's bridge

I didn't get over this loss like a head cold — I learned to live with it. When I read Pauline Boss's phrase "ambiguous loss," something in me eased in a strange way: so this nameless weight had a name after all, so I wasn't going mad after all, so the feeling that "no one died but something inside me did" was the description of something real. When I read Bowlby, I understood that my sense of being "cast out of my own home" was not a flaw of character but the human species' tens-of-thousands-of-years-old attachment software — and I got less angry at myself. Van der Kolk's "the body keeps the score" explained why that stone was there in my chest; instead of brooding over it, I learned to breathe out. It helped most of all in the part about my daughters: when I understood that I could be a father by giving reality rather than by paying off guilt, that whispering voice of guilt agreed, for the first time, to fall a little quiet. What I did was take the science and spread it like a map over my own loss — and that map turned the sentence "I'm the worthless man who was abandoned" into the sentence "I'm a father who keeps loving, imperfect but real."

Where I Stand Today

I didn't "get over" this loss like a head cold — I learned to live with it, which is a different and more honest thing. What truly changed: silence is no longer my enemy. It used to be proof of my worthlessness, today it's a space where I can breathe. I no longer merely believe the sentence "some things do not choose you, this is not your worthlessness" — I feel it. And I keep my bond with my daughters open without clutching it tight. I stopped waiting for a closed door to open again, and started truly living where I actually stand. The stone in my chest is still there — I won't lie. But it's no longer on top of me, it's beside me. I'm not carrying it; I'm walking with it. And the difference between the two is a whole life.

"Let everything happen to you: beauty and terror. Just keep going. No feeling is final."

— Rainer Maria Rilke

I am becoming, so I am still on the way. Day by day, until my last breath.
