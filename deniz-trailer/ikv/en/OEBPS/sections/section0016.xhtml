Therapist's Note – Fear-Based Attachment

What is happening here: When a parent is at once the source of both safety and threat, the child's attachment system is plunged into an unsolvable paradox. The work of John Bowlby, the founder of attachment theory, and his student Mary Ainsworth shows that a child is biologically programmed to take refuge in the parent in order to survive — protection is expected to be there. But if the danger is right there too, in the very same person, then the harbor one would flee to is also the source of the storm. The attachment researcher Mary Main calls this "fright without solution": neither approaching nor withdrawing works for the child, because the thing he fears is the same as the thing he wants to take refuge in. The result is traumatic learning: the body binds a completely neutral stimulus on its own — the "click" of the key — to full alarm. Love and fear fuse into a single inseparable emotion and stick to the same face. "To both love him and fear him" is not a contradiction; it is the mathematically necessary outcome of this equation.

Where it comes from — body and evolution

There is a very old explanation for why your heart starts running at that "click." Deep in the brain, the amygdala is the alarm bell that has kept us alive for millions of years; Bessel van der Kolk calls it "the brain's smoke detector" (The Body Keeps the Score, 2014). This detector does not reason, it works fast: the instant it hears a sound it has associated with danger, it sets the body in motion before you can think. Your chest pounding at the sound of the key, your palms sweating, that stone settling into your stomach — all of it is the bodily signature of the amygdala's "flee or freeze" command.

Stephen Porges's Polyvagal Theory takes this a step further: our body, without our awareness, scans the environment moment by moment, asking "am I safe?"; Porges calls this neuroception (Porges, 2011). A loving gaze draws the nervous system into "safe" mode, while a cold and threatening tone pulls it into "defense" mode. For years your body learned to read, from your father's footsteps and the tone of his "hello," whether that evening would be safe — this was not "oversensitivity" but a radar working flawlessly to keep you alive. Just as in Pavlov's famous experiment (the bell–saliva conditioning), your nervous system too paired the "click" with danger; this is classical conditioning, not a weakness of character. And as the title of van der Kolk's book says, the body keeps the score: even when that fear is erased from consciousness, your muscles, your stomach, and your heart go on remembering it for years.

If left unaddressed — possible bad outcomes

If this bond is left unprocessed, the brain permanently encodes the following equation: "Closeness = Danger." In adulthood this often steers a life silently, unnoticed. The likely course: disproportionate fear before authority figures and a swallowing of one's own voice; pulling back precisely when real closeness is about to form (because closeness triggers the alarm); great difficulty expressing emotions, especially love; either an excessive need for control or, on the contrary, an erasing of the self. The most insidious of all is this: a person, without realizing it, chooses partners who recreate that hot-cold oscillation he knew in childhood, because he finds them "familiar"; instead of an unknown peace, he mistakes the known unrest for safety. The familiar feels more comfortable than the healthy — and the cycle repeats itself. (Spoiler: this is exactly what happens in Chapter 6.)

Paths of healing — not one road, but several doors

Here is the good news: if "Closeness = Danger" is a learned bond, then, like everything learned, it can be rewritten. There are several doors:

• First, noticing — without shame: Catching the automatic fear response like an observer. "Aha — the old fire alarm went off, but there's no fire." This single sentence separates the response from your identity.
• Internal Family Systems (IFS – Richard Schwartz): Seeing that trembling child inside you, that part trying to make itself invisible, not as an enemy but as a "part" that protected you for years; turning toward it with compassion. Those inner voices in your head are precisely the working ground of IFS.
• Somatic Experiencing (Peter Levine) and EMDR: Trauma is resolved not with logic but with the body. Slowly discharging that frozen "flee" energy stuck in the body; EMDR, for its part, is a powerful method for reducing the emotional charge of an old memory.
• "Inner parent" work and Compassion Focused Therapy (Paul Gilbert – CFT): The secure bond that was missing back then, a person today gives to himself — by developing a soothing, dependable, compassionate inner voice. Learning to answer the sentence "I am wrong" the way you'd speak to a friend.
• A very concrete repair: Consciously, once, saying to someone you trust, "I feel safe with you." This sentence is not just words in the air; it is a direct correction made to the nervous system — it offers the body new evidence that "closeness was safe this time." If you repeat it enough, "Closeness = Danger" is slowly rewritten as "Closeness = Safe."

Tuncay's bridge

For years I didn't know why that "click" took over my body; I just thought it was a "weakness," and I was ashamed of it. When I read Porges, I felt relieved: it turned out that my freezing at the sound of my father's key was not a lack of character but neuroception — my body's effort to protect me. Van der Kolk's sentence, "the body keeps the score," explained why that stone in my stomach was still there decades later; so the body doesn't forget, but it can relearn. When I read Bowlby and Main, I found the answer to the question, "Am I broken because I both loved him and feared him?": No. I wasn't broken; I was just a child wedged into an equation with no solution, and in that equation, the inability to separate love from fear was not a mistake but the only possible outcome. IFS taught me to turn to that trembling child and say, "I'm here now, I'm the one opening the door." And the most beautiful part: before I ever read about that "inner parent" work in theory, I had already practiced it in life — by telling my own daughters "I love you" every single day. It turned out that what I was giving my children, I was at the same time giving to that child crying at the old desk. I took the science and laid it over my own life, and that map turned the sentence "I am broken" into the sentence "I am a system that can be explained, and repaired."

Where I Stand Today

I made an inner peace with my father — not by endorsing what happened, but by being able to hold the two side by side: both the love and the wound, in the same hand, without one canceling out the other. I still sometimes hear the "click" in my dreams; that old radar never shuts off completely, its volume just turns down. But awake, I am no longer that trembling child — or rather, that child is still inside me, but now there's an adult beside him, someone holding his hand. The thing I truly overcame was this question: "Am I broken because I both loved him and feared him?" No. I was just a child wedged into an impossible equation, and I did the best I could. And that promise in the inner voice — to tell my own children "I love you" — came true, every single day. My daughters' first word to me was never "click." It was "Daddy." What my father could not give me, I gave to them; and strangely, in giving it to them, I gave it to that old child too. I broke the cycle in the very place where I had lived it.
