Chapter 9 – Collapse and Disintegration

My mother… My mother is my compass. Every morning she writes to me: “How are you, my dear, I love you so much, my child.” She adds a prayer, and then says the most worldly, the tiniest thing: “Don’t forget the towel, don’t forget the slippers, change the cat’s water.” While my world was falling apart, it was those tiny sentences that kept me grounded. She and my father were often on the road with their caravan; one day they’d write from Bosnia, another from some other border. And one day I said, “When you come back, I’ll buy that caravan from you” — just in case, perhaps without realizing it, I’d already chosen the vehicle for my own journey. One day my mother wrote: “A sun that has set has never stayed set; it always rose again. Behind every darkness there is light.” That sentence became the secret heart of this book. On every night I sank, I remembered it.

Because I sank often. The money ran out. A debt-enforcement notice arrived at the house; my father wrote it to me, I was kilometers away. I tried to get a card, they wouldn’t give it to me — because my mother didn’t speak German and I wasn’t the person on the contract. A grown man, cash in hand, helpless because he couldn’t activate a card. I tried to send money through Western Union, I struggled to find a way. I was ashamed. But even the shame I noted down somewhere inside me and kept going, because for me, stopping was sinking.

A year after the divorce, by the arrangement we had made with their mother, my daughters came to live with me. In that period I was both father and mother. This went on for three years. But from the second year onward, the arrangement began to crack.

When I remember that first year today, the memory arrives first as a smell. Six in the morning. Through the gap in the curtains a light still blue, still shy, seeps in; it falls on the kitchen tiles, not cold but not yet warmed. The corridor floor I cross barefoot is cool. When I open the refrigerator door, that familiar, faintly humming coolness hits my face. I knock the egg against the rim of the pan — a crack, then a sizzle. The butter melts and foams, the smell of toast slowly fills the room, and that smell makes the house a home. The blue flame under the pot whispers softly. Outside a bird sings, as though pretending no one has woken yet.

Then little feet. First a patter, then running steps. Little hands tap the table — tap, tap, tap — like a drum, impatient, joyful. A glass of milk is knocked over, a white lake spreads across the table, and instead of getting angry, I laugh, because their laughter is so contagious. That laughter touches something in me — right in the middle of my chest, that soft place beneath the ribs. For a moment, just a moment, all the weariness I have carried over from the night slides off me like a blanket. My shoulders drop. My breath deepens. Right here, I tell myself, right here is what it means to belong somewhere.

Inner voice: I gathered those mornings without knowing it. Back then they seemed ordinary — eggs, milk, a messy table, the missed school bus. Now I understand: those "ordinary" moments were the richest of my life. A person only ever recognizes paradise afterward. While you are inside it, its only name is "exhaustion."

But the nights were something else. After they had fallen asleep, as I wandered through the house, my back would ache; not the back of a father alone, but a mother's back as well. The weight of carrying the work of two people in one body would gather at the tops of my shoulders. As I washed the last dish left in the kitchen, gathered the toys scattered across the living room, packed the bag for the next day, my body had long since emptied, yet my mind was still running. When I lay down in bed, my muscles were still on alert — one ear half awake in case someone cried, one eye half open toward the door. And the moment my eyes closed, I would see them again in my dreams. Even in my sleep I could not rest from being a father. Perhaps I never wanted to rest at all.

The Shadow That Entered My Life

Then someone new entered my life. At first she seemed to accept my daughters. She would stroke the little one's hair, she would smile. I believed that smile. I wanted to believe it — because a weary man, when he sees a shoulder, leans on it at once. But then her family got involved. To be honest, she too struggled to admit that this might be hard on her. And so — for no reason I considered worthy of note — we fought terribly. What led to the fights were always the things left unsaid. And by then I had already been through lovebombing once, so I recognized the rhythm even as I was caught in it.

The fights had a strange physics. Few words, much tension. A room would suddenly turn cold because someone had walked into it — no thermometer would show it, but the skin knows. My shoulders would rise, my jaw would clench, that familiar knot would tighten in the upper part of my stomach. Silence would be louder than a scream. As I tried to read the muscles in someone's face, I would hold my own breath; like an animal on watch, I would wait for a blow without knowing where it would come from. The unsaid things hung in the air — heavy, invisible, but drawn into me with every breath.

Then those cold looks at the dinner table, her mother's looks: "Who is this? Is he worth it?" Those looks were wordless but like a knife; sharp enough to make it hard to swallow the food on my plate. At the table everyone was talking, but the real conversation was in the eyes, and in that language I was always spelled out as "not enough." The pressure rose. The sentence "Either her… or the children" echoed in my head without pause — not like an ultimatum but as though it were a law of nature, inevitable, beyond debate.

Inner voice: There were two voices in my head, and both were shouting at the top of their lungs. One said: "You cannot be alone. Once again everyone has left, only this one remains. If you lose this too, what will be left in your hands?" The other, quieter but the truer one, whispered: "They are your daughters. They are not a bargaining chip. A father cannot even turn such a sentence over in his mind." But a weary man does not know which voice to choose. Exhaustion makes the wrong voice sound right. Fear presents the worst decision as the most reasonable one.

The Greatest Mistake of My Life

I made the greatest mistake of my life. I gave my daughters to their own mother.

The day I packed their suitcases, my hands were shaking. The metallic sound of the zipper made a strange echo in the room — with every pull, something inside me seemed to close a little more. I was folding their little T-shirts; the fabric still carried their scent, that indescribable child smell — milk, soap, sweat, and a little sun too. Once again I picked up one of their little teddy bears. It still had their scent. I paused for a moment, brought it to my nose, and my tears dripped onto the fabric. Warm drops, on the cold toy. Something was growing in my throat; I could not swallow.

The eldest stood in the doorway. The light came from behind her, her face was half in shadow. In a trembling voice she asked: "Dad, are we leaving now?" I could not answer. I opened my mouth, but there was no word inside — only a pressure, a void, a humming. I only held her tight. One last time I pulled her warm body close to me; I felt her small, fast heartbeat against my chest, I drew the scent of her hair into me, I tried to seal all of this into my memory — because a part of me already knew this was an "ending." When they got into the car, the little one waved at me through the window with her tiny hand. The glass was covered with fingerprints. I waved too, but my knees gave way. As the car turned the corner, I held on to the wall just to stay standing.

Inner voice: "It's not too late yet. Run, stop the car, take it all back, say now the sentence you couldn't say at the table." This sentence flashed in my head like an alarm. But my body had turned to stone. Sometimes the loudest thought lives in the most motionless body. Inside, I was running. Outside, I was a statue.

That night the house was utterly empty. The beds they slept in were still warm. I went into the room, sat on the floor, picked up a little shoe and smelled it. The silence of the house had turned into a humming — that silence ringing in my ears, reminding me of what had truly happened. The clock was ticking, the refrigerator was humming, a car was passing outside; but the house felt dead. And then, quietly, for a long, long time, I wept, sobbing. My body was shaking, my breath was catching, and in that moment — let me confess it — a part of me wanted that shaking never to stop, because when it stopped, only that void would remain.

For a whole year I was in the deepest depression. That year was not a year in which the days were separate from one another; it was as though it were a single, long, gray day. Searching each morning for a reason to get up; getting up anyway when I found none; and once up, forgetting why. Food was tasteless, music came from far away, the man in the mirror was familiar yet a stranger. My body was heavy — as though lead had been poured into my bones. To this day I still feel bad about this. It is no longer a wound now, but a scar.

★ I traded them for a new beginning.

The reason for this was the lack of love in my childhood — that lack that made me dependent on love. I did not love myself, I did not respect myself — and that is exactly why I allowed this. Let us not fool ourselves: I am the guilty one. I did this.

We had promised each other: "We will not become strangers to each other." But we became strangers. Another man had already entered my ex-wife's life. The children began to call him "Dad."

When I heard that sentence, I gripped the steering wheel in the car so hard that my knuckles turned white. The leather cover of the wheel creaked in my palm. The windows had fogged up; outside blurred, inside narrow. My tears were running down my face but I did not make a single sound. Only a scream rose inside me — one that could not get out, that struck the cage of my chest and turned back. The word "Dad" had once been my name. Now it had become someone else's name. The owner of a word had changed, and this was the quietest theft in the world.

The Film I Played a Thousand Times

And then my mind kicked in — that mind that never stops, that recomposes everything. That night, those suitcases, that question "Dad, are we leaving?" — I played them out a thousand other ways.

Inner voice: "What if I had said 'no'? What if I had stood up at that table and said, 'My children are not a bargaining chip'? What if I had never let that woman into my life, what if I had stood up to her family's looks even once? What if I had reopened those suitcases that night? Would we be in the same house right now, would that laughter still be heard at the breakfast table? While they called someone else 'Dad,' where was I?" This film always freezes at the same spot, always magnifies the same frame. And I, inside that life that never happened, suffer as though I had truly lived it.

For a neurodivergent person this is the cruelest gift: to be able to feel what never happened as vividly as what did. My brain stages the scene so well that I hear that sizzle in the kitchen again, I feel the light of that morning that was never lived on my face, I can weigh on my tongue the heaviness of that sentence I never said. It is so real that on some mornings, when I open my eyes, for a moment I think I am in that house; then the ceiling, the real ceiling, comes crashing down on me. The vision is so clear that when I wake I grieve twice: once for what was, and once for what was not. Imagination, which was a blessing, turns into an executioner. I regret it. To this day I still regret it. But the one thing I have learned at the end of that film I played a thousand times is this: had I not made that decision, this road would never have begun. Perhaps I would have become another Tuncay — but not the Tuncay who writes these lines, who turns this pain into something.

Therapist's Note – The Traumatic Decision and the Loss of the Father Identity

What is happening here: This is the hardest point in the book, and it deserves honesty more than consolation. A decision is made under maximum pressure — family pressure from outside, fear of loneliness from within, manipulation (lovebombing) on top of that, and an ultimatum. In such moments AuDHD intensifies both rumination (obsessive thinking) and emotional overload — until the system overflows and a decision is made "just so it ends." The loss of the father role is one of the deepest wounds a person can suffer. An important distinction must also be established right from the start: guilt and responsibility are not the same thing. Taking responsibility: "I made this decision." Drowning in endless guilt: "Because I made this decision, I am worthless as a human being." The first heals. The second destroys.

Where it comes from — body and evolution

Beneath this decision, it was not logic that was working, but a far older circuit. Constant high pressure, conflict, and an ultimatum like "either her, or the children" push the nervous system into chronic threat mode. In Bessel van der Kolk's words, when the brain's alarm bell, the amygdala — "the brain's smoke detector" (The Body Keeps the Score, 2014) — rings for a long time, the thinking brain (the prefrontal cortex) weakens; a person no longer makes the "good" decision, only the one that "ends the threat." Stephen Porges's Polyvagal Theory sheds light on this: when threat becomes unbearable, the nervous system shifts from the fight-or-flight response to a collapse/freeze (dorsal vagal) response (Porges, 2011) — your inability to answer in the doorway, your body turning to stone, is precisely this freeze state, not a "lack of character."

What you call "dependence on love" also has an address. The attachment theory of John Bowlby and Mary Ainsworth shows that a child who does not see secure love early in life experiences, in adulthood, the fear of being alone almost like an existential danger (Bowlby, 1969). Jonice Webb names this silent wound emotional neglect (Running on Empty, 2012): the failure to see a child's inner world while on the surface "everything is fine." A person who does not value themselves enough to feel whole on their own seeks that missing value outside, in a relationship; and in order not to lose it, they may risk even their most precious thing. On top of that, the "what if it had been like this" film you played a thousand times is, neurologically, an overactivity of the default mode network, that is, rumination (Nolen-Hoeksema, 1991) — the same scene coming round and round again.

If left unaddressed — possible bad outcomes

The brain hardens the sentence "I failed as a father, I was not enough" into a chronic cycle of guilt and identity crisis. Possible course: self-hatred, living constantly in the past, excessive anxiety in new relationships and either clinging or fleeing, deep and recurring depressive episodes, disruption of sleep and appetite. A person judges themselves for a decision made under conditions they would never allow today — forgetting that the person of that day did not have today's knowledge. Unaddressed grief turns, over time, into "frozen grief": a pain that can neither be cried out nor let go of, petrifying inside. And unresolved guilt quietly slides into the sentence "I do not deserve happiness" — which is one of the most common ways a person sabotages their own future.

Paths of healing — several doors

There is no single prescription for this wound; there are several doors, and all of them are built on "carrying it without denying the truth and without executing yourself":

• Compassion Focused Therapy (CFT – Paul Gilbert) and self-compassion (Kristin Neff): Answering that judging voice inside you as if you were speaking to your dearest friend, not a convict. "In that moment, with that knowledge, that person did the best they could."
• Internal Family Systems (IFS – Richard Schwartz): Seeing the "part" that made that decision not as a murderer, but as a frightened and weary protector trying to shield you from loneliness; and returning to that wounded part who froze and remained standing there with the question "Dad, are we leaving?", to keep it company.
• EMDR and Somatic Experiencing (Peter Levine): Processing, at the bodily level, the scenes nailed into the body — that doorway, that tiny waving hand — and releasing the frozen energy.
• Grief work and expressive writing (James Pennebaker): Putting unfinished grief into words — it has been shown that putting emotion into writing improves mood and even immunity (Pennebaker, 1997). This very book enters through that door.
• Post-traumatic growth (Tedeschi & Calhoun): Binding the pain to a meaning, not by saying "it was good," but by asking "where did this take me?" Meaning does not erase pain; it makes it bearable (this is also the essence of Frankl's logotherapy).

Tuncay's bridge

For years I played this scene in my head under the heading "I am a monster." Then I read Porges, and for the first time I could see my inability to answer in the doorway, my body turning to stone, as a "freeze response" — my shame did not lessen by a single gram, but the meaning changed: in that moment I had no will, because my nervous system had already shut down. When I read Bowlby and Jonice Webb, the answer to the question "why did I give away my most precious thing so as not to lose a love?" became painfully clear: because inside me I had raised a child who valued himself so little he was not worth losing. I did not erase the guilt — I still carry the responsibility, I carry it knowingly. But when I learned IFS, I stopped cornering and beating the part that made that decision; I could sit across from it and say, "I know, you were so afraid, you were trying not to leave me alone." And when I learned that the "what if it had been like this" film was actually rumination, a habit of my brain rather than an illness of it, even though I could not stop the film, I could at least say, "this is a film, not reality." Have I overcome it? Entirely, no. But I no longer sit alone in that scene, in the guilty chair — there is someone beside me who understands, and that someone is me.

Where I Stand Today

Shall I be honest? This is not "closed," and I do not want to pretend that it is. Some decisions heal not into smooth skin but into a scar — and we have the right to show the scars. What I have overcome: self-hatred. Today I can hold the two together — "I am responsible" and "I was a wounded person, dependent on love because I saw no love as a child." Both are true. I have never changed the love I feel for my daughters, only the physical closeness was lost. And the pain, no matter how great, did something: it forced me to ask why I loved myself so little that I would give away my most precious thing. That question started everything that came after.
