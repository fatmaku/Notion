Chapter 7 – Becoming a Father

I lived love the same way. Fast, deep, all at once. I can’t love halfway; either I’m not there, or I’m there with my whole being. I married. Then once more. I became a father. And there I found the greatest love of my life: my daughters. When they came, that restless voice inside me went quiet for a moment. For the first time I belonged somewhere. For them, the same thing always crossed my mind: these children will do great, beautiful things; there will be art in their homes, books, magic. I believed in them, even in the times I couldn’t believe in myself.

I will never forget the moment I learned I was going to be a father. I remember it down to my cells. The bathroom light was harsh — that cheap white fluorescent glow, flickering and yellowish, sharpening even the limescale stains along the rim of the sink. From outside, beyond the closed door, came the muffled sounds of ordinary life: a dripping tap, the distant murmur of a television, my own breath. The pregnancy test trembled in my hand. The plastic had warmed in my palm; my fingers gripped it so tightly that my knuckles had gone white.

When I saw that plus sign, my breath caught for an instant. It truly caught — the air snagged somewhere in my chest, my heart skipped a beat, then raced like mad. I could hear my own pulse pounding in my ears. Something tightened in the pit of my stomach, then loosened, then rose in a wave.

And then that wave broke inside me. I leapt up. I laughed — startled by my own voice, like a child, uncontrollably. I danced like a madman in that little room, flung my arms into the air, and shouted: "I'm going to be a father!" My voice echoed off the walls, came back to me, and stirred me up all the more. What I felt was this: Now I can do better. Now everything will be better.

Inner voice: In that moment two voices spoke inside me at once. One was childlike, hopping and skipping: "I have a chance! I'll give what was never given to me! From scratch, a clean page!" The other was lower, more fearful, whispering from a corner: "What if you can't do it? What if you do to them what your father did to you? Is love inherited too, or only fear?" The first voice was shouting, the second only whispered — but I heard them both. And I made room for both of them, there, inside that moment.

Inner voice: And what if a single line had appeared on that test? Sometimes I wonder — who would I have been in that parallel life? Perhaps a man who grew old without ever touching his wound, telling himself "the problem isn't me." If my children hadn't shown me that wound, I might never have learned to look at it.

★ Some wounds we only learn to touch while caring for someone too small to carry them.

When I walked into the delivery room, that smell struck me. First I stood at the door — inside it was warm, even hot, and over the sterile chlorine-antiseptic scent of the hospital something else was layered. Fresh, sweet, pure — the smell of a baby. Something indescribable yet unforgettable; it smells of milk, of warm skin, of a life just begun. It was as if the world stopped. All the sounds outside — the beeping of the monitors, a nurse's low voice, footsteps in the corridor — all of them pulled back at once, as if someone had turned the world's volume down. All that was left was that tiny body and me.

The tiny fingers barely fit into my palm. I bent down. Her cheeks were so soft that the moment my fingertip touched them, the baby's eyes fluttered faintly. I could feel her chest rising and falling, that small, quick heartbeat beneath my finger — like a bird's heart, hurried but steady. In that moment my hands stopped trembling. My hands, which had trembled for thirty years, were for the first time holding something so carefully, so unafraid. And a single sentence passed through me, like a prayer: "I will give you everything that was never given to me."

The First Word: Daddy

To this day I still carry within me the day she first said "Daddy." It was late afternoon; the light filtering through the curtain drew a golden, slanted ribbon across the room, dust motes turning slowly within it. Her small body lay in my arms, her weight settling right onto my chest — warm, slack, a weight that had surrendered itself to me completely. She looked at me with sleepy eyes. Her pupils sharpened for a moment, found me, and in that trembling, innocent voice she said: "Daddy…"

I have to say this with pride: the first word of every one of my children was "Daddy." Every one of them. All four. I'm not telling this as a coincidence — because I don't think it was one. In their very first days in this world, they named the voice they trusted most, the eyes that watched over them most, the chest that kept them warm, "Daddy." This was the reverse of that sentence I never heard in my own childhood. My father never once said "I love you" to me. But my daughters' first word to me was love itself.

Inner voice: When that syllable, "Daddy," struck my ear, two men stood inside me at once. One was the me of that moment — baby in his arms, eyes brimming. The other was a very old child, at the back of the classroom, in the corner of the house, little Tuncay waiting for a sentence he had never heard. I turned to that child inside me and said: "You see? So that sentence really does exist. So it can be spoken. It just wasn't my turn — now I'm the one who says it."

In that moment something melted right in the center of my chest. It was a physical sensation — a warmth beneath the sternum, a loosening, as if the lock on something that had been chained there for years was opening. As tears slid down my cheeks I only smiled; the salty taste reached the corner of my lip, but I didn't wipe it away. It passed through me: "There. The chain breaks right here." That long, rusted, cold chain reaching from my father to me, from me to this small body — right here, in this golden light, it split in two with a single syllable: "Daddy."

Night Walks

At night I would take my two daughters from my first marriage, Ceylin and Ceysu, into my arms and pace slowly, up and down the living room. The house was dark; only a faint light leaking from the kitchen and the pale trace of the streetlamp outside falling through the window. I would count my steps — right, left, right, left — trying to match that rhythm to their breathing. The wooden floor creaked softly beneath my feet; I memorized that sound until I knew it by heart, knew which board groaned in which tone, so as not to wake them.

One's head on my shoulder, the other's tiny hand clinging to my shirt. Their palms were so small that a whole hand could barely wrap around one of my fingers. The warmth of their bodies passed into me — that mild heat seeping in through my chest, my arm, my neck, dissolving all my tension. I would listen to their heartbeats; when I leaned my ear in close I could hear that small, quick pattering. Sometimes they would wake, and I would simply watch how they breathed — small chests rising and falling, rising and falling. I would bury my nose in their hair; that warm, sweet scent of milk and sleep.

When I rubbed their tiny feet, slowly running my fingers over them, they would relax — the little toes would clench first, then open in my palm, surrender. And I relaxed along with them. It was right then that I realized: for the first time, touch was not an alarm. For the first time, closeness was safe. My body, for the first time in thirty years, was learning to touch without fear.

Inner voice: Something strange was happening on those night walks. I was soothing them, but the one truly being soothed was me. Their breathing was slowing mine. As if a tiny teacher were sleeping in my arms, quietly telling me, "breathe like this, be safe like this." Sometimes I couldn't tell who was raising whom.

★ While you soothe your child, you also gather into your arms the child inside you who has been waiting to be soothed.

In Them I Recognized Myself

As they grew, I noticed something: they carried what I carried too. That intense perception. That feeling of everything deeply. That instant catching of the smallest change in tone of voice, the slightest movement of an eyebrow. They would walk into a room and their eyes would scan it in a second; who was sad, who was angry, who was simply tired — they read it all. Once, at the dinner table, while I was quietly aching over something inside without raising my voice a single note, my eldest set down her fork and asked: "Daddy, are you sad?" Before I even knew what my own face was giving away, she had understood. They were analysts too. Their feelings ran strong. Just like mine.

And it was right here that a different kind of tenderness was born in me. Not an ordinary father's love — a recognition. As I looked at them, I was looking at my own childhood. I could see their intensity not as an "excess" but as a gift, because I had paid the price for that intensity. In place of everyone who had asked me "why are you like this?", I would be able to tell them "how wonderful that you are like this."

Inner voice: "If they carry the neurodifference I carry, then my task is clear: I will never let them feel 'wrong' for being 'different.' I will not do to them what was done to me. The cycle will end with me."

Inner voice: But there was a fear too, let me be honest. "What if I'm the one who passed this intensity on to them? What if my genes carried to them both the gift and the price that has to be paid, together?" Then I answered myself: "So be it. Since I'm the one who knows the price, then I can give them the map. No one gave me a map. I walked in the dark — but they will walk with a lantern in hand. The lantern's name is: 'You are not wrong.'"

Not a Mask, but the Real Face

I knew one thing very clearly: children learn not what their fathers say, but what they are. And they are the world's finest lie detectors — especially the analyst children, the ones with strong feelings. I could not act out happiness in front of them. Because they would catch acted-out happiness at once, just as I had caught my father's two faces, the one at home and the one outside. I remember those two faces — the smile pinned on at the knock of the door, the curtain falling once the door closed. As a child I saw that switch every single time, and every single time something inside me shrank.

If I smiled in front of them while collapsing inside, the only thing I would teach them is to wear a mask. Yet masks were exactly what had broken me. I didn't want them to learn the mask. There was only one way: to be truly happy. To be truly well. To truly be. For me to be able to reflect it onto them, it first had to be real within me. You cannot explain peace to a child; you can only give peace to them by being peaceful yourself. That is why my own healing was not a luxury but a responsibility toward them.

★ The one thing you cannot give your child is the thing you do not have within yourself.

Inner voice: This idea was both a deliverance and a cruelty. A deliverance, because it gave me a direction: "Heal, because they will drink this in." A cruelty, because it closed off escape. I could no longer say, "I'll manage, I'll suppress it, I'll act." My children, without knowing it, condemned me to honesty. And I looked upon this not as a punishment but as a gift.

★ Sometimes we undertake our deepest healing not for ourselves, but for the small eyes watching us.

Life Knows Where to Strike

But life knows exactly where it will hurt most. When the separation came, everything suddenly fell silent. That silence was a physical thing — the balance of sound in the house had been broken; the space once filled with children's laughter, the patter of feet, the calls of "Daddy," was now suddenly bare. I had to learn this: I am not the only one who carries a family; in a marriage both sides must want the same thing.

As I packed my things my hands were trembling — that old tremor, the one that had stopped in the delivery room, had come back. The edges of the boxes were cutting my fingers, and I didn't care. I picked up one of my daughters' little teddy bears. Its fur was matted in places, one ear drooping. It still carried their scent — that sweet, warm smell, faint but there. I paused for a moment, brought it to my nose, and my tears dripped onto the bear; the fur grew wet and dark.

My eldest stood at the threshold of the door and looked at me. She stood in the frame of the doorway like a small shadow, her face half in darkness with the light behind her, but her eyes shining. There was a question in her eyes. In a small voice she asked: "Daddy, are you leaving now?" My throat knotted — a real knot, one I couldn't swallow past, couldn't speak through. I could not answer. I only held her. I pulled her warm little body close to me one last time; I felt her small heartbeat against my chest, that same hurried bird's heart that had beaten beneath my palm on the very first day.

Inner voice: "Say something. Even if it's a lie, say something kind. Say 'No, I'm not leaving,' say 'everything will be all right.'" But I couldn't. Because I had made a promise — that I would wear no mask. That moment was the hardest test of my life: to carry the truth without lying, yet without shattering her. How do you give a child the truth when you yourself are being crushed beneath that very truth? I never found my answer. I only held her. Perhaps holding her was the most honest sentence I could speak in that moment.

When I got into the car, the little one waved her hand at the window. That small hand, behind the glass, opened and closed — slow, hesitant. I waved back too, tried to smile, but something tore inside me; a real sensation of rending in my chest. I started the engine, the sound drowned out the trembling of my voice, and as I pulled away from that street I had to fight hard not to look in the mirror.

In the years that followed it was always the same, every time the visitation weekends ended and I had to hand them back. Every farewell was a small repetition of that first farewell; the wound never fully closes, and each time it opens in the very same place.

That first night my home was utterly empty. When I turned the key, the silence that flooded in greeted me. The beds where they had slept were still warm — truly, when I laid my hand on the sheet, that mildness was there, as though they had only just gotten up, but they were gone. I went into the room, sat on the floor, leaned my back against the bed, picked up their little pillows and breathed them in. That scent — milk, sleep, child — when it filled my nose, I could bear it no longer. And then quietly, for a long while, I wept, sobbing; trying to make no sound, as if I were still afraid of waking them.

Inner voice: "Will they hate me? Will they miss my voice? Will they forget my scent?"

Inner voice: And right after, another voice: "Did you forget your father's voice, his scent? No. You never forgot the good or the bad. So they won't forget either. So all those night walks, those first words, that warmth in their palms — none of it was erased. Somewhere inside them, the scent of you remains. Distance cannot erase it." This thought didn't quite console me — but it calmed me. Sometimes that is all we can ask for: to know that we haven't been erased.

I cannot be angry at their mother. I only feel a deep sorrow. But the truth is this: I loved them dearly. I still love them. This love has not changed. It never will.
