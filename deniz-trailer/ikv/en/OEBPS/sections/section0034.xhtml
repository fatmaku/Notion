Chapter 12 – Awakening and Surrender

The diagnosis was there. The pieces had fallen into place. Two words — ADHD, autism — had explained, in a single sentence, the life I had carried for years like a riddle. But something was still missing. Knowing wasn't enough. I was looking into myself the way you look at an X-ray: bones in place, the fracture here, the crack there. I saw all of it. Seeing did not heal. Understanding does not warm a person in the middle of the pain.

A diagnosis is like a map pressed into your hand. Every road is marked on it, every mountain named. But a map doesn't walk you anywhere. A map won't keep you from freezing. You fold the map, slip it into your pocket, and then realize you are still standing there, in the cold, alone. That is exactly how I was standing. A perfect map in my hand, and beneath my feet a terrain I had never crossed.

I didn't only want to understand. I wanted to live. That chasm between knowing and being — I felt it with my whole body.

Inner voice: "All right, I get it. So this is how I am. But why? Why all this pain? Why this loneliness? There must be a reason. Or must there not? Maybe even looking for a reason is a kind of illness. But still I ask — is there something deeper, or am I searching for a door that doesn't exist inside my head?"

I looked for an answer. I looked in books — scribbling notes in the margins, underlining some lines three times over. I looked in people — gazing into their faces, asking without ever asking, "Do you know? Have you found something I don't have?" I looked in myself — in the dead of night, listening to the sound of my own breath. And at the bottom of every search, there was a feeling that never fell silent, always coming from the same place: "There is more. There is something beyond the words. You are trying to catch it with words, but it sits in a room where those words don't live."

Spirituality

First I tried the doors one by one. I took Tantra trainings in three different countries and a shamanic training as well. For hours we worked with the body; I learned breathing techniques, touch, energy work. The rooms were always alike: the coolness of the wooden floor, the heavy, sweetish smoke of burning incense, a drum or a bell sounding softly, someone saying in gentle English, "now follow your breath." At first my body relaxed in those rooms. My shoulders, the ones that had been climbing toward my ears for years, came down a little. My stomach loosened a little.

I traveled a great deal to learn about neurodivergence and spiritual cultures, about the place of neurodivergent people in these traditions. I grew more and more "spiritual" — I learned the words, I learned the postures, I learned to close my eyes at the right moment. But every time, the instant I stepped out the door into the noise of the street, the same emptiness quietly came back. As if there were a room inside me that no incense could fill; its door locked, its key lost.

Inner voice: "Not this either. Look, not again. I'm learning techniques — good techniques, techniques that work — but I don't want techniques. I'm searching for truth. The difference is this: a technique says 'do this,' truth says 'you are this.' I'm still doing. I haven't yet become."

Inner voice: "Or… maybe there is no such thing as truth and I'm just tired? No. No, this emptiness has a shape. Even an empty room has a shape. I'm searching for that shape."

Preparation

Then I encountered a deep experience of consciousness — in a legal and supervised setting, under expert guidance, a work that went beyond the limits I knew. I deliberately do not name the substance in this book; because my aim is to share an experience, not to steer anyone toward anything. I did not do it for fun. It was not an escape. It was a real, naked, frightening search.

I prepared for months. I became vegan. I gave up smoking and alcohol completely. In that period I didn't kill even a fly — I remember taking one that buzzed at the windowsill into my palm and setting it free outside; that tiny, panic-filled tremor in my palm, and a strange tenderness passing through me. In the last days I fasted. My body grew light; when I woke in the mornings, my steps felt lighter, as though some ballast had been cast out of me. My mind cleared; thoughts no longer piled one on top of another as before, but came one by one, slowly.

But alongside the lightness there was a shadow that never left it: fear. A stone in the center of my chest, lodged there even when I swallowed. At night, before sleep, I would probe that stone the way you probe a tooth with your tongue.

Inner voice: "Am I ready? Am I truly ready, or am I lulling the fear to sleep by saying I'm ready? What if I can't come back? What if I return from where I go not as Tuncay, but as something else? What if I never return?"

Inner voice: "But there's also this: what if I never go? What if I wait at this door until the end of my life, on the threshold, saying 'maybe someday'? There are two fears before me. I have to step into one. And I think what I fear more is dying without ever taking a step."

The Experience

I entered the room. A dim light — tinged with orange, easy on the eye, as intimate as a candle. The scent of incense hung in the air; heavy, sweet, a little earthy. A soft cushion had been laid on the floor. I lay on my back. I looked at the ceiling. My heart was like a drum in my chest — not fast, but deep, every beat echoing in my ribs. I counted my breaths. One. Two. Three.

Then I took it. In the first minutes nothing happened. The ceiling was the ceiling, I was myself, my heart was still beating. A small, mocking voice rose inside me.

Inner voice: "There. I knew it. All this preparation, all this fear — and nothing. Maybe this doesn't work on me either. Maybe I'm so 'different' that even the universe skips my turn."

Then it began. First it came from my body. From my fingertips, from the soles of my feet, a loosening spread inward — but it was more than loosening. My body dissolved. Bones, muscles, skin — all of them, in turn, slowly, dissolved like a candle melting in warm water. I did not know where I ended and where the room began. There was no boundary left.

I fell into a boundless ocean. But it was not water. If it had been water I would have drowned; this carried me. It was pure energy. There was no time — neither past nor future, neither "how much longer" nor "once upon a time." There was no space. And strangest of all: there was no longer an "I." That "I" that never fell silent, that stared at the ceiling at three in the morning asking questions — it was gone. Only a feeling remained. Intense, wordless, boundless. Until that moment I had not known a feeling could be so vast, so full.

From within — though there was no longer any place called "within" — I asked the one question I had carried for years: "Am I right? What is wrong with me?" A thirty-six-year-old question. The question I asked in the cold of the caravan, asked at the back of the classroom, asked while looking into my daughters' faces.

No voice came. No word came. But a feeling came — and that feeling spoke more clearly than millions of words. As if the whole universe paused for a moment, turned to me, and smiled. It embraced me; not the way a mother embraces her child, but the way existence embraces a part of itself. And within that embrace, beneath the words, three things were true at once: "Everything is right." "You are worthy." "Nothing is wrong." I did not understand it with my head. Every cell in me, each one separately, understood it in its own language.

Inner voice: "So everything is right. So I am not wrong. All these years… all this 'why am I like this'… It turns out none of it was a defect. My path was simply different. Different doesn't mean wrong. I knew this, actually — I had read it, written it — but for the first time it's true not because I know it, but because I feel it."

In that moment I understood everything. But I cannot tell it. Even now, writing these lines, the words slip out from between my hands; just like trying to hold water in your palm. Some things are lived, not spoken — the moment they are spoken, they shrink. It was like dying, but a death without fear. Pure. Clear. The ego dissolved entirely; that armor that had "protected" me my whole life, that had in truth confined me, melted for an instant. "I am Tuncay" was gone. The name was gone, the past was gone, the wound was gone. Only "being" remained. Infinite, peaceful, flawless.

And then I slowly came back. First the weight came — my body was a body again, a load pulled toward the ground. Then the breath; I felt the rise and fall of my chest again, as if noticing it for the first time in my life. I moved my fingers, they were there. When I opened my eyes the room was the same: the same dim light, the same incense, the same cushion. Nothing had changed. But I had changed. The room was the same; the eye looking at it was another.

Inner voice: "I was born again. And this time, as I was being born, no one greeted me as 'wrong.'"

I did this two more times, each time at a different depth, passing through a different corridor. But the message was always the same, always a single syllable, always clear: "Go." Not a question, not a command — a direction. That old compass inside me was pointing, for the first time, to a place without wavering.

Surrender and the Journey

From that moment on I surrendered myself. My whole life I had tried to control — everything, everyone, the next step, calculating every possible catastrophe in advance. For an AuDHD brain, control is not a habit but a survival strategy. And for the first time I let that strategy go. No plan. No control. Only flow.

Inner voice: "I have a task. How I know this, I cannot explain. But I know it — I know it in my bones. And the strange thing is this: knowing it does not frighten me, it rests me. As if I had worked the wrong job all my life, and someone came and said 'this is actually your work.'"

I went to Bali. The shamans performed rituals; they cleansed me, they looked at my energy — closing their eyes for a moment, then looking at one another. And they said: "You are one of us. But you are different."

Inner voice: "What do you mean? 'One of us' but 'different' — isn't this that sentence again? The sentence I've heard all my life: 'He's like us, but not quite.' But this time they're not saying it as something bad."

Inner voice: — "You were a shaman from the very beginning. An old soul…" — "An old soul? Or just a young man worn out far too early? Maybe they're the same thing."

In Japan I meditated in mystic temples. The silence of the stone gardens, the creak of the wood, the cold tatami under bare feet, the deep vibration of a bell from afar. There I learned something, and what I learned shifted a door inside me from its frame: some Buddhists, through years of deep meditation, have reached states of consciousness similar to what I lived in that room, without needing any substance, entirely by natural means. Only with breath. Only with patience. Only by looking inward.

Inner voice: "So it was always inside me. That ocean, that embracing universe, that feeling of 'everything is right' — it didn't come from outside. A substance didn't put it into me. It only opened the door for an instant. But the door was my door. I searched outside, in distant countries, in those rooms, in books — when they had put the key in my pocket the day I was born."

This was not a rote sentence of comfort. It landed in my body like a release. Because if the door is inside, then to reach it I would never again need that threshold, that fear, that risk. The source of what I sought was me. Once I understood this, everything changed — and nothing changed. I was still Tuncay. But no longer an incomplete Tuncay.

Happy and the End of Following

Here I must pause for a moment and speak of Happy. That Labrador puppy was beside me throughout this whole journey. He did a dog's purest, most unquestioning work: he followed me. Wherever I went he came after me, matching his step to mine, never taking his eyes off me. If I got up to go to the kitchen, he got up; if I headed for the door, his tail quickened; if I sat, he curled up at my feet. His whole world was me. And I — for years, without ever noticing — was doing exactly the same thing.

A shaman, a teacher, a book, a country… I was always following someone's path. "Maybe he knows. Maybe I'll find it there. Maybe what that man says is the key I've failed to find my whole life." So I went, in the footsteps of others. In Bali after a sage, in Japan after the silence of a temple, much younger after a Goku, after a cartoon hero. There was always someone "walking ahead"; I was always behind, matching my step to his.

Inner voice: "Wait a second. I'm watching Happy — how he follows me, how he never takes his eyes off me — and I feel something strange. As if I'm looking in a mirror. He follows me; and I follow someone. We're both walking after someone. So who is at the very front? Is there someone walking at the front who follows no one? Or have we all latched onto someone else's tail?"

When I lost Happy — here, on these lands, when a car carried him off too — a sentence opened inside me. Pain is sometimes like a door; it opens at the moment you least want it to, in the harshest way. What I understood while watching him became a truth once he was gone: even a dog, by following the one it loves, arrives somewhere — but that place is never its own path. It is always a borrowed path. The path of love, but not your own. And for years that is exactly what I had done. Going after someone, tracing their trail, imitating them, saying "I'll do it the way he did it"… None of this was a "path." This was, at best, walking on someone else's path.

Inner voice: "The point was never to go after someone. It was never to follow. It wasn't admiration, it wasn't imitation. The point is to build your own path, to walk your own path. Happy followed me; and I followed others for years. But a path becomes yours only when you walk it yourself. A human life isn't to be spent on a dog's loyalty. That loyalty is beautiful — but my loyalty must now be to my own step."

That day I bid farewell both to Happy and to that old Tuncay. Both had brought me a certain distance — and I am grateful for the distance they brought me. But at the threshold, both of them had to stop. The rest I had to walk myself. And there that motif came into play again, that inner voice that never falls silent, but this time without haste, this time calm: I have not become who I am, I am becoming every day — and I now walk this path no longer behind anyone, but with my own step.
