Chapter 4 – Father, Fear, and Respect

My father… was one man out in the world. At home, he was someone else entirely.

It was as if I had two fathers. One lived outside the door, the other inside it. The one outside was sunny; the one inside was rainy. And I could never know in advance which father I would find — so I stayed braced for both at once. What heavier burden can be laid on a child's shoulders than this: to be ready, in the same instant, for both moods of the same face.

Out in the world, I would watch him. I would draw back into a corner and study him closely. How he spoke with people — leaning in, listening, patting a shoulder, laughing out loud. His voice was warm; the words took on a softness as they left his mouth, as if he tucked a little honey into every sentence. His laughter was contagious; if he laughed at one end of a table, the other end laughed too. The woman next door would say, "What a courteous man." Shopkeepers would step out from behind the counter just to shake his hand. Everyone loved him. Everyone.

Inner voice: "Why isn't he like this with us? Who is this man? Where does that laughing man outside go, that he gets lost somewhere on the way home? Or is the real one the one inside, and I'm the one being fooled by the one outside?"

To see that warmth out in the world and then come home to its absence… For a child, there is nothing more bewildering. Because a bad father is easy to understand; but a father who is good outside and distant inside is impossible to understand. It meant he was capable of it. It meant the warmth was there in him. It just never reached me. And a child's mind reads this only one way: "So the fault is mine."

But the moment the apartment door clicked shut, everything changed.

The Sound of the Door

The turn of the key in the lock… Click.

I will never forget that sound. To this day I still sometimes hear it in my dreams — and when I wake, my heart has already started running before I've even opened my eyes. So the brain keeps that watch even while it sleeps.

Before that sound arrived, there was a period of preparation in the house. When the hour hand reached a certain point, the density of the air would shift; it was as if the pressure in the room rose. My mother would busy herself as if clearing the table, we would withdraw to our rooms, our ears turned toward the hallway — the whole house tensing toward the moment that was coming. Then, outside, the muffled drone of the elevator. Then footsteps, a pause at the door, the jingle of keys. And then: Click.

At that "click" my body would change automatically. As if a button had been pressed. My heart would suddenly race, pounding wildly in my chest — like a fist hammering my ribs, saying "get out, run, hide." My palms would sweat; my fingers would go cold, damp on the inside. A heavy, cold stone would settle into my stomach; I can still picture that stone, I remember it down to the kilo. My breath would go shallow, as if someone were sitting on my chest. My eyes would lock onto the door — involuntarily, the way a rabbit locks onto the rustle in the bushes. And time… time would slow down. Seconds grew heavy, turned into hours. Those three or four seconds before the door opened are the longest hours of my childhood.

Inner voice: "What's going to happen now? Did I do something wrong? Was I too loud? Did I forget to tidy my room? Which father is coming today — the one who laughs, or the silent one? What can I do to keep the weather sunny? Maybe if I'm very good, maybe if I make myself invisible, maybe if I don't even breathe…"

What a sorrow it is when a child pours all of his intelligence into a single thing: becoming invisible. In class I struggled to make sense of math, but at home I was a genius at reading the pressure. From how my father took off his shoes, where he threw his jacket, the tone of his "hello," I could map out the entire evening. A child's genius is never wasted; it is simply spent in the wrong place — on survival.

I never once felt at ease near my father. Even sitting side by side, there was an invisible pane of glass between us. There was respect — yes, the respect was complete, flawless, without a single crack. But love… I never felt it. I knew respect, because respect lived in the body as tension. I did not know love, because love lives in the body as release — and not once, beside him, was I ever able to release.

A Moment

One day I gathered my courage.

I hadn't planned it. It simply rose up in me — maybe that day I'd seen the laughing father from outside, maybe the child in me had hoped, for a moment, "maybe this time." We were in the living room. He was sitting in an armchair. The light came from the side, half his face lit, half in shadow. Something stirred in me; I wanted just a crumb of that warmth too. Just a single crumb.

I went over to him, gently. I still remember how slowly my feet moved across the carpet. I only wanted to lay my hand on his shoulder. It wasn't a big thing. Not a sentence, not a gift, not an achievement — just a hand, a shoulder, a moment. Just to be close. Just to say, "I'm here, you're here too, let's be together for a moment." My hand was in the air, about to come to rest on his shoulder.

Inner voice: "Maybe he'll smile. Maybe he'll put his hand over mine. Maybe just once, just this once…"

He looked at me. His eyes were stern. Cold and calm. And he said: "I am your father. I am not your friend. Watch how you behave."

My hand stayed in the air. Then it sank quietly, fell to my side. That sentence drove into me like a knife — it didn't even hurt at first, the way very sharp knives don't; the pain came afterward. Silence. The light in the room was the same, the furniture was the same, but something had shifted permanently.

Inner voice: "So… I was too much. What I wanted was too much. Even wanting love was too much. Closeness is a crime, and I didn't know it. Never again. Never again will I reach out this hand to anyone."

Something broke in me in that moment. A small shard of glass. It made a sound, but no one heard it — only I heard that little crack, inside, somewhere behind my breastbone. And from that day on, for years, whenever I wanted to be close to someone, I remembered the way my hand had hung in the air. Once the body learns "don't reach out," it does not easily forget.

Inner voice: "What if he'd said something else that day? What if he'd laid his hand on my shoulder, what if he'd said, 'come here, son'? That single sentence would surely have made me into someone entirely different. But then again, maybe if he had been a father who could say that sentence, I wouldn't have become the man writing this book. Strange — the place where the wound is is also the place where the pen sinks in."

Love

My father never once said, "I love you."

Not a single time. As a child, as a teenager, as I grew — not once. He never said, "I'm proud of you." Even when he saw a grade, an accomplishment, an effort, those words never left his mouth. I grew up waiting for those words; the way one waits for rain in a desert. Sometimes my mother would step in and say, "Your father loves you very much." Maybe to console me, maybe because it was really true.

Inner voice: "Then why doesn't he say it? If he loves me, why is the word so expensive? Does love run out when you speak it, does it shrink? If it's there inside, why doesn't it come out? Or am I the one who can't hear it — maybe he says it in another language, and I just don't know that language?"

Years later there was an answer I learned through my mother, a sentence that belonged to that generation: "If we say it, we'll spoil them."

The first time I heard it, I froze. So the issue wasn't that they didn't love me; the issue was that they saw love as a danger, and saw me as something to be protected from that danger. In their eyes, love was like water — too much of it would drown you. And yet I was withering from thirst.

Inner voice: "Does love really spoil you? Or is it the opposite — is it the absence of love that cripples you? And what was that thing they called respect, anyway? Was it a bowing of the head born of fear, or an admiration that comes from within? Because I bowed my head, but inside me there was no admiration, only a trembling. Is it fear that creates respect?"

My grandfather was different. He was an entirely different man. He always said one sentence, whose meaning I only grasped years later: "Respect doesn't come from a cigarette." What he meant was this: he would not accept a hollow respect born only of fear, stuck to the surface, empty inside. We didn't smoke our cigarettes in front of him — but not out of respect, out of fear of being scolded. And it was precisely this kind of "respect" my grandfather refused; it was as if he were saying, "don't pretend not to smoke for my sake; respect me from within, or don't respect me at all." Two men of the same family were teaching me two utterly different lessons: one was packaging fear as respect, the other was unwrapping that package and saying, "this is empty."

Inner voice: "How can this be? The same blood, the same table, the same roof — two people this different. One tells me, 'know your distance,' the other tells me, 'be genuine.' Who do I take after, who do I want to take after? I think both of them are inside me; one is the father who raises through fear, the other the grandfather who teaches authenticity. Which one will speak through my mouth, to my own child?"

Maybe in those days, without realizing it, I had begun to make a choice. I had chosen to take in not my father's sentence, but my grandfather's.

Control and Punishment

My father controlled everything.

Money? We never knew what there was, where it came from or where it went; he was the household's sole and invisible center. Decisions? No one was consulted. There was no debate, no exchange of ideas, no "what do you think?" He decided, we obeyed. The house was not a democracy but a silent monarchy; and the rules were unwritten, so you only learned which one you'd broken, and when, once the punishment landed.

When my grades were bad, there was punishment. But this time it wasn't a beating — it was something more insidious. A blank sheet of paper. Ten pages. Writing the same sentence over and over.

I would sit down at the desk. Before me, those blindingly white, seemingly endless pages. I would pick up the pen. Write. Write. Write. On the first page the hand is still fresh, the letters come out neat. By the third page the space between the fingers grows hot. By the fifth page that red line the pen leaves on the side of my index finger appears, then turns purple. My hand would ache; a throbbing pain rising up from the wrist. The pen dug into my fingers. The letters grew uglier and uglier, the lines slid downward, my eyes welled up. But I wasn't allowed to stop. To stop meant to start over.

Inner voice: "I am wrong. I am not enough. I have to do better. I have to work harder, make fewer mistakes, be more invisible. Maybe one day, if I'm good enough, then… then what? Then will he love me? Or even then, will he just raise the bar higher?"

When you make a child write the same sentence a hundred times, you aren't making him write the content of that sentence — you're making him write a belief: "You are a mistake that needs correcting." That belief rises off the pages and seeps into the bone, and years later, even when no one is punishing you, you keep punishing yourself.

There is a bitter irony here, one I only noticed as an adult, and when I noticed it I laughed for a long time — a sorrowful laugh, but a real one: they punished me with writing. And writing is the very thing that heals me today. The pen I'm writing these lines with is the grandchild of that pen from the punishment. My tool was born of the punishment; my medicine, of the wound. Life sometimes gives you the medicine disguised as the wound — the packaging so terrible that it takes years to see the gift inside.

Inner voice: "Imagine if I could go to that child crying at the desk and tell him: 'One day this hand, of your own free will, will write through whole nights — and that writing will save you.' Would he laugh at me, would he cry? Maybe he'd just fall silent. But maybe, just maybe, for a moment, the pen in his hand would feel to him not like a punishment, but like a promise."

Two Truths

The Table and the Field

There was also the dinner table. Today I know: because of my autism, I couldn't eat everything — some textures, some smells were too much for my body, and my stomach pushed them back. But at our table that was called "being fussy." My father had a very deep, frightening voice; and a gaze that went straight through you, that pinned you where you stood — a lethal look. "No one gets up before the plate is empty." I would stand over the plate, tears running, snot running, trying to force down what my body refused. I wasn't allowed to get up — because getting up would have made him angrier. That is how I remember it: a child, a plate, and across from him, a fear.

Inner voice: "My body says 'no,' but 'no' is forbidden. So is the body wrong — or am I wrong?"

Then the scale tipped the other way. That fear lying between me and food flung me in the opposite direction: I began to eat too much, I gained weight. Now I was fat. And one day, without anyone asking me, they signed me up at a football club — the very club where my friends were. My father came to one of the games. In front of all my friends he yelled at me, called me "fatso," belittled me before everyone's eyes. Was I eight, was I ten — I don't remember exactly. But one thing I remember with certainty: from that day on, I never had anything to do with football again.

When my father came home, I would tremble.

This was not a reflex — that is, not something I was born with, but something acquired afterward. It was a conditioning. Just like the dogs that drool at the sound of a bell, my body too had learned to go on alert at a sound, at the turn of a key. My body would slip into "danger" mode on its own; without my deciding, without my wanting it. Long before the brain could say, "wait, maybe there's no danger," the body had already prepared to flee.

And yet — the strangest, the most inexplicable, the most bewildering part: I loved my father. Truly. I loved him from that pure place where a child is meant to love his father — sincerely, deeply. But at the same time I was afraid of him. Both at once, in the same chest, looking at the same face.

Inner voice: "How is this possible? To both love the same person and be afraid of him… Aren't love and fear opposites? If I'm afraid, I shouldn't love; if I love, I shouldn't be afraid. But both are there. So there must be something wrong inside me. Am I broken? Or is this something that lives inside everyone, and no one has been able to say?"

When I learned my father had cancer, I cried for hours. I shut myself in my room, closed the door, and wept — so much that I couldn't even fully tell what I was weeping for. I was crying for the man I'd been afraid of. I was crying for the arms that had never once held me. In that moment, through the tears, a naked and merciless truth surfaced: my father had never truly been a father to me. I never received that role, that warmth, that "I'm here for you." And yet I loved him. I love him today too.

I think that's what I was weeping for: I wasn't weeping for the father I was about to lose, but for the father I never had. A person grieves most for the thing he never got to live.

Inner voice: Maybe this was his greatest legacy: he showed me how not to be a father. He gave me one of the most precious lessons of my life, by the most painful road. And it was precisely this that I wanted to do differently. If I ever had children of my own — I promised, in that room, through the tears — I would tell them "I love you" every single day. I would be there for them like a friend; they would be able to lay their hands on my shoulder, and I on theirs. And they wouldn't even have to keep waiting for the "click" of the door to feel safe. For them, a door would be just a door.
