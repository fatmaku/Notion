Appendix — Overall Assessment and Roadmap

At the end of every chapter I left you a “Your Turn” space. If you filled it in along the way, you now hold something scattered but very valuable: the pieces of your own inner map. This final part brings them together. I am not giving you a diagnosis here — only a way to read your own answers and choose the next step. Take your time. You don't fill these pages in a single day, but over several sittings.

1. Go Back: Your Notes Are Your Map

First, close the book and look again at your “Your Turn” notes. In which chapter did you write the most? At which question did your hand pause, your eyes fill, or did you think “that's exactly me”? Those chapters are no accident — there's a door there. Mark them. Note down the three chapters that touched you most; the whole roadmap will form around them.

3. How to Read Your Results

Now look at the numbers. Lines you rated 4–5 are open wounds — the places that most run your life today, which means your priority. Lines you rated 1–2 are healed or healing; protect them and draw strength from them. Most people think they have a single wound; usually, though, 2–3 themes feed each other (for example, “being invisible” + “over-giving” + “tying worth to looks” come from the same root). Look at the common root of your high scores: almost always it leads back to the belief “I am not enough / not worthy.”

★ A wound is not a shameful flaw; it is a compass that shows you where to begin.

4. Where to Begin — The Order

Order matters. People usually want to start with the most painful wound; but a nervous system on alert cannot carry the deep work. So the suggested order is:

1) Body and safety first. Whatever theme dominates, the first task is to calm the nervous system. Because while the body is in “danger” mode, the mind cannot learn (the polyvagal logic from Chapters 1, 4, 11). Several times a day: long exhales, feet on the ground, a short walk.

2) Then self-compassion. No wound heals without softening the inner critic. Learn to speak to yourself as you would to a friend (Chapters 8, 13).

3) Then boundaries. A small “no,” a small “this doesn't feel good to me.” A boundary is self-worth in bodily form (Chapters 3, 6).

4) Then the core wound. The theme you scored highest in step 2. Now that you have safety and compassion as ground, you can touch it — ideally with professional support.

5) Meaning last. Turn your pain into a task, a line that reaches toward others (Chapters 14–16). Meaning is not the result of healing; it is what carries it.

5. Which Theme → Which Method

Find your high-scoring theme; the direction beside it is drawn from that chapter's therapist's note. These are not instructions, only directions: all are walked more safely with a professional.

• Invisibility / emotional neglect → journaling, sharing “what's in me today?”, connection; schema/compassion-focused approaches.
• Shame / body shame → grounding + Compassion-Focused Therapy (CFT, Gilbert); reframing shame as “other people's mirror” instead of “my flaw.”
• Fear-based bonding / fear of authority → “inner parent” work, Internal Family Systems (IFS), closeness exercises with a safe person; EMDR if needed.
• Love / over-giving / lovebombing → boundary work, IFS, practicing “my needs are normal.”
• Attachment / loss / grief → a witness for the grief (therapist/journal), making meaning; for “ambiguous loss,” specialized support.
• Worth / body → self-compassion (Neff), practicing “my worth is independent of my looks.”
• Guilt / responsibility → self-forgiveness; internalizing “in that moment, I did what I could with what I had.”
• ‘I'm wrong’ belief / late diagnosis → the frame “I am different, a system” + structure (routine, sensory control).
• Control / anxiety → breath/body, surrender practice, meditation.
• Anger / emotion regulation → bodily calming + the awareness “this anger is the echo of old wounds.”

6. When Professional Help Is Essential

This book is a friend, not a therapist. In the following cases, please turn to a professional (psychologist/psychiatrist) — that is not weakness, but a wise step: thoughts of self-harm or suicide; being unable to keep up daily functioning (work, sleep, eating); substances/alcohol or another addiction taking over your life; past trauma constantly flooding the present. If you are in immediate danger, call your country's emergency line or a person close to you right away. You don't have to carry it alone.

★ Asking for help is not the end of the road; it is arriving at the right place.

7. A Small Four-Week Start

Big plans overwhelm; small steps transform. Four weeks, a few minutes a day:

Week 1 — Body: Twice a day, 1 minute of long exhales + feet on the ground, saying “right now I am safe.”
Week 2 — Compassion: Each evening, write one single kind sentence for a moment you blamed yourself that day — the way you'd speak to a friend.
Week 3 — Boundary: Say one small “no” or “I'm not comfortable with this”; then write down what you felt.
Week 4 — Connection: Open up to a trusted person or a neurodivergent community with one single sentence: “Sometimes I feel this way too.”

After four weeks, do the assessment from step 2 again. Your numbers don't have to change — it's enough that you notice them. Because healing is not an arrival, but a direction.

The Story of My Logo

This butterfly is no ordinary mark. Every line of it tells my story.

The spiral on its wings is a strand of DNA. Because my difference was not added later; it was there from the very beginning, in my core, in my genes. Neurodivergence is not a flaw — it is a design.

That same DNA also traces a sideways infinity sign (∞) — timeless and placeless. This is the worldwide symbol of neurodivergent people: we have existed in every age, in every place — and we always will.

The butterfly was once a caterpillar — a creature that crawled on the ground and was thought to be “unfinished.” But when it found its essence, it transformed. It is the emblem of change and of finding oneself; proof that what is called ugly is in truth a door into beauty.

And the colours … they are the colours of neurodivergence. Each one a different mind, a different way of seeing.
