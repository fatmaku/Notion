Therapist's Note – Self-Actualization and Identity Integration

What is happening here: Here we see the visible form of what Abraham Maslow called "self-actualization": once the core wounds have been tended to some degree, a person is pushed not inward but outward — wanting to have an effect, to make meaning, to share, to build. In Maslow's later writings, "self-transcendence" is added to this: fulfillment is no longer individual, but comes from touching others. The creative, system-building, pattern-seeing power of AuDHD finally opens up in the right environment: out of the "wrong piece" comes a builder, a storyteller, a bridge. Especially healing is the word Takiwātanga: a language that does not shame but honors can truly close a lifelong wound of identity. "Learning to stop" in Kadıköy is no small detail either: taking movement out of being an escape and turning it into a choice is an important sign of identity integration (of the path toward what Erik Erikson called "ego integrity").

Where it comes from — body and evolution

The impulse to "share" has very deep biological roots. The human being is a species that owes its survival to its group; a lone ancestor would have been swallowed by the savanna, while the tribe lived on. That is why our brains code "being seen" and "making a contribution" as rewards — the dopamine that kicks in when you share a story is the same system that nourished the person who carried information back to the tribe in our ancestors. The relief felt by ten thousand people listening to someone on a stage say "I'm like this too" is no accident either: the brain's mirror neuron systems (discovered by Rizzolatti and his team in the 1990s) allow us to "try out" another person's experience in our own bodies — this is the neural basis of empathy and of the feeling "I am not alone." When Tuncay tells of someone's wound, the listener's nervous system recognizes that wound and relaxes.

That "hollow opening in the stomach when the red light of the camera comes on" in the studio is real physiology too: in Stephen Porges's Polyvagal Theory this is called sympathetic arousal — the body reads the signal "I am being evaluated" like an ancient threat (Porges, 2011). The voice trembles, the mouth dries, the back of the neck sweats. But Tuncay's saying "and then I opened up" is the other side of that same theory: the interested, non-judging gaze of the faces opposite (social connection) draws the nervous system back into "safe" mode — into the ventral vagal state. A person can only be calmed by the safe presence of another person. Moreover, the sentence "once I stopped, I became reachable to people" is the adult echo of John Bowlby's attachment theory: without a secure base (an address, a continuity) one can fully approach neither oneself nor others. Tuncay went through life with an avoidant/scattered pattern; Kadıköy is the secure base he built for himself, belatedly.

If left unaddressed — possible bad outcomes

This threshold is as slippery as it is beautiful. The first risk: turning movement into a new escape — forever forward, so that nothing and no one can catch up. Then the sentence "the road is my form" becomes not a freedom but an excuse for fleeing; relationships always stay half-finished, roots never take hold. The second risk: the burnout of being a bridge — carrying everyone to the other side and being unable to tell anyone of one's own fatigue; in people who are neurodivergent, highly empathic, and prone to the "rescuer" role, this can lead to a silent burnout, to compassion fatigue, and to withdrawing once again. The third risk: the price of visibility — criticism, trolls, comparison; if no boundaries are set, the newly born self-esteem can become dependent on outside approval all over again (just as "ten thousand people loved it" nourishes today, "few likes" will wound just as deeply tomorrow). If left unaddressed, the wing that has opened seeks the cocoon again.

Paths of healing — several doors

At this threshold there are several doors that help, and all of them are about "protecting yourself while continuing to grow":

• Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT – Steven Hayes): instead of solving the dilemma "to stay or to go?", acting according to value. The question becomes not "where am I?" but "which of my values am I serving?" Tuncay's saying "my form is movement" is healthy only if it is tied to a value (to share, to be a bridge).
• Internal Family Systems (IFS – Richard Schwartz): seeing the voice that says "keep going" and the voice that says "stay" not as enemies, but both as parts protecting you — and letting the "I" (the Self) mediate between them. Tuncay's stopping those two voices and distinguishing them in this chapter is a textbook IFS move.
• Compassion Focused Therapy (CFT – Paul Gilbert) and self-compassion (Kristin Neff): the bridge carries everyone, but the bridge needs maintenance too. Showing yourself the very same compassion you show those strangers you carry. Setting boundaries is not selfishness; it is the structural maintenance of the bridge.
• Narrative therapy (White & Epston): the exact name for what Tuncay is doing. Rewriting his life from the story of "a broken person" into the story of "someone making his way in his own time and his own space." This book itself is a session of narrative therapy.
• Post-traumatic growth (Tedeschi & Calhoun): what comes after the breaking is not a false "I'm healed" but a genuine deepening: new relationships, new meaning, new strength. What is visible here is exactly that — the wound is turning into material.

Tuncay's bridge

I lived all of this before I ever read about it — then, when I read it, I'd say, "so this has a name," as it always went. When I read Maslow, I understood that the pressure inside me — "I'll explode if I don't share" — was not an illness but a push toward the top of a pyramid; I felt relieved. When I learned about mirror neurons, I grasped why a listener's eyes well up when someone on a stage tells of their wound, and why I too am relieved by their relief — this is not magic, it's biology. Thanks to Porges, I stopped being angry at that hollow in my stomach in the studio; now I can say to it, "hello, old friend, stage fright again." And what helped me most of all: Takiwātanga. When I heard that word, what all the psychology books were trying to do, a single word did in one stroke — it honored me. Honest bridge: have I crossed over? Is the integration complete? No, not entirely. I still swing between "stay" and "go," I still sometimes notice the fatigue of being a bridge too late. But here's the difference: I now see the swinging not as an illness but as a rhythm. I don't know whether the wings have fully opened — but I am flying, and I am not falling. Like every therapist's note in this book, this too is a map I'm holding out to you: perhaps the place you call "what's wrong with me" is simply your own time and your own space.

Where I Stand Today

This is not a closing chapter where a box finally gets ticked, but an open door — and that is exactly how it should be. I stopped trying to choose between "staying" and "going" and accepted that my form is movement. But Kadıköy taught me that I can stand still within movement too: for six months I've had an address, I give time to myself, I've become reachable to people. I carry the word Takiwātanga with me like an ID card: in my own time, in my own space. HappyTuncay, the podcasts, the television, Happyiz, the butterfly — all of them became real; not because I am perfect, but because I am real. Let me be honest: I still notice the fatigue of being a bridge too late, I still sometimes have to stop and weigh whether the "keep going" voice is escape or direction. But I can do that now — I can stop and weigh it. The cocoon has torn open. I don't know whether the wings have fully unfolded. But I am already flying. And this time I am not falling.
