As you read this book, take my place. Close your eyes, then open them with mine. Be me for a while — step into my head, see what I see, protect me when I’m afraid, laugh when I laugh. Because I’m not telling you about myself; I’m making you into me for a while. When you look back at the end, you’ll know how I changed not from the outside but from within. If you’re ready, let’s begin.

Look, let me tell you. From the beginning.

Chapter 1 – Who Am I, or What Am I?

I always lived inside my head. My body was in one place, I was in another. The teacher would explain something at the board, and I would watch the bird passing by the window: where is it going, why did it choose that branch, how many leaves does that branch have. “Tuncay!” When I heard my name, I’d flinch, come back, but I wouldn’t know what had been asked. The bird was still in my mind. They called me inattentive. But it wasn’t that I couldn’t give my attention — my attention went to everything at once.

Back then I didn’t know it had a name. No one did. “This child’s a bit up in the clouds,” they’d say. I was up in the clouds, yes. But what I saw up there, no one else could see. Even my name was chosen by my grandfather; my mother told me later, “I didn’t want to say anything, no one asked me.” Before I was even born, even my name was someone else’s decision. Maybe that’s why I spent my whole life asking: who am I?

My mind never went quiet. Never. Even while I was living something, another film was running inside me: what if it had been like this, what if I’d said something else that day, what if I’d stayed, what if I’d gone. What happened, happened once — but in me it replayed a hundred times, each time with a different ending. Sometimes that film sped up so much that I was afraid I’d lose myself. A day I wrote to my mother is still in my mind: “Pray for patience for me, Mum, so I don’t lose myself, so I can keep control, pray for that.” Against that film in my head, I was sometimes so helpless that I needed a mother’s prayer. They called it “overthinking.” I call it surviving; without thinking I’d have fallen apart, and thinking wore me out. Both were true at once. With me everything was always like that — true in two ways at the same time.

I'm in Istanbul. In my father's caravan. Night. In Üsküdar. Alone.

It's coming up on three o'clock. Outside, a streetlamp burns all by itself; an orange, flickering light seeps in through the fogged window of the caravan and forms a pale stain on the ceiling. I stare at that stain. The light shifts a little every time the wind makes the glass tremble — as if it's breathing in and out.

The inside of the caravan is dim; the corners are dark, and in the middle that single orange trace. And beneath that trace, me.

The cold is merciless. It doesn't come from under the blanket; it comes from somewhere inside, starting at my skin, working its way into my organs, and down to the bone. After a certain point, being cold turns into pain — my jaw clenches, my shoulders rise toward my ears on their own. The metal walls of the caravan shiver faintly in the wind: a constant, never-ending, thin vibration. As if someone is running their fingertips over the metal and softly saying, "I feel you. You're here. You're still here."

My breath turns to mist; every exhale that leaves my mouth becomes visible for an instant in that orange light, then scatters. My hands are stiff. My fingertips are numb, and when I try to bend them they tingle. A mixed smell fills my nose: old engine oil, rusty metal, damp fabric, and beneath them a faint trace of cheap cleaner — that smell my father wiped away years ago but that never fully left. This smell makes me feel both at home and like a stranger at the same time.

Sounds come. A ferry horn in the distance — muffled, deep, stretching out across the water. Then a siren; long, sharp, tearing someone's night to pieces somewhere. A dog barks in broken bursts — lonely, tired, a bark that can tell no one its trouble. Two streets over, the tea sellers are packing up their last stalls; the metallic scrape of trays, the rattle of chains, a chair dragging against stone. Outside, life goes on with its usual indifferent rhythm. I lie there without moving. In the middle of all those sounds, it's as if I'm inside a glass dome: I hear everything, but I can touch none of it.

The phone screen lights up for a moment, showing the time. But I'm really looking at another clock — the one inside me, the one that never stops. And that clock has only one question: "How much longer?" How much longer will I fight? How much longer will I hold on? How much longer will I try to understand? How much longer will I sit alone, at night in a caravan, inside my own head?

And beside me, right here, lying in the fold of the blanket, there is something. Something that hasn't left my side for years. A question.

"Who am I?"

Inner voice: I haven't been asking this question since yesterday. I've been asking it for years. As a child at the back of the classroom, as a young man in the middle of a shift, as a husband at the edge of the bed, as a father looking into my daughters' faces… always the same question, never falling silent. But shall I be honest? I never found a clear answer. And now, inside this cold metal box, I notice something strange: maybe I'm not the one searching for an answer. Maybe, after carrying the question for so long, I became the question itself. I walk through the world like a question — open-ended, its answer deferred, but alive.

Life's Contract

For years I asked myself: "Did I understand life, or did I just memorize it by taking blow after blow?" Then I noticed something. Life runs on this small, cruel rule: whatever you want, you first get its opposite. The universe gives the exercise first and unlocks the gift afterward.

I asked for patience. What did I get? Waiting. Injustice. Wasted time. People.

★ Patience isn't learned — it's forced on you.

I wanted to be strong. What did I get? Loneliness. Helplessness. Those "there's no one here" moments.

★ True strength is born not from support, but from its absence.

I wanted an easy life. What did I get? Debts. Responsibility. Burden.

★ Money isn't freedom — it's a test.

That's when I understood: this isn't a punishment. This is… an education. The source, the universe, the system — call it whatever you like. The rule doesn't change: "I give you the opposite of what you want. If you succeed… you'll have earned it, and then it will be given."

Age 36 – The Caravan – Ground Zero

I'm 36 years old. In Istanbul. Living in my father's old caravan. I'm not a tourist. I didn't come to have fun. After my second divorce, I set out — on a long journey across the world and into research. I wanted to find myself again.

On other continents I worked intensely on neurodivergence, gathered studies and reports, and saw how different cultures relate to neurodivergent people. In that period I had my dog Happy with me, a Labrador puppy. He was about to begin training as a therapy dog to work with autistic children. He was the real reason I was able to set out: with him I didn't feel completely alone, and I had a responsibility. I could take care of him.

But the cost was heavy. During the separation in my second marriage, my cat Mokka was killed by a car. Later, here in Turkey, Happy went the same way. These losses taught me loneliness and the journey all over again. Life has a strange sense of humor: it sends you a companion so you can learn to let go — then takes them back so you really learn.

I was born in Germany, in Landau an der Isar. A small town. But for me, a great beginning. I'm a Turk with a German passport, a Bavarian-Swabian mix — even my origin is a question with more than one right answer. We are three siblings. I was the eldest. But I never truly managed to be the "big" one.

Two marriages. Both ended. Two girls from the first marriage, Ceylin and Ceysu; two more from the second, Aykız and Balkız. Four girls in all. About a year and a half lies between each sister, and nearly ten years between the children of the two marriages.

★ I never had a son. But inside me I had to raise a boy, a child who was never born.

Diagnosis: Understanding Myself for the First Time

At 32: ADHD. At 33: autism. Today they call the combination of these two AuDHD. This was no ordinary diagnosis. This was the explanation of my life.

Inner voice: Until that day I kept asking, "Why am I like this?" Then I began to say, "So this is who I am."

The puzzle pieces fell into place: why I think so fast. Why I can't focus yet see everything at once. Why I feel people so intensely. Why I trusted the wrong people. Why I always gave and got so little in return.

★ Neurodivergence is not a malfunction. It's a correct system that grew up in the wrong environment.

Love, betrayal, separations, conflict, illnesses — there were very hard lessons in my life. Some of them made me stronger. Some nearly broke me. But today I see it: all of them brought me to exactly this place. And yes… it's hard to say, but it's true: "It's good that it happened this way. Otherwise I wouldn't be the person I am today."

I lie inside a thin sleeping bag, in my father's old caravan. By choice. But the cost was heavier than I'd guessed. Outside, the wind strikes the metal wall — tap, tap, tap. As if someone is knocking on the door saying, "Open up, let's talk." I answer from within: "Not now, brother. Right now I'm talking with myself."
