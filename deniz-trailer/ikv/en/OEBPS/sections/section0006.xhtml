Chapter 2 – Childhood: The Silent Scream

The house was warm. Genuinely warm. And here is the strange part: I still remember that warmth on my skin today, yet in that house I never once felt truly "seen." A body can grow warm while the inside keeps shivering. I learned that much later — as an adult, inside a cold caravan, on a strange night when my body froze while something inside me grew warm. It was one of the many backwards lessons life had saved up for me: as a child I was warm on the outside and cold within; once I grew up, I flipped it around.

The radiator burned so fiercely that on cold winter evenings I would wedge my sock-clad feet between two of its panels. The cast iron would first sting through the sock, almost sizzling; you want to pull your foot away but you don't — because right after that sting comes something else: the warmth climbs slowly from the soles to the calves, from there to your waist, your back, up under the nape of your neck. My shoulders dropped. My jaw loosened. That constant knot in my belly came undone for a moment. And right then, my fingers stuck to the hot metal, I would say quietly to myself: "Now I am safe." A child's safety could sometimes fit inside one great big radiator.

On top of the radiator, between the iron grooves, there would be a thin line of dust; with the heat, the smell of that dry, faintly scorched dust would rise — the smell of a radiator. Even today, if I catch that same smell somewhere, my body remembers before I do: my shoulders drop. So safety has a smell, then; and I memorized it at five years old, without any words.

Inner voice: "Why can I bear the sting of the heat so well, yet I can't bear that nameless chill inside me at all?" Then I would laugh at myself: "Because you know where the radiator burns, little man. As for the chill inside, nobody ever gave you its address."

My mother's cooking smells would drift in from the kitchen. First the sharp scent of onions sweetening as they fried; then the sizzle of minced meat in oil, the deep aroma of tomato paste, a pinch of cinnamon, a bay leaf. This smell would seep under the door, travel like a trail through the cold air of the corridor, and reach the door of my room. The moment it touched my nose my stomach would clench, my mouth would water — but that hunger did not belong to the stomach alone. That smell told me, "you are here, you have a home, evening will come, the table will be set." Through smell, food spoke the reassurance that words never could. Perhaps that is why my relationship with food was always this tangled: I looked for love most of all there, on the plate.

The table would be set, plates clinking, forks rattling against full bowls. My stomach would fill. And yet, when I rose from the table, some place inside me stayed hungry — a place I couldn't name, couldn't fill. As a child you don't notice this; you only think, "would it pass if I ate a little more?" It didn't pass. Because that hunger had nothing to do with food.

Inner voice: "How can a person still feel something missing at a table that smells this good, that is filled this full?" Years later I found the answer: a plate says, "I am feeding you," but it cannot say, "I am hearing you." I had confused the two. And I went on confusing them for years.

The Voice That Always Talked, Never Asked

My mother often muttered to herself. Sometimes out loud, sometimes only a faint hum. But most of the time the phone was at her ear, and she talked on and on. The conversations never ended. She always told the same story: what she had been through, the pains she carried, what had piled up inside her. Her voice would rise and fall. I sat at the kitchen table, listening with my legs swinging. I didn't understand everything. Or rather: I didn't understand the words, but I felt them. A strange, heavy feeling would form inside me.

I remember the tone of her voice more than her words. That tremor left hanging in the air when a sentence ended. The telephone cord — phones had cords in those years — would wind around her finger, unwind, wind again. It was as if I counted every turn of that cord. The kitchen light was yellow, and beneath the lamp there was a halo like soot. Onions, tomato paste, cigarette smoke perhaps, and my mother's voice — all of it hung suspended in the same air. I was there, yet it was as if I were behind glass: I heard everything, I could touch none of it. This feeling would later become very familiar to me; in a caravan, in the night of Istanbul, I would find myself behind that exact same glass.

Inner voice: "Why does my mother always talk but never ask me anything?" Then I would get angry at myself: "Maybe I'm the one who doesn't know how to be asked." And a smaller, more insidious voice would add: "Maybe you're just not interesting enough to be asked about."

Now, as an adult, I can look at that scene with different eyes. The woman on the other end of that phone wanted to be seen somewhere too. She too was waiting to tell her own pain to someone, for someone to say, "I am hearing you." She carried a burden she couldn't bear and had found no other place to unload it. As a child I couldn't have known this — I only felt that my turn never came. Yet perhaps no one's turn ever came. Perhaps in that house everyone sat with the same hunger to be seen, only in different corners.

Inner voice: "What if one day she had sat down at the table, hung up the phone, and said, 'How was your day?' Just once." I have composed that sentence so many times that it sits in my mind like a memory that really happened. In truth it never did. But it is a strange thing, isn't it, that a sentence that never was can make you long for it this much?

The Television That Never Fell Silent, and the First Heroes

The television in the house never fell silent. The newscaster's voice, the theme music of some series, the maddening jingles of commercials… There was no silence. There was a constant drone. That drone turned into something physical in me: a tension at the nape of my neck, a pressure inside my ears, a constant "flee" feeling. Now I know its name — sensory overload. Back then I only knew that I couldn't bear it. At some point I would break and withdraw to my room. I would close the door, and that "click" of the door was the most beautiful sound in the world: the noise dropped, the pressure fell, my breath deepened. I would sit cross-legged on the bed and turn on the television.

Inner voice: "Isn't it funny? I flee the television in the living room and turn on the television in my room." But there was a difference: the sound in the living room hit me, while in my room I chose the sound myself. The issue wasn't the noise. The issue was whose hands it was in. The one noise I had control over stopped being noise at all.

Dragon Ball. Every single time, Goku got beaten, knocked to the ground covered in blood. His face battered and bruised, his breath ragged, his knees giving out. I would press right up to the screen, my nose nearly touching the glass. Because that was the critical moment for me: will he get up? Then, with that big, dumb grin, he would rise to his feet again. His hair turned gold, a golden light burst around him, his power surged. Something inside me leapt out of place; my chest swelled and I clenched my fists.

Inner voice: "How does this work? He nearly dies almost every time, yet he rises stronger. Should I be like that too? Is enduring this easy?" Then a deeper question would come: "Or is it not easy — is there just no other choice?"

Goku became my first therapist. A cartoon hero glowing blue and gold taught me what no human had: getting back up is not a matter of strength, it's a matter of stubbornness.

★ What makes a hero a hero is not that he is never struck, but that he rises every time he is struck down.

I would use this lesson many more times — in two divorces, in losses, beneath debts, in the cold of a caravan. That golden-haired boy, without knowing it, had prepared me for life.

When I first watched The Lion King, something inside me broke. The scene where Mufasa dies… Little Simba stands beside his father's lifeless body, trying to wake him. He shoves his tiny paw under the huge body and pushes, nestles against his ear, says, "Dad, come on, get up…" In that moment my throat knotted. A heavy stone settled on my chest. My eyes filled, a warm tear slid down my cheek, and I felt ashamed — so that no one would see. I had switched off the light in the room; thank goodness I had.

Inner voice: "Am I like that too? If my father died, would I feel guilty too? Would I want to run away too?" And right after, with a child's logic: "I wonder if all the bad things happen because of some mistake of mine?"

This film truly broke me. Because that little lion was me. Alone. Guilty. Not knowing what to do. Here is the interesting thing: a cartoon could touch a place in me that the real people around me never touched. Perhaps that is why I took refuge in stories — because stories expected nothing from me, they only made me feel. A screen had "seen" me more than the people across the table. Even writing this hurts; but at the same time I am grateful to those screens. Without them, perhaps I would never have learned to feel at all.

A Different Frequency

I didn't like school. I didn't like the classroom. I didn't understand people. I wanted friends. Truly. But they didn't want me. Their way of talking was different, their jokes were different, their laughter was different. I was on a different frequency. I couldn't keep up. A group of kids would laugh at something, and I would start laughing where the laughter had already ended — a second too late. That one second always left me on the outside. As if everyone had been handed a manual, and mine hadn't been handed out.

I remember the recesses: I would stand in the corner of the yard, against the wall. The sun warmed one side of me, while my other side shivered in the shade. The children's shouting, the ball slamming against the concrete, a girl's shrill scream, the sharp ring of the bell — all of it piled one on top of another. I would look at the crowd and think, "should I join in?" But I didn't know the rule for joining. How do you get into a ball game? How do you jump into the middle of a conversation? Everyone knew, I didn't. Well, actually I did know — I rehearsed it over and over in my head. It was just that my feet never matched that rehearsal.

Inner voice: "If I went over, if I said, 'can I play too?'… What if they said 'no'? What if they laughed?" And I would stop, staying there against the wall. Not even risking rejection was safer than being rejected. So even as a child I was running calculations like this. The coward's arithmetic, it seems, is learned early.

I fought constantly with my siblings. From the outside it looked as if we couldn't get along at all. Shouting, squabbling, slamming doors. But in truth I wanted to play with them. I wanted to be near them. I just didn't know how. A fight was a child's clumsiest, most painful way of saying, "look at me, I'm here too." A shove was a kind of contact after all. Maybe it was the only contact I knew: the hand that doesn't know how to touch with love touches by shoving.

Inner voice: "Wasn't I supposed to be the big brother? The eldest, right? But I feel less like the big one and more like the lost one." Being the eldest child was a crown; one that kept slipping off my head, one that never sat right.

Do you know what loneliness means at that age? It isn't silence. It's being invisible while people are all around you. Sitting in a room and still asking, "Am I really here?" In the middle of an entire house's warmth, amid all those sounds, smells, and movement — it's like standing one step behind, and no one noticing that one step.

★ The deepest loneliness is not being alone; it is being invisible in the middle of a crowd.

I didn't run away. I built my own world. There I had friends, stories, heroes. Goku was there, Simba was there, and a whole host of other heroes I had invented and told no one about. There, I even chose the color of the air. And above all: there, control was mine. The child who knew the rules to nothing on the outside was, on the inside, the god who set every rule.

Inner voice: "Here no one says to me, 'Why are you doing it like that?' Here I am as strong as I want to be." Entering that world was like stepping into a warm room. Leaving it was like going back into the cold corridor.

The house was warm. The radiator burned. The food smelled good. But inside me there was an emptiness no one saw, no one touched. And in time this emptiness grew. That emptiness grew up alongside me; it went to school, lived out its youth, got married, became a father, set out on the road. Years later, when I came face to face with that emptiness inside a cold caravan, I understood: I had fed it, carried it, given it a room. Seeing it, though — that would come much, much later.
