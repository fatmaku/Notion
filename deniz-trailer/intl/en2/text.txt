Not every child tells you the same way. Deniz speaks in this book. But some children speak little or not at all — and they still tell you: they show pictures, hand over cards, point, take your hand, or use a tablet or an AAC (augmentative and alternative communication) device. All of these are valid sentences. If your child doesn’t speak the way Deniz does, translate the story into their way: “How would you show me?” — and accept that the answer may be silence. They tell you this: small adjustments matter enormously. Moving to the front row, cutting out a tag, allowing headphones, or giving five minutes’ warning — these look small, but they change a child’s whole day.
== p001.xhtml A Note for Grown-Ups This book is not a medical resource; it offers no diagnosis and no treatment. Deniz’s adventures are inspired by the everyday lives of children who notice body signals late, who reach their limits sensorially during care routines, or who need support with transitions. A toilet accident, holding in a poop, trouble with toothbrushing or difficulty sleeping are not a child’s “disgrace”. Our aim is to give the child a safe language for listening to his body — and to give adults small tools for working together instead of applying pressure. If there is pain, blood, burning when peeing, fever, long-lasting constipation, frequently repeated accidents, marked snoring or pauses in breathing, or any difficulty that seriously affects daily life, please speak to your pediatrician, your dentist or the relevant specialist. Read the book together. Do not answer in Deniz’s place. Ask, “How is it for you?” — and accept that the answer may be a word, a picture, a gesture or silence.
== p003.xhtml GETTING TO KNOW The Body Post Office That morning Deniz stopped halfway through putting on his socks. “Pusula,” he said, “when my body wants to tell me something, why does it sometimes whisper and sometimes suddenly shout?” Pusula flew a small loop in front of the window. Tiny envelopes drifted down from her gold-patterned wings. There was no writing on the envelopes. One had a drop of water on it, one a moon, one a toilet, one a toothbrush. “Because your body is a post office,” said Pusula. “Every organ sends you letters. The bladder says, ‘I’m filling up.’ The bowel says, ‘I’m ready.’ The teeth say, ‘Clean me.’ The brain says, ‘Let’s go to sleep.’ But your mailman sometimes runs very fast — and sometimes he delivers the letter late.” Deniz held an envelope to his ear. Not a sound came out. “And what if the letter never comes?” asked Deniz. “Then we don’t wait around feeling alone,” said Pusula. “We check. Clocks, pictures, orders and small routines are the helpers of the body post office.” Deniz smiled. “So if I don’t understand my body, I’m not a bad child?” Pusula’s wings lit up. “No. You’re only just learning the map of the post office.”
== p004.xhtml 🦋 Pusula says If you don’t hear your body’s message right away, it isn’t your fault. Sometimes it helps to do a quick check instead of waiting for a feeling: “Toilet? Water? Rest? Care?” 💜 For Parents and Teachers Noticing hunger, thirst, the need for the toilet and tiredness inside your own body is called interoception . Some children feel the signal very early, others only at the last moment. Instead of “But I just asked you!”, build short check-in moments into the natural transitions of the day: before leaving the house, before arriving at school, after meals, before a long stretch of play and at bedtime. Checking does not mean forcing a child to sit on the toilet. The aim is to build body awareness — and to separate an accident from shame. A picture sequence, the same words every time and calm repetition work better than long explanations.
== p006.xhtml ADVENTURE 1 The Pee Bell That Rings Too Late Deniz was building an enormous city with his toy trains. The tracks ran under the sofa, curved around a mountain of cushions and finally reached the blue station. His mom called from the doorway: “We’re going out in a minute. Shall we do a toilet check?” “Nope!” said Deniz. “I don’t need to go at all.” And that really was how it felt. The train still needed its last bridge. How would the city work if a bridge was missing? Five minutes later Deniz suddenly pressed his legs together. The bell had gone straight from a whisper to a siren. “I NEED TO GO NOW!” he shouted. He ran. But a few drops had already gone into his pants. Deniz’s face turned bright red. “I’m not a baby,” he whispered. Pusula landed on the mirror above the sink. “An accident doesn’t make you smaller,” she said. “Your pee bell rings late sometimes. For a late bell, we ask the clock for help.” “How will the clock know when I need to go?” asked Deniz. “It won’t,” said Pusula. “It will only remind you to check with your body.” His mom brought clean clothes. She didn’t tell him off and she didn’t pull a face. “An accident. We’ll clean it up,” she said. “Next time we’ll check before we go out.”
== p007.xhtml WHAT PUSULA SAYS The next day his mom hung three pictures next to the door: shoes, toilet, door. The order for going out was now: get the shoes ready, do a toilet check, then out through the door. On the first day Deniz said, “But I don’t need to go.” “You don’t have to need to,” his mom answered. “This is only the check.” Deniz went to the toilet, listened to his body — and noticed that he did need to go a little after all. “Wow,” he said. “I found it before the bell even rang!” Pusula did a somersault in the air. “That’s called being a body detective.” For a whole week the bell rang sometimes early, sometimes late. One more accident happened. Nobody said, “Not again?” They cleaned up, made a note of it and carried on. In the end Deniz found his magic sentence: “I don’t know if I need to go. I’m going to check.”
== p008.xhtml 🦋 Pusula says If an accident happens, you don’t have to hide. You can say, “There’s been an accident, will you help me?” An accident gets cleaned up — the shame doesn’t stick to you. 💜 For Parents and Teachers Toilet accidents are usually not on purpose. A picture sequence, clothes that are easy to pull down, an easily reachable toilet, a footstool and regular check-in moments can all help. Never point it out in front of other people; use a private, neutral tone. Frequently repeated accidents, burning or pain when peeing, fever, a strong smell, peeing very often or very rarely — or new accidents in a child who was previously dry — should be checked by a doctor.
== p010.xhtml ADVENTURE 2 The Poo Train and the Waiting Station For three days Deniz had not wanted to poop. It wasn’t really not wanting to. Last time it had hurt. Now there was a train in his tummy, and every time that train came near the station, Deniz shut the gate. “I’m not coming out,” he thought. “If I come out, it will hurt again.” But the longer the train waited, the heavier the carriages became. Deniz’s tummy started to ache and he went off his food. In the middle of playing he would suddenly go up on tiptoes, squeeze his legs together and then say, “It’s nothing.” Pusula settled on the towel in the bathroom. “Why do you keep sending the poop train back?” she asked. Deniz lowered his head. “Because the tracks hurt.” This time Pusula didn’t make a joke. “Then we won’t solve this on our own. If something hurts, we tell a grown-up. Your body isn’t asking for a punishment — it’s asking for help.” Deniz went to his mom. It was hard to say, but he used the short sentence: “Pooping hurts me.” His mom took him seriously right away. “Thank you for telling me,” she said. “We’ll talk to our doctor.”
== p011.xhtml WHAT PUSULA SAYS The doctor listened to Deniz’s tummy and explained to the family what to do. She didn’t tell Deniz off. “This happens to a lot of children,” she said. At home they put a little footstool in front of the toilet. Now when Deniz sat down, his feet no longer dangled in the air. His knees could bend comfortably. After meals they had a short station time. The aim was not to poop right away. The aim was to sit calmly enough for his body to hear the train. If Deniz wanted, he looked at a picture book while he sat. When the time was up, he got up. His mom praised him for sitting and listening to his body — not for a result. A few days later the train rolled slowly into the station. When Deniz felt the relief, Pusula beat her wings. “You didn’t pull the train by force,” she said. “You made the tracks safe.”
== p012.xhtml 🦋 Pusula says If pooping hurts, don’t keep it to yourself. Say, “It hurts me.” Pain is not your fault, and asking for help is nothing to be embarrassed about. 💜 For Parents and Teachers Holding in stools, hard stools and soiling can be linked to long-standing constipation; the child is not doing it on purpose. Pressure and shaming make the holding worse. A footstool and a calm routine after meals are helpful adjustments to the environment — but the treatment plan should be decided by a pediatrician. If there are hard or very large stools, pain when passing a stool, blood, a bloated tummy, loss of appetite, several days without a stool or repeated soiling of pants, speak to your pediatrician without delay. Do not start or stop any medication without medical advice.
== p014.xhtml ADVENTURE 3 Bubble Mountain and the Bathroom Map On Sunday evening his dad said, “Bath time.” Every brake inside Deniz was pulled at once. “Now?” said Deniz. “But I’m not ready!” A bath looked like one single job. For Deniz there were a hundred jobs inside it: stop playing, take off your clothes, check the water temperature, get wet, put up with the smell of the shampoo, keep the bubbles out of your eyes, dry off, put on pajamas. “This isn’t a bath,” said Deniz. “This is an expedition up Bubble Mountain!” Pusula landed on a tile. “You don’t climb a mountain without a map,” she said. His dad drew six simple pictures on a sheet of paper: a parking space for toys, the laundry basket, a hand in the water, washing your body, a towel, pajamas. Then he gave Deniz two choices: “Blue towel or orange towel? Hair today or tomorrow?” Deniz chose the orange towel. He checked the water temperature himself with his wrist. Because the noise of the fan bothered him, they switched it off. Bubble Mountain was still high. But now he could see the path.
== p015.xhtml WHAT PUSULA SAYS Every time a step was finished, Deniz turned the card over. Toys parked. Card turned. Clothes in the basket. Card turned. Water temperature checked. Card turned. At one point the bubbles came close to his ear. Deniz’s shoulders shot up. “Break,” said Deniz. His dad turned off the water. They waited for three breaths. Then they carried on, when Deniz was ready. When the bath was over, Deniz wrapped himself in the orange towel. “I climbed the mountain myself,” he said. Pusula smiled. “Because you could see where the path went.”
== p016.xhtml 🦋 Pusula says If a big job looks like a mountain, break it into small steps with pictures. You can ask, “What’s the next step?” 💜 For Parents and Teachers Daily care is not a single action for a child but the sum of many transitions and sensory experiences. Advance warning, a picture sequence, limited choices, the right water temperature, unscented products and the right to a short break can all reduce resistance. The goal is not to give up on hygiene, but to adapt the route to the child’s nervous system. Respect the child’s privacy, explain any necessary help beforehand and leave the child the steps he can do himself.
== p018.xhtml ADVENTURE 4 The Toothbrush Dragon Every evening the toothbrush waited in the cup in the bathroom. The longer Deniz looked at it, the more the bristles looked like dragon’s teeth. The toothpaste smelled very sharp. The foam filled his mouth, and when the brush touched his gums his whole face tingled. “Two minutes,” said his mom. For Deniz, two minutes meant two hours in the dragon’s cave. One evening he ran away without even putting the brush in his mouth. Pusula settled on the edge of the mirror. “We do have to protect your teeth,” she said. “But you don’t have to fight with only one brush and only one toothpaste.” The next day they tried three soft brushes: one with a small head, one with a thick handle and one that didn’t vibrate. They asked the dentist about suitable toothpastes. Deniz chose the one with the milder taste. Then they changed the order. First Deniz brushed. Afterwards his mom asked, “Is it my turn?” — and gently went over the spots he had missed. Deniz still didn’t love it. But now the dragon had a door.
== p019.xhtml WHAT PUSULA SAYS Deniz found his own rhythm: front teeth, side teeth, back teeth. For each section, one color of the little sand timer ran out. When there was too much foam, he spat. Instead of pressing hard, he moved the brush slowly. One evening Pusula asked, “Has the dragon gone?” Deniz thought about it before he rinsed his mouth. “No,” he said. “But now I know how to talk to him.” Then he held the brush out to his mom. “Now you can check.” It never became Deniz’s favorite job. Even so, he learned that body care is sometimes not about liking it, but about finding a safe way to do it.
== p020.xhtml 🦋 Pusula says If a care job feels hard, instead of saying “I won’t do it”, have a look at “What exactly is hard?” Is it the smell, the taste, the touch or the order? 💜 For Parents and Teachers Toothbrushing is a health need and cannot be left entirely to the child’s choice; but the size of the brush, the handle, the taste of the toothpaste, the order, the use of the mirror and the way an adult helps can all be adapted. Many children need adult supervision and help with brushing for a long time. If there is pain, bleeding gums, visible decay, bad breath, or if brushing stops working altogether, consult a children’s dentist. Rather than forcing the mouth open, build a step-by-step plan with a dental practice that understands sensory needs.
== p022.xhtml ADVENTURE 5 The Haircut Space Mission When Deniz saw the barber’s chair, the back of his neck started itching before a single hair had been cut. The paper around his neck, the plastic cape, the buzzing of the clippers, the cut hair falling onto his skin … each one was its own alarm. “Only ten minutes,” said the barber. But ten different things were happening in Deniz’s body at once. Pusula landed on his shoulder. “We’ve set off on a space mission without preparing,” she said. This time they didn’t start right away. Deniz first held the clippers while they were switched off. Then he felt the vibration in his hand. The barber cut one strand and stopped. Deniz saw in the mirror what had happened. His dad opened a bag beside him: a soft cotton cape, ear defenders, a spare T-shirt, a damp cloth and Deniz’s favorite little spaceship. Deniz chose his own signal. When he raised his hand, everyone would stop. “Is mission control ready?” asked the barber. Deniz took a deep breath. “Section one is ready.”
== p023.xhtml WHAT PUSULA SAYS The haircut took three sections. Section one: the sides. Break. Section two: the back of the neck. Change of T-shirt. Section three: the top. Done. After each section Deniz stood up, shook out his shoulders and wiped his neck with the damp cloth. When he left, his hair wasn’t perfect. One side was a little longer. But Deniz walked out not in tears — he walked out having been heard. His dad asked, “What shall we change next time?” Deniz answered right away: “No paper tape. And the back of my neck first.” Pusula spread her wings. “That’s the mission report!”
== p024.xhtml 🦋 Pusula says Before you go somewhere difficult, it helps to look, to touch, to try out the sound and to choose a break signal. Preparing is not cowardice — it’s clever courage. 💜 For Parents and Teachers During a haircut the sensory load arrives through many channels at once. Preparation with photos or video, a quiet appointment time, a practice run at home, a cotton cape, spare clothes and a cut divided into sections can all help. Instead of “It’s nothing”, recognize that the child is experiencing five separate things at the same time. Don’t aim for a salon-perfect result every time. Trust and bodily boundaries are worth more than a symmetrical haircut.
== p026.xhtml ADVENTURE 6 The Night Shift of the Brain That Won’t Sleep Night had come. The house was quiet. But Deniz’s brain was not quiet. He thought about the picture he had painted that morning. He thought about tomorrow’s trip. He thought about whether dinosaurs dream. Then he thought, “What if I can’t get to sleep?” — and that thought woke up all the other thoughts. “Close your eyes,” said his mom. Deniz closed them. The lights in his brain shone even brighter. Pusula settled on the pillow. “Some brains don’t have an off switch,” she said. “They need a landing strip.” Together they made a night shift plan. After the exciting game, bed would not come right away. In between there would be a bridge: toilet, teeth, dim light, a short book, parking tomorrow’s questions on a piece of paper — then bed. Deniz drew his question, “Do dinosaurs dream?”, as a picture on the paper. He put it in the “look it up tomorrow” box. Once his brain understood that the question wouldn’t be lost, it slowed down a little.
== p027.xhtml WHAT PUSULA SAYS On the first night the plan didn’t work yet. Deniz turned over again, got up again, asked for water again. His mom didn’t say, “The plan has failed.” She said, “Your body is still learning.” On the third night Deniz followed the order himself. Teeth, book, question park, bed. When he was in bed, Pusula taught him a game: squeeze your toes, let go. Squeeze your legs, let go. Squeeze your hands, let go. Then feel how the bed is carrying you. Deniz didn’t chase after his thoughts. He let them drift past. After a while one of the lights on the night shift went out. Then another one. In the morning his mom asked, “How did you sleep?” Deniz yawned. “My brain ended the shift without telling me.”
== p028.xhtml 🦋 Pusula says When sleep is hard, don’t be cross with yourself. Sleep is not a butterfly you catch; it settles by itself in a calm place you have made ready. 💜 For Parents and Teachers A predictable routine that runs in a similar order every night helps the body understand what comes next. Instead of an abrupt jump from stimulating activity into bed, build a calm bridge. Rather than forcing the child to sleep, make the environment sleep-friendly. If there is long-lasting sleeplessness, clear difficulty functioning during the day, very loud snoring, pauses in breathing, night-time pain or intense anxiety, speak to your pediatrician.
== p030.xhtml ADVENTURE 7 Not a Morning Race, but Morning Stations Mornings always took Deniz by surprise. He had only just got out of bed and already somebody was saying, “Come on!” “Come on, get dressed.” “Come on, breakfast.” “Come on, your bag.” “Come on, your shoes.” Every “come on” threw another ball at his brain. Deniz didn’t know which one to catch. He would put on one sock, then spot a toy on the floor, then go for a drink of water, then forget why he was standing in the kitchen. One day the school bus arrived and Deniz had only one shoe on. “I’m so slow,” he said sadly. Pusula flew above the door. “You’re not slow. Your stations are getting muddled up.” That evening the family set up four stations around the house. A dressing basket next to the bed. A care card in the bathroom. The breakfast place in the kitchen. A parking spot for the bag and shoes by the door. In the mornings the whole house would no longer shout at once. Only the next station would speak.
== p031.xhtml WHAT PUSULA SAYS The first station was getting dressed. The clothes had been in the basket since the night before. The second station was face and teeth. The third station was breakfast. The fourth station was the door: bag, coat, two shoes. If Deniz moved on to the next station before finishing the one before, his mom didn’t give a long lecture. She simply pointed at the card. One morning Deniz arrived at the door station and stopped. He looked at both of his shoes. Then he burst out laughing. “Today you both came along!” He didn’t have to run for the bus. He walked there in good time. Pusula landed on his cap. “The morning was never a race,” she said. “It just didn’t have a map.”
== p032.xhtml 🦋 Pusula says If lots of “come ons” are muddling you up, you can say, “Just tell me the next step, please.” 💜 For Parents and Teachers Morning difficulties usually come not from unwillingness but from a combination of sleep inertia, sense of time, working memory and switching between tasks. Laying out clothes the night before, fixed places for belongings, one-step instructions and a visual route work better than verbal pressure. Measure success not by “he didn’t dawdle at all”, but by his being able to follow the sequence with less and less outside support.
== p034.xhtml ADVENTURE 8 The Door of My Body Belongs to Me After school a distant relative came over and opened his arms wide. “Come here, let me give you a great big hug!” said the relative. Deniz stepped back. He didn’t want to be hugged. But the sentence “That would be rude” popped into his head. When the relative came one step closer, Deniz’s body froze. He could say neither “yes” nor “no”. Pusula landed on Deniz’s cap. “You’re the guard at the door of your body,” she whispered. Deniz looked at his mom. She came straight over to him. “Deniz doesn’t want a hug today,” she said. “He can wave, do a high five, or just say hello.” Deniz chose to wave. At home his mom explained two kinds of secrets. Secrets that end in happiness, like a birthday surprise. And secrets that sit heavy inside you, that frighten you, or where somebody says, “Don’t tell anyone.” “A secret that sits heavy inside you is not kept,” said his mom. “You tell me, your dad, your teacher or another grown-up you trust. You will never get into trouble for it.”
== p035.xhtml WHAT PUSULA SAYS His mom explained the private parts of the body to Deniz in an age-appropriate way, using the correct names. She told him that the places covered by a swimming costume are private. “Sometimes a doctor or a carer helps for health or hygiene reasons,” she said. “But they explain first what they are going to do. When it’s possible, they ask permission. If it feels uncomfortable, you can say so. A grown-up you trust will be there with you.” Then they played a game. His mom said a situation and Deniz chose the safe sentence: “Can I have a hug?” — “Not right now, thank you.” “Don’t tell anyone this secret.” — “I tell people about secrets that sit heavy inside me.” “Come on, let’s go without telling your mom.” — “I’ll ask a grown-up I trust first.” Pusula drew a golden door. She gave the key to Deniz. “The door of your body belongs to you,” she said. “Love never forces a door open.”
== p036.xhtml 🦋 Pusula says If you don’t want a hug, you can say “no”. And if you freeze, that isn’t your fault either. You can tell a grown-up you trust about it afterwards. 💜 For Parents and Teachers Bodily autonomy is learned in everyday life: never making a child kiss anyone, stopping tickling the moment “stop” is said, and offering alternative greetings. Teach children the correct names for private parts, the difference between safe secrets and secrets that weigh on them, and help them name more than one trusted adult. If a child tells you about an uncomfortable touch, listen without interrogating, say that you believe him, stress that it is not his fault, and get professional support. In the US you can contact the Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline (1-800-422-4453); in the UK, the NSPCC Helpline (0808 800 5000) or Childline (0800 1111, free and confidential for children). Your school’s safeguarding lead, school counselor or local children’s services can also help.
== p038.xhtml THE ENDING I Know the Language of My Body Pusula laid the envelopes of the body post office out in front of Deniz again. The pee bell. The poop train. Bubble Mountain. The toothbrush dragon. The haircut mission. The night landing strip. The morning stations. The body door. “Have you solved them all?” she asked. Deniz shook his head. “No. Sometimes I still notice too late. Sometimes I don’t want a bath. Sometimes sleep doesn’t come.” Pusula smiled. “Then what have you learned?” Deniz looked at the envelopes. “That I’m not bad. My body has a language. If I can’t hear it, I check. If something is hard, I say so. If something hurts, I ask for help. And I guard the door of my body myself.” Pusula’s wings painted golden paths on the walls of the room. “That’s the instruction manual,” she said. “Not knowing every answer — but knowing that we look for the answer together.”
== p040.xhtml GAME My Own Body Post Office Together with your child, make five cards with no writing on them: Toilet symbol — “Do I want to check right now?” Water and food symbol — “Does my body need fuel?” Moon/pillow symbol — “Do I need a rest?” Brush/towel symbol — “Which part of getting ready is hard?” Heart-and-door symbol — “Is my body saying yes, no, or I don’t know?” The child can use a card by speaking, pointing, drawing or simply choosing. The aim is not to find the right answer, but to start a conversation with the body. “My body is my home. I’m learning its messages and I guard its door.”
