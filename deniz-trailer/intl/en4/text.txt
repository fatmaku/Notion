All rights reserved. This book is not a medical resource. It does not diagnose, does not recommend treatment, and is not a substitute for professional assessment. Deniz’s adventures are inspired by the everyday lives of neurodivergent children and do not describe any particular child. If you have concerns about your child, speak to your paediatrician, a child and adolescent mental health professional, a psychologist, or your school’s support team. Not every child tells you the same way. Deniz speaks in this book. But some children speak little or not at all — and they still tell you: they show pictures, hand over cards, point, take your hand, or use a tablet or an AAC (augmentative and alternative communication) device. All of these are valid sentences. If your child doesn’t speak the way Deniz does, translate the story into their way: “How would you show me?” — and accept that the answer may be silence. The purple boxes: The purple box at the end of each adventure is the For Parents and Teachers ; in a few sentences it explains to parents and teachers what is going on behind the scenes. The series: My Neurodivergent Compass — a five-book children’s series. Book 1 · My Neurodivergent Compass Book 2 · Deniz and the Secret Messages of His Body Book 3 · Deniz Finds His Own Way at School Book 4 · Deniz and the Friendship Compass Book 5 · Deniz and the Island of Changes The adventures in this book don’t tell you about big solutions. They tell you this: small adjustments matter enormously. Moving to the front row, cutting out a tag, allowing headphones, or giving five minutes’ warning — these look small, but they change a child’s whole day.
== p001.xhtml A Note for Grown-Ups Friendship is not only talking a lot, making eye contact or joining in a big noisy game. Laughing together, doing separate things at the same table, taking turns to share what you know, and hearing each other’s “stop” — those are friendship too. While you read this book: Do not turn it into a test of “which is the correct social behavior?” Think about what Deniz needs and what the other child needs, together. Never force a child into eye contact, hugs or crowded games. Try the short sentences in role play, at a calm moment. Compass for grown-ups: The aim is not for the child to hide himself, but for him to be able to build relationships that are clear, safe and mutual.
== p003.xhtml GETTING TO KNOW The Invisible Speech Bubbles Deniz was watching two children in the park. One of them said, “You are such a potato!” The other one roared with laughter. Deniz was puzzled. Nobody looked anything like a potato. Then a child said, “Come over if you want.” But he turned his face the other way. When Deniz came closer, the game stopped. “Did he really want me to come?” he thought. “Or didn’t he?” The words that came out of people’s mouths sometimes went in a straight line, sometimes curved, and sometimes carried a meaning you couldn’t see at all. Pusula landed on Deniz’s shoulder. “Does the social world feel like a foggy forest?” she asked. Deniz nodded. “Everyone else has a map. I haven’t.” “The map may be missing,” said Pusula. “But you have your compass: ask, check, set your boundary — and listen to the other person too.” 🗝️ Your sentence “Can I ask what you meant by that?”
== p004.xhtml WHAT PUSULA SAYS Four Directions in Friendship Pusula opened her wings to all four sides: Ask: If you didn’t understand, ask for an explanation. Say: Tell people what you want, or what you don’t want. Listen: Notice the other person’s words and their boundaries. Move away and get help: If it isn’t safe, you don’t have to stay in the game. Deniz said, “Can I do all of these every single time?” “No,” said Pusula. “Friendship isn’t a performance. It’s practice.”
== p005.xhtml 💜 For Parents and Teachers Instead of judging a social situation once it is over, break it into small pieces: What was said? What did Deniz understand? What might the other person have understood? Which short sentence would help next time? Generate possibilities without imposing one single “correct reading”. A child’s quietness, side-by-side play or need for a rest is not a lack of friendship. Mutuality, safety and willingness matter far more than outward appearance.
== p007.xhtml ADVENTURE 1 Joke or Real? At lunchtime Ece looked at Deniz’s enormous sandwich. “If you eat that, you’ll get as big as an elephant!” she said. Deniz put the sandwich down. He looked at his tummy. Elephants were big. What if something really did happen? Ece laughed. “I was joking.” Deniz’s face went hot. He was cross with himself for not getting the joke. Pusula landed on the fruit box. “Guessing whether something is a joke can be hard,” she said. “You don’t have to guess.” Deniz turned to Ece. “Did you say that for real, or was it a joke?” “A joke,” said Ece. Then she stopped. “Did it scare you?” “A bit,” said Deniz. “Could you say it’s a joke first?” Ece nodded. “All right. Elephant joke coming up: this sandwich is enormous!” This time Deniz was ready. He could laugh if he wanted to — or he could simply say, “I see.” 🗝️ Your sentence “Did you say that for real, or was it a joke?”
== p008.xhtml WHAT PUSULA SAYS You Are Always Allowed to Check the Meaning Pusula taught three checking sentences: “Is that real, or a joke?” “What did you mean by that?” “I know it was a joke, but I didn’t like it.” Deniz asked, “Do I have to laugh at a joke?” “No,” said Pusula. “The point of a joke is to enjoy it together. If only one person is enjoying it and the other one is being made smaller, it needs to stop.”
== p009.xhtml 💜 For Parents and Teachers Never say “How can you not get it?” to a child who finds hints, teasing, idioms or tone of voice hard to read. Make it normal to state your intention out loud. In class, work on the difference between a joke and a put-down with one question: “Are both sides safe, and do both sides want this?” When a child takes something literally, do not laugh at him. Give reassurance first, then show the two possible meanings.
== p011.xhtml ADVENTURE 2 The Three Doors into a Game Out in the playground the children were playing space pirates. Deniz wanted to join in. He ran straight into the middle of the game. He picked up a shiny stone from the ground. “This is a Mars crystal now!” he said. One child shouted, “No! That was our treasure!” Deniz stepped back. It felt as though the door to the game had shut in his face. Pusula drew three invisible doors on the playground. First door: Watch. Second door: Ask. Third door: Offer a role. Deniz watched for a while. He noticed that the children were guarding the treasure. “Can I join the game?” he asked. “I could be the space mapmaker.” One of the children said, “We do need a mapmaker,” and handed him a piece of chalk. Deniz drew a planet path on the ground. This time he hadn’t fallen into the game — he had come in through the door. 🗝️ Your sentence “Can I join the game? I could do this role.”
== p012.xhtml WHAT PUSULA SAYS Joining In Can Be Learned — and Being Turned Down Can Happen Pusula asked, “If you use the three doors, does everyone have to say yes?” “I wish,” said Deniz. “Sometimes a game is full,” said Pusula. “Sometimes children want to play on their own. A ‘no’ does not mean you are worth less.” They made a back-up plan: Ask about a different role. Look for another game. Start your own game. Take a short break.
== p013.xhtml 💜 For Parents and Teachers “Go and make friends” is far too wide a task. At a calm moment, act out watching, choosing the right moment, a short opening sentence and the back-up plan. Rather than always placing the child inside a game an adult has set up, build small groups around shared interests. Do not leave repeated exclusion filed away as the child’s own social skills gap. Look at the group dynamic and the school environment as well; adult support may be needed. If the exclusion is persistent or tips into bullying, ask the school for its anti-bullying policy in writing and work through it with the class teacher, the playground supervisor or the school counselor. Free confidential help is available: in the US, the Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline (call 1-800-422-4453), plus the guidance at StopBullying.gov; in the UK, Childline on 0800 1111.
== p015.xhtml ADVENTURE 3 The Rocket Waiting Its Turn In class everyone was talking about the weekend. Deniz remembered the red fire engine he had seen on Saturday. The siren, the ladder, the wheels... he wanted to tell them all of it, right now. But there were still five people before his turn. The sentence inside Deniz worked like a rocket. The longer he waited, the hotter its engine got. While another child was speaking, Deniz burst out: “I saw a red fire engine!” The child went quiet. When Deniz looked at his friend’s face he knew something had gone wrong — but the sentence rocket had already launched. Pusula gave him a small rocket card. Deniz drew a picture of the fire engine and put the card in front of him. “Your idea can wait here,” said Pusula. When his turn came, Deniz looked at his card. The rocket was still there. First he said to the child who had been speaking, “I’m sorry I interrupted you.” Then he told his own story. 🗝️ Your sentence “I’m going to leave myself a sign so I don’t forget my idea.”
== p016.xhtml WHAT PUSULA SAYS To Wait, Put Your Idea Somewhere Safe “Waiting your turn is not only about being quiet,” said Pusula. “It’s about making room for someone else without losing your idea.” Deniz chose three tools: Draw or write one single word. Hold up one finger as a “say it later” sign. At the right moment, say, “When you’ve finished, can I tell mine too?”
== p017.xhtml 💜 For Parents and Teachers Instead of a long lecture at the moment of the interruption, use a silent signal you agreed on beforehand. Give the child a reliable structure that shows his turn really is coming. In circle time, a talking object, a name order or a visual turn card can all help. Teach a short repair after a mistake: “I interrupted you. Please carry on.” Repair allows a child to take responsibility without being shamed.
== p019.xhtml ADVENTURE 4 My Rule, Our Game Deniz and Arda were building a toy train. As far as Deniz was concerned, the blue carriage always belonged right behind the engine. That was the fastest way. Arda put the blue carriage at the very end. Deniz put it straight back. Arda moved it to the end again. “You’re doing it wrong,” said Deniz. “It’s my train too,” said Arda, and he walked away from the game. Deniz was baffled. Why would you do it another way when there was a right way? Pusula drew two tracks. One had a “my choice” symbol on it, the other a “shared choice” symbol. Deniz went over to Arda. “I like the blue carriage at the front. Where do you want it?” “At the back.” They built two trains. Then they brought the trains together at a station in the middle. Deniz’s rule hadn’t been lost. And Arda’s game hadn’t been squashed. 🗝️ Your sentence “I want it like this. How do you want it?”
== p020.xhtml WHAT PUSULA SAYS In a Shared Game There Are Two Brains Pusula showed three play areas: My area: My own set-up, with my own things. Your area: My friend’s choice, with my friend’s things. Shared area: Decide by taking turns, by drawing lots, or by combining the two ideas. Deniz said, “Do I have to like every change?” “No,” said Pusula. “But in a shared game you do need to see where the other brain fits.”
== p021.xhtml 💜 For Parents and Teachers Flexibility does not mean the child giving up everything he wants. First understand which part matters to him; then try one small change, offered as a choice. Instead of “stop being stubborn”, ask, “Why is this rule important to you?” Use clear boundaries in shared play: a personal set-up can be protected, while decisions in the shared section are taken in turn. Timed turns and a clear point at which things are handed back feel safer than forced sharing.
== p023.xhtml ADVENTURE 5 The Earthquake Inside Me When I Lose At playtime Deniz and his friends had a tower race. Deniz’s tower toppled just as it was about to get really tall. Ece’s tower stayed standing. “I won!” said Ece. Inside Deniz, the ground shook. His hands went hot. His ears roared. He didn’t want to build a new tower — he wanted to knock every single block down. He threw one block hard at the floor. Pusula did not say “calm down”. First she showed him the safe space. Deniz took two steps back, pushed his hands against the wall and breathed out strongly three times. The earthquake slowed down. Then he looked at the game. “Losing was really hard for me. Throwing the block wasn’t right.” Ece asked, “Do you want another round?” Deniz checked his body. “Not now. In a little while.” That was a choice too. 🗝️ Your sentence “Losing was hard for me. I’m going to take a break first.”
== p024.xhtml WHAT PUSULA SAYS Feelings Are Free — Doing Harm Is Not “You’re allowed to be sad about losing, to be cross, or not to want another go,” said Pusula. “You just need a plan so that people and things don’t get hurt.” Deniz’s earthquake plan: Take one step away from the game. Give your hands a safe job: push the wall, squeeze a ball. Say the short sentence. Once you’re calm, repair it or choose again.
== p025.xhtml 💜 For Parents and Teachers Do not push a child to be a “good loser” at the most intense moment of the game. Help the body settle first. Teach the loser to ask for a break safely, just as carefully as you teach everyone to congratulate the winner. Practice with short, low-stakes games at a calm time. Alongside games whose outcome is unknown, make room for cooperative games too. The goal is not for the child never to be upset; it is for him to stay with the feeling without doing harm.
== p027.xhtml ADVENTURE 6 Is She Cross with Me? The Evidence Detective In the morning Ece said a quick “hello” to Deniz and went straight into the classroom. An alarm went off in Deniz’s head. “She’s cross with me. I talked too much yesterday. She doesn’t want to be my friend any more.” All day long, everything Ece did got tied to that thought. When she sat with somebody else, the alarm grew louder. Pusula put on a detective’s hat. “Your thought matters,” she said. “But a thought and a piece of evidence are not the same thing. Let’s open the file.” They made two columns. She might be cross: She only spoke briefly. There might be another explanation: She was in a hurry, she was tired, the teacher called her. Deniz asked Ece, “You only said a quick hello this morning. Are you cross with me?” “No,” said Ece. “I’d forgotten to finish my homework. I was in a real panic.” The alarm switched off. Sometimes the alarm might turn out to be right. But a detective collects the evidence first. 🗝️ Your sentence “My head is telling me you’re cross with me. Is that true?”
== p028.xhtml WHAT PUSULA SAYS Listen to the Thought, Then Check It Pusula asked four detective questions: What did I see or hear? What meaning did my head give it? Could there be two other explanations? If it’s safe, how could I ask? Deniz said, “And what if she really is cross?” “Then you look for an open conversation and a way to repair it,” said Pusula. “Somebody being cross with you does not mean you are a bad person.”
== p029.xhtml 💜 For Parents and Teachers Do not close down a child’s fear right away with “don’t be silly, nobody’s cross with you”. Accept the feeling first, then separate the observation from the interpretation. Make “what happened?” concrete: Which words? Which face? When? In safe relationships, teach direct and kind checking sentences. If the child seeks constant reassurance about every social signal, the aim is not to supply endless answers, but to build — slowly — his skill at gathering evidence and sitting with uncertainty.
== p031.xhtml ADVENTURE 7 Dinosaur Rain and the Talking Ball Deniz loved dinosaurs. He brought his brand-new Ankylosaurus fact into class. Ece said, “I went swimming on the weekend.” Deniz said, “The Ankylosaurus had a club of bone on the end of its tail.” Arda said, “I’ve got new glasses.” Deniz said, “Some dinosaurs had rings of bone around their eyes.” After a while, everyone went quiet. Deniz had so much to tell them, and he couldn’t work out why nobody was listening. Pusula rolled a small talking ball. “Your rain of facts is wonderful,” she said. “But in a friendship, the ball goes back and forth.” Deniz turned to Ece. “What did you like best about swimming?” Ece told him. Then she asked, “So what was that tail club actually for?” Deniz’s dinosaur knowledge hadn’t gone anywhere. And now there was an ear turned toward him. 🗝️ Your sentence “I’ve told you mine. Now I want to listen to you.”
== p032.xhtml WHAT PUSULA SAYS A Special Interest Is a Bridge — It Doesn’t Have to Cover the Whole Road Pusula showed the three moves of a two-way conversation: Tell one piece. Ask a question. Say something that connects to the answer you get. Deniz asked, “How many minutes am I allowed to talk for?” “It depends on the person and the moment,” Pusula answered. “You can ask, ‘Would you like to hear about this?’”
== p033.xhtml 💜 For Parents and Teachers Do not treat a special interest as an obsession to be silenced. It can be a source of learning, connection and self-confidence. At the same time, teach the signals of two-way conversation openly: asking permission, keeping a section short, pausing, asking a question, and saving the rest for another time. Setting up a trusted telling-time at home — a “five-minute expert’s platform” — honours the child’s knowledge and can ease the pressure of treating every moment as the only chance to tell someone.
== p035.xhtml ADVENTURE 8 We’re Friends Side by Side Too It was “friendship day” in class. The teacher asked pairs to make a picture together. Deniz and Arda sat at the same table. Deniz wanted to draw a spaceship. Arda wanted to line up his little cars quietly. The teacher said, “Play together, you two.” They both tensed up. Deniz hunted for something to talk about. Arda drew his arms in around his cars to protect them. Pusula laid a long sheet of paper across the middle of the table. On one side she drew a space road, on the other a city road. In the middle she left a shared bridge. Deniz drew his ship. Arda drove his cars along the city road. For a while they didn’t speak. Then Arda drove one car across the bridge. Deniz added a small garage to his ship. They both smiled. Nobody talked non-stop. Nobody forced their eyes round to look at the other. And still, they were together. 🗝️ Your sentence “Can we do separate things side by side?”
== p036.xhtml WHAT PUSULA SAYS There Is No Single Shape of Friendship Pusula drew four kinds of friendship: Talking together Playing the same game Doing different things side by side Resting quietly together “Which one is real friendship?” asked Deniz. “Every shape where both people are safe, seen and willing,” said Pusula.
== p037.xhtml 💜 For Parents and Teachers Instead of pushing children into constant face-to-face, high-intensity interaction, offer side-by-side, low-pressure activities with shared materials. Drawing together, a Lego table, collecting things outdoors or quiet reading can all be more reachable ways in to a relationship. Do not measure friendship only by “how many friends has he got?” One safe relationship — where the child can relax, where his boundary is heard and his interests are met halfway — is worth a great deal.
== p039.xhtml THE ENDING My Friendship Compass Is Working In the park the children set up a game again. Somebody made a joke. Deniz didn’t understand it, so he asked. One game was full. Deniz chose his back-up plan. Arda put the blue carriage somewhere else. They built two separate trains. Deniz wanted to talk about dinosaurs for a long time. First he said, “Would you like to hear about it?” Sometimes his friends said “yes”. Sometimes they said “not now”. And sometimes Deniz said “no” himself. Pusula asked, “So do you know all the rules of the social world now?” Deniz laughed. “No. The rules keep changing anyway.” “Then what do you know?” asked Pusula. “If I don’t understand, I ask,” said Deniz. “I say what I want. I listen to the other person. And if I’m not safe, I move away and get help.” Pusula’s four directions shone like gold. “Being a friend doesn’t mean losing myself — it means making room for each other.”
== p041.xhtml GAME Friendship Compass Cards Make four cards with no writing on them, one for each direction: The asking direction: A question mark — “Can I ask what you meant?” The saying-myself direction: A speech bubble — “I want this / I don’t want this.” The listening direction: Two ears and a talking ball — “It’s your turn now.” The safety direction: An open door and a grown-up — “It didn’t stop. I’m going to get help.” Act out short situations with a toy. Let the child pick just one card; don’t expect him to solve every situation from beginning to end. Grown-ups can model their own mistakes too: “I interrupted you. Now I’m going to listen.” 💜 A closing question for grown-ups “In this relationship, are we expecting the child alone to adapt — or are we making room on both sides?”
