Deniz’s adventures are inspired by the everyday lives of neurodivergent children and do not describe any particular child. If you have concerns about your child, speak to your pediatrician, a child and adolescent mental health professional, a psychologist, or your school’s support team. Not every child tells you the same way. Deniz speaks in this book. But some children speak little or not at all — and they still tell you: they show pictures, hand over cards, point, take your hand, or use a tablet or an AAC (augmentative and alternative communication) device. All of these are valid sentences. If your child doesn’t speak the way Deniz does, translate the story into their way: “How would you show me?” — and accept that the answer may be silence. The purple boxes: The purple box at the end of each adventure is the For Parents and Teachers ; in a few sentences it explains to parents and teachers what is going on behind the scenes. The series: My Neurodivergent Compass — a five-book children’s series. Book 1 · My Neurodivergent Compass Book 2 · Deniz and the Secret Messages of His Body Book 3 · Deniz Finds His Own Way at School Book 4 · Deniz and the Friendship Compass Book 5 · Deniz and the Island of Changes The adventures in this book don’t tell you about big solutions. They tell you this: small adjustments matter enormously. Moving to the front row, cutting out a tag, allowing headphones, or giving five minutes’ warning — these look small, but they change a child’s whole day.
== p003.xhtml Welcome Deniz and Pusula the Butterfly This is the story of Deniz. Deniz is seven years old, and inside his head he carries a super brain. What’s a super brain, you ask? It’s this: Deniz’s eyes see everything — even the tiniest crumb on the carpet. His ears hear everything — even the refrigerator’s song. Yes, refrigerators sing. If you can’t hear it, your ears are simply a different kind, that’s all. Deniz’s skin feels a lot, too — the tag in his sweater, the seam in his sock, the temperature of the water. Some children never notice these things; for Deniz, every one of them is huge. One day, a blue butterfly landed on Deniz’s window. Its wings sparkled and shimmered. “Hello, Deniz,” said the butterfly. “My name is Pusula. I find children with super brains, and I teach them the instruction manual for their amazing heads.” Deniz’s eyes grew big and round. “My brain is super? But everyone keeps telling me ‘you’re too loud,’ ‘why won’t you eat,’ ‘sit still’…” Pusula laughed. When butterflies laugh, their wings tremble — did you know that? “That’s exactly why I’m here,” said Pusula. “You have super powers, but nobody has ever shown you the settings. Are you ready? The adventure begins!” 🦋 “Being different doesn’t mean being broken. Being different means coming with a different super power.” 💜 For Parents and Teachers Read this book together; every adventure is a scene from everyday life. If your child asks, “Is that me?”, the loveliest answer is: “It does sound like you, doesn’t it? Tell me — how is it for you?” This book is a conversation starter — not a lesson.
== p005.xhtml Adventure 1 Super Eyes and the Disco Supermarket On Saturday, Deniz went to the supermarket with his mom. He stopped right at the door. The lights were so bright. And they were blinking — on and off, on and off, faster than anyone could see. But Deniz could see it. The shelves were bursting with colors, there were words everywhere, and the checkouts went beep-beep-beep. “Mom,” said Deniz, “this place is like a disco!” His mom laughed. “A disco? Sweetheart, it’s just a plain old supermarket.” Pusula landed on Deniz’s shoulder and whispered, “Your mom is right — for her eyes, it is plain. But your eyes are SUPER. You see everything — even the flicker of the lights. That’s why the supermarket makes you tired. And being tired is nothing to be ashamed of; super machines get warm when they do lots of work.” “So what do I do?” Deniz asked on the inside. (He could talk to Pusula without making a sound — that’s another power super-brain kids have.) “You take a break,” said Pusula. “Tell your mom, ‘My eyes are tired.’ That’s a magic sentence.” Deniz tried it. “Mom, my eyes are tired.” His mom stopped, looked at Deniz, and held out her hand. “Come on, let’s wait outside — Dad can finish the shopping.” Outside, it was calm. Deniz took a deep breath. Once his super eyes had rested, the world was beautiful again. 🦋 Pusula says If your eyes see a whole lot, it’s normal for them to get tired. Saying “My eyes are tired” is the magic sentence that comes before crying and shouting. Try it! 💜 For Parents and Teachers This is called “sensory overload”: a brain that takes everything in without a filter genuinely works harder in places like supermarkets. Instead of “Come on, you’re fine,” take a short break — five minutes of quiet beats half an hour of meltdown. Teaching a child to say “I’m tired” is one of the most valuable skills there is.
== p007.xhtml Adventure 2 Super Ears and the Forty-Seven Radios On Monday morning, the teacher was explaining something in class. But Deniz’s ears were full of other sounds too: the radiator was gurgling. An eraser was squeaking in the back row. A bird was singing out in the yard. Footsteps in the hallway… “Deniz!” said the teacher. “You’re not listening to me!” Deniz felt sad. Because he was listening. He was listening to everything at once — and that was exactly the problem. Pusula glided in through the window. “There are forty-seven radios inside your head,” she said. “All of them on at the same time. Your teacher’s voice is just one of those radios. Other kids have a dial: they keep one radio playing and turn all the rest down. You don’t have that dial — you have SUPER EARS instead.” “But my teacher gets cross…” “Because she doesn’t know. Let’s tell her a secret: sitting in the front row turns some radios down. And remember — you can listen even while you’re looking out the window. Some kids listen with their eyes. You listen with your ears!” When he got home, Deniz told his mom all about it. The next day she went to school and explained how Deniz’s ears work — the forty-seven radios. So the teacher, the school counselor and the principal sat down together. They agreed on small adjustments for Deniz — not just in the classroom, but across the whole school: Deniz moved to the front row, he was allowed to wear headphones at recess — his “hero helmet” — and on loud days there was a quiet corner he could go to. With the hero helmet on, the radios grew quieter, and the teacher’s voice grew big and clear. 🦋 Pusula says You can listen without looking someone in the eye — your ears are super! And wearing headphones is nothing to be embarrassed about; that’s your hero helmet. 💜 For Parents and Teachers Saying “Look at me!” doesn’t make listening easier for a child who hears without a filter — it makes it harder: eye contact eats up processing power. If he looks away, he is often listening better. A front-row seat, permission to wear headphones, a gentle tap on the shoulder before you say his name: three small adjustments, one enormous difference.
== p009.xhtml Adventure 3 The Itchy Tag Monster On Tuesday morning, there was a war at home. The reason: a new sweater. “Come on, put it on,” said his dad. “Look how lovely and soft it is.” But Deniz knew better. Right in the collar of that sweater sat a TAG MONSTER. A teeny-tiny monster with pointy teeth, and oh, how it itched. Nobody could see it — but Deniz’s super skin could feel it. Scritch, scritch, scritch! “It’s itchy!” said Deniz. “There’s nothing there,” said his dad. Pusula landed on Dad’s shoulder (Dad had no idea, of course) and winked at Deniz: “Show your dad the scissors.” Deniz fetched the scissors. “Dad, if we cut the tag out, the monster goes away. I promise I’ll wear it then.” His dad raised one eyebrow… then he laughed and cut the tag out. Deniz put the sweater on. The monster was gone! Pusula fluttered her wings. “Did you notice?” she said. “You fetched the scissors. That was a sentence too.” “But I talked,” said Deniz. “You did. Some children tell you with words. Some don’t speak at all — and they still tell you: they show a picture, hand you a card, point with a finger, take your hand and lead you there. A fetched pair of scissors, an offered card, a picture pointed at … those are all sentences. What matters isn’t that it comes out of your mouth — it’s that the person with you understands.” That evening, Deniz was sad; it had been a hard day at school. He asked his dad, “Daddy, will you hug me TIGHT? But tell me first.” His dad was surprised at first — a light touch always bothered Deniz, but a tight hug… He tried it. A great big, tight, long hug. The whole storm inside Deniz slowed down. “You’re my charger, Daddy,” said Deniz. 🦋 Pusula says Your skin is super: it feels even the tiniest things. Cutting out tags is nothing to be ashamed of! And here’s a secret: a light touch tickles, but a TIGHT hug charges you up. Ask people to warn you first — “Hug coming!” 💜 For Parents and Teachers Don’t say “There’s nothing there” — on his skin, there is; in these children, the getting-used-to-it signal never arrives. Cut out the tags, buy seamless socks. And know the paradox: an unannounced light touch is an alarm, expected deep pressure is medicine. Asking “Can I give you a hug?” takes two seconds — and turns touch into medicine.
== p011.xhtml Adventure 4 The Food Fortress and the Ketchup Rule Deniz’s plate has rules. Rule one: the ketchup does NOT touch the sausage. Rule two: the rice stays on its own side. Rule three: if everything is the same every day, everything is all right. When his aunt came to visit, she didn’t know the rules. “Just try one bite,” she said. “You’ll starve!” Deniz looked at the plate. The food looked soft and slimy. His mouth was saying “NO!” before it even got close. Pusula whispered from behind the fruit bowl: “Deniz, did you know there’s super skin inside your mouth, too? The feel of some foods sets off its alarm. You’re not being naughty — your mouth is like a guard at the gate: it won’t let in anyone it doesn’t know.” “Will it always be like this?” “No! The guard just needs time to meet the new food. First, let it sit at the edge of your plate — just sit there. Tomorrow, you give it a sniff. The day after, maybe a tiny little touch. No hurry at all.” Deniz told his mom. His mom smiled at the aunt: “Deniz has his safe foods; the new ones come as guests, little by little.” And nobody made Deniz eat. And do you know the funny part? Two weeks later, Deniz tried that “slimy” food all by himself. Just a fingertip’s worth. Not bad at all! 🦋 Pusula says Loving the same food every day is nothing to be ashamed of — that’s your safe food. Let a new food sit at the EDGE of your plate first, like a guest. You can’t force a friendship — not with people, and not with food. 💜 For Parents and Teachers Picky eating is usually about texture, not taste — the inside of the mouth is “super skin” too. “He’ll eat when he’s hungry enough” doesn’t work with these children: hungry + alarm = panic. Keep the safe foods steady and let new foods be pressure-free “guests at the edge”: sitting → sniffing → touching → maybe tasting. It can take weeks; that is the way.
== p013.xhtml Adventure 5 The Traffic Light in Your Tummy On Wednesday afternoon, Deniz suddenly got very, VERY angry. He cried because his Lego broke, he yelled at his little brother, he slammed the door. Then he surprised even himself: “Why did I do that?” Pusula came and pointed a wing at Deniz’s tummy: “There’s a traffic light in your tummy, but its bulb is turned way down low. The hungry light, the thirsty light, the tired light… They’re glowing, but you can’t see them. Then all of them turn BRIGHT RED at once and — BOOM!” “So… I was hungry?!” “Try it and see. Go to the kitchen.” Deniz ate a banana and drank a big glass of water. And… like magic: the world fixed itself. The Lego looked fixable. His little brother wasn’t so annoying after all. “WOW,” said Deniz. “I wasn’t angry. I was hungry!” From that day on, Deniz’s family had a game: whenever Deniz got grumpy, they asked three questions first — Water? Food? Break? Most of the time, the answer was one of those three. 🦋 Pusula says When you feel very angry, try this first: drink a glass of water, eat a little snack, rest for a bit. Sometimes anger is really hunger — wearing a costume! 💜 For Parents and Teachers Inner signals (hunger, thirst, needing the toilet, tiredness) come through faintly in these children — it’s called “interoception.” An explosion “out of nowhere” is usually an inner announcement arriving late. Instead of “What’s wrong?”, run the checklist: water → snack → toilet → quiet corner. Half of all crises get solved at the kitchen table.
== p015.xhtml Adventure 6 The Rocking Dance When Deniz is happy, he jumps and flaps his hands like a bird. When he’s thinking, he rocks back and forth. It feels so good — the jumble inside him untangles as he rocks. But one day at the playground, a boy laughed: “Look at him, rocking like that — how weird!” Deniz froze. His hands slid into his pockets. He didn’t rock at all that day. And by evening he was full to the brim — a Deniz who can’t rock had been collecting a storm. Pusula landed on the end of his bed: “I’m going to tell you a secret. Everybody rocks. Grown-ups jiggle their legs, click their pens, twirl their hair, squeeze a stress ball, crack their knuckles. Waves rock in the sea, trees rock in the wind — even the Earth spins! Your rocking is a lullaby your body sings to itself. Is a lullaby something to be ashamed of?” “No…” “No! The boy who laughed just didn’t know, that’s all. When someone doesn’t know, you teach them — you don’t feel ashamed.” The next day at the playground, the same boy stared again. This time, Deniz didn’t stop. “This is my super charging dance,” he said. “Try it if you like — it feels amazing.” The boy was surprised at first… then he tried it. The two of them rocked together, and laughed. 🦋 Pusula says Rocking, jumping, flapping your hands — that’s your charging dance. It’s how your body calms itself down. Not shameful, not broken: SUPER. 💜 For Parents and Teachers This is called “stimming”: the nervous system regulates itself through rhythmic movement — suppress it, and tension piles up and the storm grows. Don’t block it; if needed, find forms that fit the setting (a stress ball, ankle circles). And teach your child that one sentence: “This is my charging dance.” Naming things is the best vaccine against shame.
== p017.xhtml Adventure 7 Storm Day and the Calm Harbor Thursday was a bad day. School was crowded, his jacket got soaked, his favorite pencil went missing, and somebody yelled very loudly on the school bus. It was as if there were a balloon inside Deniz — and with every bad thing, it puffed up a little more. It puffed, and puffed, and puffed… And at home, when his little brother accidentally knocked over his tower — POP! The balloon burst. Deniz cried, he screamed, he threw the pillows. The sounds were too much, the lights were too much, everything was TOO MUCH. Even his words disappeared; Deniz couldn’t talk anymore. His mom came in. She didn’t shout. She didn’t even say “Calm down.” She dimmed the light. She took the little brother out of the room. And she said only this: “I’m here. You’re safe.” Then she waited. Storms settle when you wait for them — his mom knew that. Slowly, slowly, the balloon went down. Deniz snuggled up close to his mom. His mom asked, “Would you like some water? Can I hug you?” Deniz nodded. One tight hug. One glass of water. The storm was over. Pusula was watching from the window, her wings glowing with happiness: Deniz’s mom was a calm harbor. And a ship with a calm harbor comes safely out of every storm. 🦋 Pusula says If the balloon inside you is puffing up, say so: “My balloon is puffing up!” Ask for a break. And if the balloon bursts — it is not your fault. The storm passes. It always passes. 💜 For Parents and Teachers A meltdown is not a tantrum; it’s a full system overflowing — it wants no audience and makes no deals. In the storm: few words (“I’m here. You’re safe.”), dim the lights, lower the noise, ask before touching, and afterwards no lecture — just water and warmth. Your calm is contagious; this is called “co-regulation.” Talk tomorrow, when everyone’s tank is full.
== p019.xhtml Adventure 8 The Day We Touched the Clouds On Friday evening, Deniz was looking at the sky through the window. “Daddy,” he said, “can we touch the clouds?” His dad was just about to say “We can’t, sweetheart, clouds live up in the sky”… when he stopped. Because at that very moment, Pusula had landed right on the tip of his nose. (Dads can’t see butterflies, but they can feel them.) His dad thought about it: a cloud is really just millions of tiny water drops floating in the air. Fog is made of tiny water drops too. And the fog on a mountaintop… is a cloud! “You know what,” said his dad, “we can touch them. This weekend, we’re going up a mountain.” On Saturday morning, they set off. The road was long. Deniz asked for two breaks, and once he said, “My balloon is puffing up” — so they stopped, and the balloon went down. And at last… they stood on the top of the mountain, right inside the fog. Deniz opened his hands to the air. The cloud flowed between his fingers. It was cool and wet. “DADDY!” Deniz shouted. “CLOUDS ARE WET!” His dad laughed, and that evening he wrote a single sentence in his notebook: “I’m so glad I didn’t say ‘we can’t.’” 🦋 Super-brain kids don’t believe in “impossible.” They ask, “Why not?” And sometimes that question carries you all the way to the clouds. 🦋 Pusula says Your questions are precious. Never, ever be ashamed of asking “Why?” Everything that makes the world beautiful began with somebody asking, “Why not?” 💜 For Parents and Teachers This story is true: the author drove 30,000 km in a camper van because his son wanted to touch the clouds. Before every “no,” pause for one second: is it really impossible — or just unusual? Answered well, a super-brain child’s questions turn into a compass.
== p021.xhtml Farewell Are You a Super-Brain Kid Too? Pusula fluttered her wings at the window that evening. Sometimes she would visit other children, sometimes she would appear beside Deniz, and sometimes Deniz would find her voice in a sentence within himself. “But I’m leaving something with you,” she said. “What?” asked Deniz. “Your instruction manual. You know it now: your eyes are super, your ears are super, your skin is super. You’ve learned to check your traffic light, to do your charging dance, to announce your balloon. And most important of all: being different doesn’t mean being broken.” 🎈 Game: Find your own super powers! Answer these questions together — there’s no right or wrong, there’s only YOU: 1. Do you sometimes hear sounds that nobody else hears? 👂 2. Are tags and itchy clothes your enemies? 👕 3. Could you eat your favorite food every single day? 🍝 4. Do you jump and flap your hands when you’re happy? 🦘 5. Do you sometimes love one thing SO much that you learn everything about it? 🦖 How many times did you say “yes”? Every “yes” is a super power. And listen: your “no” answers are super too — because every brain is super in its own way. 🦋 “We’re different, not less.” — Never forget that. Pusula is always with you. 💜 For Parents and Teachers — farewell If you’ve read this book, you have given your child the greatest gift there is: a language for understanding themselves.
