Adventure 2

Super Ears and the Forty-Seven Radios

On Monday morning, the teacher was explaining something in class. But Deniz’s ears were full of other sounds too: the radiator was gurgling. An eraser was squeaking in the back row. A bird was singing out in the yard. Footsteps in the hallway…

“Deniz!” said the teacher. “You’re not listening to me!”

Deniz felt sad. Because he was listening. He was listening to everything at once — and that was exactly the problem.

Pusula glided in through the window. “There are forty-seven radios inside your head,” she said. “All of them on at the same time. Your teacher’s voice is just one of those radios. Other kids have a dial: they keep one radio playing and turn all the rest down. You don’t have that dial — you have SUPER EARS instead.”

“But my teacher gets cross…”

“Because she doesn’t know. Let’s tell her a secret: sitting in the front row turns some radios down. And remember — you can listen even while you’re looking out the window. Some kids listen with their eyes. You listen with your ears!”

When he got home, Deniz told his mom all about it. The next day she went to school and explained how Deniz’s ears work — the forty-seven radios.

So the teacher, the school counselor and the principal sat down together. They agreed on small adjustments for Deniz — not just in the classroom, but across the whole school: Deniz moved to the front row, he was allowed to wear headphones at recess — his “hero helmet” — and on loud days there was a quiet corner he could go to.

With the hero helmet on, the radios grew quieter, and the teacher’s voice grew big and clear.

🦋 Pusula saysYou can listen without looking someone in the eye — your ears are super! And wearing headphones is nothing to be embarrassed about; that’s your hero helmet.
💜 For Parents and TeachersSaying “Look at me!” doesn’t make listening easier for a child who hears without a filter — it makes it harder: eye contact eats up processing power. If he looks away, he is often listening better. A front-row seat, permission to wear headphones, a gentle tap on the shoulder before you say his name: three small adjustments, one enormous difference.
