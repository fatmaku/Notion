== p000.xhtml My Neurodivergent Compass · Book 3 of 5 Deniz Finds His Own Way at School Adventures about Learning, Homework and School Courage — for Neurodivergent Children (Autism, ADHD) Author: Tuncay Sancak (HappyTuncay) · happytuncay.com · Instagram @happytuncay © Tuncay Sancak. All rights reserved. This book is not a medical resource. It does not diagnose, does not recommend treatment, and is not a substitute for professional assessment. Deniz’s adventures are inspired by the everyday lives of neurodivergent children and do not describe any particular child. If you have concerns about your child, speak to your paediatrician, a child and adolescent mental health professional, a psychologist, or your school’s support team. Not every child tells you the same way. Deniz speaks in this book. But some children speak little or not at all — and they still tell you: they show pictures, hand over cards, point, take your hand, or use a tablet or an AAC (augmentative and alternative communication) device. All of these are valid sentences. If your child doesn’t speak the way Deniz does, translate the story into their way: “How would you show me?” — and accept that the answer may be silence. The purple boxes: The purple box at the end of each adventure is the For Parents and Teachers ; in a few sentences it explains to parents and teachers what is going on behind the scenes. The series: My Neurodivergent Compass — a five-book children’s series. Book 1 · My Neurodivergent Compass Book 2 · Deniz and the Secret Messages of His Body Book 3 · Deniz Finds His Own Way at School Book 4 · Deniz and the Friendship Compass Book 5 · Deniz and the Island of Changes The adventures in this book don’t tell you about big solutions. They tell you this: small adjustments matter enormously. Moving to the front row, cutting out a tag, allowing headphones, or giving five minutes’ warning — these look small, but they change a child’s whole day.
== p001.xhtml A Note for Grown-Ups This book does not claim that all children learn the same way. Some children learn more easily by listening, some by seeing, some by moving — and many through a mix of all three. While the child is listening to the story, they may: point to a moment that happened to them too, try out the short sentence when they feel ready, choose a symbol if they would rather not speak, read one adventure again and again. Three questions for grown-ups: What can the child not do yet? Which part of the task is invisible or too big? Which small change in the surroundings would raise the chance of success? Important note: If there are lasting concerns about learning, attention, language, vision, hearing, motor skills or safety at school, the family, the teachers and a suitable specialist should look at it together. This book does not diagnose; it strengthens the child’s voice.
== p003.xhtml GETTING TO KNOW The First Morning in School City Deniz stopped at the school gate. Inside, bells were ringing, shoes were running, doors were opening and closing. His mom said, “You can go to your classroom now.” But the word “can” opened a huge empty space in Deniz’s head. Which door? Where does the bag go? When does the coat come off? What do you say to the teacher? Where is the toilet? And what if the bell rings? The other children walked as if they were carrying an invisible map. Deniz was carrying only his backpack. Right then a blue butterfly landed on his orange hat. “I’m lost,” Deniz whispered. Pusula opened her wings. “Being lost is not the same as failing. Maybe your map just isn’t visible yet.” “Where do I find the map?” asked Deniz. “By asking, by looking, and by leaving yourself small signs,” said Pusula. Pusula flew ahead. Deniz followed her. School City was still big — but now he had his first sign. 🗝️ Your sentence “Can you show me where to go first, please?”
== p004.xhtml WHAT PUSULA SAYS Not Knowing the Way Is Not the Same as Not Being Able Pusula unfolded a small map of the school. It had only five pictures on it: The door The coat pegs The classroom The toilet A grown-up I trust “You don’t have to learn the whole school in one day,” said Pusula. “You can learn the way you need today.” Deniz drew a little star next to the picture of his teacher. “What’s that?” asked Pusula. “The person I go to if I get lost,” said Deniz. “That is exactly what school courage is,” said Pusula. “Not knowing everything on your own — but knowing who to turn to.”
== p005.xhtml 💜 For Parents and Teachers School rules are clear to grown-ups and often invisible to children. Before the first days, or when a child moves to a new class, prepare a short walk-through with photos, a simple picture of how the day runs, and a card showing “people you can ask for help”. Instead of “you know that already”, show it once, practice it together, then slowly fade the prompt. If a child is very frightened of school, is left out over a long period, or complains of tummy aches and headaches, do not read it as simple reluctance. Talk with the child and the school early, in concrete terms and without blame.
== p007.xhtml ADVENTURE 1 The Invisible Map of the First Day On Monday the class teacher said, “Get your exercise books out and start the day.” Zips opened all around the room. Pencils came out onto the tables. Chairs scraped. Deniz stayed where he was. Which movement was “starting the day”? The math book? The English book? Was he supposed to write the date? Or listen to the teacher? The child next to him was already writing. Deniz felt his cheeks go warm. When the teacher said, “Come on, Deniz, we’re waiting for you,” the path in his head got even more tangled. Pusula popped her head out from behind the pencil case. “When one sentence has lots of doors in it, you’re allowed to ask about the first door,” she said. Deniz put his hand up. “Please could you tell me the first thing to do?” The teacher paused. She drew three small pictures on the board: blue book, date, a listening ear. Deniz took out the blue book. The first stone of the path was in place. 🗝️ Your sentence “Please could you tell me the first thing to do?”
== p008.xhtml WHAT PUSULA SAYS A Clear Instruction Is an Open Door Pusula said, “For your brain to understand a task, it wants to know three things.” What am I doing? What am I doing it with? How will I know I’m finished? Deniz looked at the three pictures on the board. “I take out the book. I write the date. Then I stop and listen.” “Yes,” said Pusula. “Now the way is visible.”
== p009.xhtml 💜 For Parents and Teachers Instead of wide instructions like “get ready”, “do it properly” or “pull yourself together”, name one step that can be seen: “Put the blue book on the table.” Check understanding not with “Did you understand?” but with “What’s the first step?” Use a three-part visual sequence, always in the same place on the board: now — next — done. This support does not only help Deniz; it often helps the whole class.
== p011.xhtml ADVENTURE 2 The Start Button and the One-centimeter Step Deniz came home. There was a worksheet on the table. The sheet had ten questions on it. But Deniz did not see ten questions — he saw a mountain reaching up over his head. He picked up his pencil. Put it down. Drank some water. Straightened his hat. Looked for his eraser. Stared out of the window. His dad said, “Come on, just start.” Deniz wanted to start too. But the start button was hiding. Pusula landed on the table and covered most of the sheet with a blank page. Only the name line was showing. “Don’t look at the mountain — look at the one centimeter in front of your shoe,” she said. Deniz wrote his name. Pusula slid the paper down a little. Only the first line of the first question appeared. Deniz read it. “Have I started now?” “Yes,” said Pusula. “Starting isn’t always finishing. Sometimes it’s just the first move anyone can see.” 🗝️ Your sentence “Can you show me the tiniest first step?”
== p012.xhtml WHAT PUSULA SAYS Before Motivation Comes the Starting Bridge Deniz put three small stones next to his worksheet. First stone: Write my name. Second stone: Read the first question. Third stone: Try one answer. After each step he dropped a stone into a little box. The “clink!” told him he was moving forward. Pusula said, “Sometimes the moving comes first, and the wanting follows it.”
== p013.xhtml 💜 For Parents and Teachers Not being able to start is not always defiance. The task may be too big, too vague, too dull, or it may stir up a fear of getting it wrong. Shrink the first step until the child can manage it, and work calmly beside them for a few minutes. You could call this “body company”: the grown-up does not do the work instead of the child; they are simply a presence that carries the starting. Say “let’s start together for two minutes” instead of “finish it all”. For longer homework, use short work-and-rest cycles and a visible finish line.
== p015.xhtml ADVENTURE 3 The Lost Planets in the Backpack The next morning the teacher said, “Take out your trip permission slips.” Deniz’s stomach jumped. He had held the slip in his hand yesterday. Then he had put his water bottle in. Then something had happened... but what? He opened his backpack. Inside was a small universe. A sock planet. Two crumpled paper asteroids. A pencil rocket with no lid. Half a sandwich moon left over from last week. There was no permission slip. A knot tightened in Deniz’s throat. “I always forget,” he said. “You are not your forgetting,” Pusula answered. “You just haven’t built the place where your things come home to yet.” Together they made three stations inside the backpack: a clear folder for school papers, a box for pencils, a separate section for food. By the door they hung a small leaving picture: folder, bottle, bag. The next day the permission slip came straight out of the clear folder. 🗝️ Your sentence “Where does this one live?”
== p016.xhtml WHAT PUSULA SAYS To Remember, Put a Sign Outside Your Head “Sometimes your brain keeps the information but doesn’t open the door at the right moment,” said Pusula. “Signs are the doorbell.” Deniz did not write three rules; he used three pictures: Paper that comes home goes straight into the clear folder. Finished homework goes straight into the bag. At night the bag goes straight beside the door.
== p017.xhtml 💜 For Parents and Teachers Instead of a long telling-off for every forgotten item, make the system visible. Give things a fixed home, use color coding, a bag with few compartments and a short check picture by the door. Plan a weekly “bag reset” time. Packing the bag for the child every time solves the problem in the short run, but it can leave the skill invisible. Fade the support in stages: first together, then with the picture only, and finally an independent try.
== p019.xhtml ADVENTURE 4 The Time Bubble At recess Deniz was drawing a spaceship. The teacher said, “We’re going inside in five minutes.” Deniz nodded. Five minutes felt like a long road to him. He drew the ship’s wing. He added flames to the engine. He made a new planet. Then the bell rang. “But I’ve only just started!” Deniz shouted. Everyone else was already in line. His five minutes had gone “pop” like a bubble. Pusula brought an hourglass. At first the top part was full. As the sand ran, the time that was left could be seen. Two minutes before the end, the teacher showed Deniz a finishing card. Deniz drew one last window on the ship and put the paper in his “I’ll carry on” folder. The bell rang again. This time, time did not shove him from behind; he walked across the bridge in front of him. 🗝️ Your sentence “If I have to stop before I’m finished, will you tell me beforehand?”
== p020.xhtml WHAT PUSULA SAYS “In a Minute” Can Be Made Visible Pusula said, “The number on the clock and the time inside your body are not the same thing.” For Deniz, a change-over had three signs: Five minutes left. Two minutes left; choose your last piece. Stop now — take a photo of it, or put it in the carry-on folder.
== p021.xhtml 💜 For Parents and Teachers Make time concrete with more than words: a visual timer, an hourglass, a piece of music or a finishing card. Instead of “stop right now”, give the child a chance to close the activity safely. When leaving a special interest is hard, an “I’ll carry on” folder, a photo, or showing the next time on the calendar can help a great deal. A transition warning is not a negotiation; it is preparation time for the nervous system. The boundary can stay exactly the same — the way to it just becomes more visible.
== p023.xhtml ADVENTURE 5 The Dancing Letters At reading time it was Deniz’s turn. The lines in the book were straight. But when Deniz started to read, the letters did not want to turn into sounds. The “b” and the “d” looked so alike that it took him a moment to tell which sound belonged to which. Long words looked like trains with their carriages muddled up. Deniz stopped at the very first word. Someone at the back giggled. Deniz wanted to close the book. “I can’t read,” he thought. Pusula laid a small reading window over the page. Only one line showed through. The teacher said, “If you like, look it over quietly first. Then you can read just two sentences.” Deniz followed the line with his finger. He read the first sentence slowly. On the second one he got stuck. “Let’s break this word up together,” said the teacher. Once the word was in small pieces, the carriages lined up again. 🗝️ Your sentence “I’d like to follow the line and have a bit of time to get ready.”
== p024.xhtml WHAT PUSULA SAYS Reading Is Not an Intelligence Race “If working out one word takes a lot of energy, there may be less energy left for understanding the story,” said Pusula. Deniz listened to the same story first. Then he followed the text with wider spaces between the lines. He noticed that he understood the story very well indeed. “So the story was in my brain all along,” said Deniz. “Of course it was,” said Pusula. “You just needed a different way to reach the door.”
== p025.xhtml 💜 For Parents and Teachers Never let reading aloud become a source of shame or a speed race. Try supports such as preparation time, a shorter section, a line window, clear and generously spaced print, following along with an audiobook, and extra time. Look at the child’s decoding skill and their comprehension as two separate things. Where the difficulty persists, do not stop at “try harder” — this may be dyslexia. Start the school’s support process (SEN support, an IEP or 504 plan, depending on your system) and ask for assessment by an appropriate specialist. Reasonable adjustments and accommodations are not a privilege; they are the way in to learning.
== p027.xhtml ADVENTURE 6 The Hand Whose Pencil Gets Tired The teacher wrote a long sentence on the board. Deniz understood it. In fact, he had an even better sentence in his head. But the moment his pencil touched the paper, his hand slowed down. Fitting the letters onto the line, spacing the words, putting the period in the right place... each one was a separate job. After a while his fingers ached. The board was about to be wiped. Deniz was only halfway. “I didn’t finish in time,” he said — and nobody heard the lovely sentence in his head. Pusula pointed to the little voice-recording button on the teacher’s desk. “You can tell me your idea first,” said the teacher. “Then you write your short part.” She also gave him a printed copy of the board. Deniz said the sentence that had been in his head. His classmates turned round to listen. His pencil might be slow, but his idea was quick and bright. 🗝️ Your sentence “Can I say my idea out loud first?”
== p028.xhtml WHAT PUSULA SAYS Knowing and Being Able to Write It Down Can Be Two Different Skills Pusula laid three ways of telling in front of Deniz: Writing it Saying it Drawing it and then explaining it “But don’t you have to write at school?” asked Deniz. “You’ll keep on learning to write,” said Pusula. “And at the same time you can have a bridge that shows what you know.”
== p029.xhtml 💜 For Parents and Teachers Long copying from the board may not measure what a child knows; it may only measure visual-motor speed. Talk through options with the school team: printed notes, less copying, extra time, a keyboard, spoken answers, or speech-to-text support. Do not brush aside a child’s hand pain or heavy tiredness. If difficulties with motor skills, vision or learning continue, ask for a proper assessment. The aim is not to take writing away from the child, but to stop their knowledge staying locked up while they are still learning to write.
== p031.xhtml ADVENTURE 7 Before the Answer in My Head Escapes In science the teacher asked, “Why do bees visit flowers?” Deniz knew the answer. Bees, pollen and bright flowers went flying through his mind. He forgot to put his hand up. “For honey!” Deniz called out. “Don’t interrupt,” said the teacher. Deniz went quiet. At the next question he raised his hand. This time the teacher chose someone else. Deniz felt the answer about to escape, and he spoke out again. Pusula left a small “park my idea” card on his desk. Deniz drew one single word from his answer on the card: flower. He put his hand up. While he waited, he looked at his card. The answer wasn’t escaping any more. When his turn came he said, “While the bee collects nectar, it carries pollen too.” The teacher smiled. “How did you keep hold of your idea this time?” Deniz held up the card. 🗝️ Your sentence “I’m going to leave myself a sign so I don’t forget my idea.”
== p032.xhtml WHAT PUSULA SAYS Open a Channel for the Wish to Join In “Holding on to an answer that pops into your head can be hard,” said Pusula. “That does not mean you have nothing to say.” Deniz and his teacher agreed on three ways: Put your hand up. Write or draw one single word on the card. If it is really important, show the silent “help” sign. The teacher also began giving the whole class thinking time on some questions, then a share-with-your-partner stage, then saying it to the class.
== p033.xhtml 💜 For Parents and Teachers Do not read frequent interrupting only as rudeness. Impulse control, working memory and waiting skills may all be under strain at once. Teach the expected behavior in advance, briefly and visually; do not only correct it in the moment it goes wrong. A few seconds of thinking time before answers, jotting a note on a small card, sharing with a partner, and offering other ways to take part will let far more children be heard.
== p035.xhtml ADVENTURE 8 The Bullying Alarm and the Safe Three At recess three children took Deniz’s hat. “Give it back,” said Deniz. One of them threw the hat to another. “We’re only joking.” Deniz did not laugh. His tummy went tight. When he tried to get his hat back, they copied the way he moved. A grown-up called from a distance, “Play nicely, everyone.” But the hat still wasn’t Deniz’s. “Maybe I’m making too much of it,” Deniz thought. Pusula spoke in a serious voice. “If it keeps happening, if it hurts you, and if it doesn’t stop when you say stop — then you are allowed to sound the help alarm. That is not telling tales.” Deniz remembered his safe three: his class teacher, the playground supervisor and the school counselor. He went to the playground supervisor. “They’re taking my hat and throwing it. I said stop and they didn’t stop. Will you come with me?” The teacher went with him, got the hat back, and wrote down what had happened. Over the next few days she checked in again. Deniz saw that he had not been left on his own. 🗝️ Your sentence “I said stop and it didn’t stop. Will you come with me?”
== p036.xhtml WHAT PUSULA SAYS Asking for Help Is Not Telling Tales “Telling in order to get somebody into trouble is one thing,” said Pusula. “Telling in order to keep someone safe is quite another.” Deniz broke his account into four parts: Who? What did they do? Where and when? What do I need now? “They took my hat. It happened at break. They didn’t stop. I need a grown-up to come with me.”
== p037.xhtml 💜 For Parents and Teachers Do not tell a child “just ignore it”, “hit them back” or “you should have stood up for yourself”. Listen first, make them safe, write down the concrete details, and follow the school’s process through. Do not treat one conversation as the end of it; ask the child again over the following days. If the child finds it hard to tell you what happened, use drawings, a body map, place-and-time cards or multiple-choice questions. In immediate danger, take the child out of the situation and set the setting’s safeguarding procedure in motion. Ask the school for its anti-bullying policy in writing. Free confidential help is also available: in the US, the Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline (call 1-800-422-4453), plus the guidance at StopBullying.gov; in the UK, Childline on 0800 1111.
== p039.xhtml THE ENDING My Way Is a Way Too At the end of the school year the teacher asked the class, “What have you learned about school this year?” One child said, “My times tables.” Another said, “The layers of the Earth.” Deniz thought about it. “I’ve learned how to find my way,” he said. The class looked at him. Deniz went on: “If I don’t understand a task, I ask for the first step. If I can’t see time, I use a timer. If my idea won’t fit into my pencil, I say it out loud. If someone hurts me, I go to a grown-up I trust.” Pusula opened her wings in front of the window. School City was still big. On some days the paths still got tangled. Some tasks were still hard. But now, before Deniz said “I can’t do it”, he asked himself a different question: “Which way does my brain take to get to this one?” “My way may be different — it still arrives at learning.”
== p041.xhtml GAME My School Wayfinding Bag The child, the parents and the teacher make five small cards together: First-step card: A footprint — “What is the tiniest first step?” Time card: An hourglass — “I want to see how much time is left.” Idea card: A lightbulb and a parking space — “I’ll park my idea here.” Break card: A quiet corner — “I need a short break to sort myself out.” Safety card: Three grown-ups — “It didn’t stop. I need help.” The cards are not introduced in the middle of a telling-off, but at a calm moment, and tried out in role play. If the child can speak, they say the sentence; if speaking feels hard, they show the card. The teacher knows in advance what each card means. 💜 A closing question for grown-ups “Does the child need to try harder — or does the way need to become more visible?”
