Deniz’s adventures are inspired by the everyday lives of neurodivergent children and do not describe any particular child. If you have concerns about your child, speak to your paediatrician, a child and adolescent mental health professional, a psychologist, or your school’s support team. Not every child tells you the same way. Deniz speaks in this book. But some children speak little or not at all — and they still tell you: they show pictures, hand over cards, point, take your hand, or use a tablet or an AAC (augmentative and alternative communication) device. All of these are valid sentences. If your child doesn’t speak the way Deniz does, translate the story into their way: “How would you show me?” — and accept that the answer may be silence. The purple boxes: The purple box at the end of each adventure is the For Parents and Teachers ; in a few sentences it explains to parents and teachers what is going on behind the scenes. The series: My Neurodivergent Compass — a five-book children’s series. Book 1 · My Neurodivergent Compass Book 2 · Deniz and the Secret Messages of His Body Book 3 · Deniz Finds His Own Way at School Book 4 · Deniz and the Friendship Compass Book 5 · Deniz and the Island of Changes The adventures in this book don’t tell you about big solutions. They tell you this: small adjustments matter enormously. Moving to the front row, cutting out a tag, allowing headphones, or giving five minutes’ warning — these look small, but they change a child’s whole day.
== p001.xhtml A Note for Grown-Ups When a child cries because the plan has changed, cannot come away from a screen, or tears up the paper over one small mistake, it is not always about getting his own way. His brain may be struggling to build the crossing, to carry the not-knowing, or to slow the speed of the feeling down. This book holds three things at the same time: The feeling is real. The boundary can still be real. The child needs a bridge between the feeling and the boundary. A compass for grown-ups: In the moment of overflow — few words, safety and settling. In the calm moment — teaching and planning together. If there is long-lasting, intense anxiety, a risk of harm to himself or others, a clear loss of everyday functioning, or a sudden change in behavior, ask for proper support. Speak to your pediatrician or the school counselor and student support team. For a child or a parent who needs to talk something through confidentially: in the US, the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (call or text 988); in the UK, Childline is free on 0800 1111.
== p003.xhtml GETTING TO KNOW The Island Whose Map Changes Every Day There was a calendar in Deniz’s room. Across Saturday he had drawn an enormous picture of the park. On Saturday morning it started to rain. “The park’s off,” said his dad. “We’re staying in today.” Deniz looked at the calendar. Then at the window. Then back at the calendar. “But there’s the park,” said Deniz. “It’s raining,” said his dad. “But there’s the park!” It was as if the road in front of him had dropped into the sea. He didn’t know where to put his foot. Pusula came flying in between the raindrops. “Welcome to the Island of Changes,” she said. Deniz frowned. “I didn’t want to come.” “I know,” said Pusula. “We mostly arrive here without buying a ticket. But we can pack you a bridge bag to carry with you.” 🗝️ Your sentence “The plan has changed. I need to see the new one.”
== p004.xhtml WHAT PUSULA SAYS A Change Makes Sense in Three Pieces Pusula divided a card into three. Old plan: The park. Why it changed: Rain, and staying safe. New plan: Build a den indoors; put the park on the calendar for another day. “Do I have to like the new plan?” Deniz asked. “No,” said Pusula. “You can be sad and still cross over to the new plan.” Deniz did not throw the park picture away. He moved it along to Sunday. The lost thing might not come back — but he had found it a place inside the not-knowing.
== p005.xhtml 💜 For Parents and Teachers Instead of “What’s the big deal?”, name the part that has changed, plainly. Where you can, show the old plan, the reason and the new plan visually. Do not make false promises about something that cannot be replaced; make room for the sadness. A child does not need unlimited control. Offer one small choice: “In the new plan, den first or a warm drink first?” The choice does not undo the change; it gives the child a branch to hold on to while he crosses.
== p007.xhtml ADVENTURE 1 Plan Changed! The Crossing Bridge There was going to be an art lesson at school. Deniz had put his paint box in his bag that morning. The teacher came into the room. “The art teacher can’t come in today. We’re doing PE instead.” The class cheered. Deniz did not. He had already got everything ready in his mind: the smell of the paints, the space picture he was going to make, the order of things on the table. Now everyone was standing up and chairs were scraping back fast. Deniz covered his ears. “No! It’s art time!” Pusula left three pictures on his desk: a gentle line through the paintbrush, a trainer, and ten minutes of drawing at the end of the day. “You’re sad that art was canceled,” said the teacher. “Right now it’s PE. Your paint box is safe. In the last lesson I’ll keep ten minutes for drawing.” Deniz did not cheer up right away. First he did three strong wall pushes in the corridor. Then he put his sneakers on. The bridge had not rubbed the feeling out. It had given him somewhere to cross. 🗝️ Your sentence “Can you show me the old plan, the new plan and when it’s happening?”
== p008.xhtml WHAT PUSULA SAYS News First, Then the Bridge, Then a Choice Pusula put three tools into the change bag: News: Say what has changed, briefly and honestly. Bridge: Show the very first move between the old and the new. Choice: Offer two options inside one small part of the new plan. “What if there’s no time to warn me?” said Deniz. “Then the grown-up talks less, keeps you safe, and shows you the first solid step,” said Pusula.
== p009.xhtml 💜 For Parents and Teachers Do not bring the change card out only in a crisis; rehearse it with small, safe changes. “Plan A / Plan B” language can help. Deliberately springing surprises every day, though, tends to build mistrust rather than tolerance. If the child has already overflowed, cut back on long explanations and persuading. Short information, a visual new plan, help with settling the body and time to do it all work better. Once he is calm, look together at what actually helped.
== p011.xhtml ADVENTURE 2 The Case of the Missing Red Pencil Deniz wanted to draw a rocket with his red pencil. He opened his pencil case. There was no red pencil. He looked on the table. He looked in his bag. He looked on the floor. His heart started to race. “Someone’s taken it!” said Deniz. “I’m always losing my things!” He tipped the pencil case out. He pulled his notebooks onto the floor. The harder he looked, the less his eyes seemed to see. Pusula blew a detective’s whistle. “Stop the searching storm. Let’s open the case file in order.” Place one: The last table you used. Place two: The pencil’s home. Place three: Pockets and floor along the way. Step four: Ask one person. Deniz checked the art table first. Nothing. He opened the narrow side pocket of his pencil case. The red pencil was there. Deniz took a deep breath. “My eyes had looked, but my brain hadn’t seen.” 🗝️ Your sentence “I panicked. I need a search order.”
== p012.xhtml WHAT PUSULA SAYS Searching Is a Plan Too “The lost thing may be small,” said Pusula, “but the alarm in your body can be big.” Deniz’s search plan: Stop your hands and breathe out once. Say the name of the thing and where it was last seen. Check the same place carefully once, not quickly ten times. If you can’t find it, ask for help and choose a stand-in plan. The stand-in plan might be using another pencil, or photographing the work, or looking again later.
== p013.xhtml 💜 For Parents and Teachers Saying “it’s right in front of you” does not teach a child how to search. Sweep the area systematically together, and give belongings fixed homes in fewer, open compartments. Add a bright marker or an attached case to whatever goes missing most. While the alarm is up, blaming, hurrying and everyone searching at once will only make the tangle bigger. One person, one order, one visible plan.
== p015.xhtml ADVENTURE 3 The Picture That Wasn’t Perfect Deniz was drawing a butterfly. The right wing came out exactly as he wanted. The left wing stayed a bit too small. He used the eraser. The paper went thin. He drew it again. The line came out crooked. A voice inside Deniz shouted, “Wrong! Start again!” He was about to screw the paper up. Pusula drew a tiny, lopsided star in the corner of the page. “That star’s gone wrong,” said Deniz. “That star is a trying star,” said Pusula. “Its job isn’t to be beautiful. Its job is to teach you the next move.” Together they opened three doors: fix it, work it into the design, or leave it as it is. Deniz added a small line of wind beside the smaller wing. The butterfly looked as though it were turning. The picture was not flawless. But it was still Deniz’s picture, and it was finished. 🗝️ Your sentence “This is a try. I can pick one of three ways.”
== p016.xhtml WHAT PUSULA SAYS A Mistake Is Not Where the Work Ends Pusula drew a “trying ladder”: Begin. Notice a mistake. Fix it, change it, or leave it. Carry on with what you have learned. “But what if it doesn’t come out nice?” said Deniz. “Not every job has the same aim,” said Pusula. “Sometimes it’s to learn, sometimes to tell someone something, sometimes to finish, and sometimes just to enjoy it.”
== p017.xhtml 💜 For Parents and Teachers Praising only how good the result looks can raise the pressure to be perfect. Notice the strategy instead: “You worked the crooked line into the design”, “When it got hard you tried another way”, “You left a try that was finished.” Make the measure of success concrete from the start. For example: “Write three sentences; it doesn’t have to be flawless.” Grown-ups can repair their own small mistakes openly too. When mistakes are safe, the brain keeps on trying.
== p019.xhtml ADVENTURE 4 The Long Minutes in the Waiting Room Deniz went with his mom to an appointment. The receptionist said, “There’ll be a bit of a wait.” How many minutes was “a bit”? The light above the chair was buzzing. The wall clock went tick, tick. His mom looked at her phone. Every time the door opened, Deniz stood up. Five minutes felt like fifty. “When?” Deniz asked. “Soon,” said his mom. Soon did not come. Pusula divided the waiting into three boxes: what we know , what we don’t know , and what we’re doing now . What we know: Our turn will come. What we don’t know: The exact time. Now: One thing chosen out of the quiet bag. Deniz put his headphones on and took out his little drawing pad. Every five minutes his mom would go and check how much longer it would be. The waiting was not lovely. But it had stopped being a shapeless fog. 🗝️ Your sentence “Can you tell me what we know, and what I can do while I wait?”
== p020.xhtml WHAT PUSULA SAYS Break the Not-Knowing into Small Boxes “We don’t make certain promises about uncertain things,” said Pusula. “But we don’t hide what we do know, either.” Inside Deniz’s waiting bag there were: Headphones A small drawing pad or a puzzle A squeeze ball Water A “how many people left?” checking card
== p021.xhtml 💜 For Parents and Teachers If you do not know how long it will be, do not offer made-up numbers like “two minutes”. Say, “I don’t know the exact time; I’ll go and ask again in ten minutes” — and then keep your word. Make the start, the check-in points and the end of a wait visible. In waiting places that are sensorily hard, ask about the choice of where to sit, waiting outside, headphones or a movement break. A prepared waiting bag is not a privilege; it is a way in.
== p023.xhtml ADVENTURE 5 Screen City and the Bridge Back Deniz was building a city on his tablet. He was just about to lay the last piece of the bridge. “Time’s up, switch it off,” said his dad. Deniz had heard him. But half his brain was still in the city. “One minute,” said Deniz. Within that minute the bridge was finished; then a new mission opened up. Deniz tapped that too. When his dad took the tablet out of his hands, something inside Deniz felt torn. “No!” he shouted, and reached for it. Pusula drew a bridge back along the edge of the screen. The next day they built the plan from the beginning: ten minutes, with a two-minute warning; one single action to finish; a picture for saving; carrying the device to its charging home; and a body activity, chosen in advance, for afterwards. When the time was up, Deniz laid the last stone of the bridge, saved the game and took a screenshot. “Carry on from here tomorrow,” he said. Then he put the tablet on the charger himself. The crossing was still hard — but it was not a cliff edge. 🗝️ Your sentence “I want to know where I’m stopping and what happens next.”
== p024.xhtml WHAT PUSULA SAYS A Boundary and Help Crossing It Can Go Together Pusula split the screen plan into five parts: Agree it before the time starts. Make the time that is left visible. Choose a safe stopping point. Save, and mark where you’ll come back to. Show what the next activity is beforehand. “What if I never want to stop?” said Deniz. “Not wanting to is real,” said Pusula. “The boundary is real too. The bridge helps you walk between them.”
== p025.xhtml 💜 For Parents and Teachers Using screens only as a reward or a punishment can load the crossing even more heavily. Look at the content, the length, its job in the day and the crossing skill as separate things. Do not belittle a child’s special interest; be curious about what he is actually making in the game. Use a warning you can be trusted on and a closing routine. Taking the device away suddenly should only be considered where safety genuinely requires it. If sleep, movement, school, relationships and everyday responsibilities are clearly affected over a long period, ask for a professional assessment together with the child; the number of minutes on its own does not tell the whole story.
== p027.xhtml ADVENTURE 6 When “No” Makes Your Heart Ache Deniz showed his teacher his picture of a spaceship. “Shall we put it up on the class board?” he asked. “There’s no room on the board this week,” said the teacher. “We could look again next week.” Deniz’s ears heard only two words: “No. No room.” It felt as though a small stone had dropped into the middle of his heart. “She didn’t like my picture,” he thought. He stuffed the paper into his bag and did not look at his teacher all day. Pusula picked the heart stone up. “Sometimes a no comes to the thing you asked for — not to the whole of you.” Deniz turned back to his teacher. “My mind is telling me you didn’t like my picture. Is that right?” The teacher looked surprised. “No — I looked at your picture very carefully. There’s other work on the board this week. If you like, we’ll put your name on the list for next Friday.” Deniz did not feel lighter right away. But a line had been drawn between “no room” and “you’re worth nothing”. 🗝️ Your sentence “Is this no about me, or about what I asked for?”
== p028.xhtml WHAT PUSULA SAYS “No” Tells You Something — It Doesn’t Say Who You Are Pusula asked three questions: What exactly was said no to? Can the reason be explained? Is there another time, another way, or a repair? “What if it still hurts?” said Deniz. “It may hurt,” said Pusula. “You give your body time for the feeling to pass. Then you check what it meant.”
== p029.xhtml 💜 For Parents and Teachers The feeling of being turned down can arrive very fast and very big. Instead of “You’re overreacting”, keep the boundary short and name the relationship separately: “We can’t go to the park now. I love you; I can see you’re sad.” Saying that does not remove the boundary. Once the child is calm, sort out together what actually happened: a request was turned down, an idea was postponed, a behavior was limited, or the relationship really does need repairing. You do not have to hand over another treat after every “no”; clear information and a bond that continues are worth more.
== p031.xhtml ADVENTURE 7 Two Wishes, One Brain Deniz wanted to tidy his room. At the same time he wanted to finish the book he had half read, complete his train track and have a drink of water. First he picked up the book. The train caught his eye. While he was straightening the track he remembered he was thirsty. On his way to the kitchen he spotted a sock on the floor. He picked the sock up and forgot where he was taking it. Ten minutes later the room was in an even bigger muddle. “You’re not finishing any of it,” said his dad. Deniz sat down on the floor. “They’re all shouting at the same time.” Pusula drew the jobs on small pieces of paper and put them all in a parking space. She took just one card into the “now” box: have a drink of water. Then the second card: put the socks in the basket. The train and the book had not disappeared. They were waiting in the “later” place. For the first time, Deniz heard a single voice in his brain. 🗝️ Your sentence “They’re all coming at once. Will you help me choose one?”
== p032.xhtml WHAT PUSULA SAYS Line the Jobs Up Outside Your Head, Not Inside It Pusula made a board with three sections: Now: One small job only. Later: Two jobs at most. Parking space: Good ideas and things for another time. After each finished job, Deniz chose the next card himself. “What if I pick the wrong order?” asked Deniz. “If there’s no safety or time limit, you can try a different order,” said Pusula. “The plan isn’t there to run you; it’s there to line the voices up.”
== p033.xhtml 💜 For Parents and Teachers Several instructions at once can overload working memory. Give one step, or use a picture card. Stay beside the child as he starts; then step back. Instead of “tidy your room”, define the area and the finish: “Put the socks on the floor into the basket; bring me the card when you’re done.” Use a “parking space” sheet for the new ideas that pull his attention away. Storing an idea safely, rather than dismissing it, can make it easier to come back to the job in hand.
== p035.xhtml ADVENTURE 8 Asking for Help Is a Superpower The class was getting ready for a trip. Deniz had three jobs: put his coat on, collect his permission slip, and line up. His coat zip jammed. The class began to move. Deniz pulled the zip. He pulled it again. He pulled harder. His hands were shaking. “I have to do it myself,” he thought. When everyone else had gone to the door, his eyes filled up. The zip still wasn’t done. Pusula lit the help lantern. “Doing it on your own and being independent are not the same thing.” “What do you mean?” asked Deniz. “Being independent means being able to find the tool or the person you need,” said Pusula. Deniz went to his teacher. “My zip’s stuck. Could you show me once, and then I’ll try?” The teacher eased the fabric out of the zip. Deniz pulled it up himself and joined the line. The help had not taken the job away from him. It had unlocked the way through. 🗝️ Your sentence “I’d like help with this bit; then I’ll carry on.”
== p036.xhtml WHAT PUSULA SAYS A Good Request for Help Has Three Parts Pusula broke the help sentence up: The job: What am I doing? Where I’m stuck: Which part is hard? The help I want: Show me, start with me, remind me, or give me a short break. Deniz came up with a few examples: “I’m reading the question; I didn’t understand the first word; could you say it another way?” “I’m tidying my room; I don’t know where to start; will you choose the first card?”
== p037.xhtml 💜 For Parents and Teachers When a child asks for help, do not take the whole job over. Give as much support as is needed: a hint, a model, the first step, a tool, or a short start together. Then leave room for the child to carry on. Shaming responses like “You can’t even do that?” close the door on asking for help. See a request for help as a skill in self-awareness and communication. If the child cannot say it out loud, he should be able to use a help card, a color or a sign.
== p039.xhtml THE ENDING My Bridge Bag Is With Me The following Saturday, Deniz made a plan for the park again. This time the weather was clear. But just as they were leaving, the car had a flat tire. The old storm rose up inside Deniz. “The plan’s changed,” he said. His voice was shaking. Pusula waited quietly beside him. Deniz opened his bridge bag. What was the old plan? The park. Why did it change? The car isn’t safe. What’s the new plan? A walk in the gardens nearby while the tire is fixed; the park moved to the afternoon. What’s the first step? Putting my shoes on. Deniz was still sad. He wanted to go to the park right now. But between his feeling and the new road there was a bridge. “So do you like changes now?” Pusula asked. “No,” said Deniz. “But when a change comes, I lose myself less.” “I can’t stop the storm — but I can carry my bridge with me.”
== p041.xhtml GAME My Change Bridge Keep six wordless cards in a folder: Old plan: An arrow pointing back New plan: An arrow pointing forward First step: A single footprint Break: A small safe island Parking space: A box of waiting ideas Help lantern: A hand holding up a light Choose one small change: a different cup, whose turn it is in a game, or another route home. Let the child lay out the old plan and the new plan with the cards. Then let him choose the first step. If it becomes too much, the rehearsal can be stopped; the aim is not to make a child like change by force. 💜 A closing question for grown-ups “While I was holding the boundary, did I also show the child the bridge he could cross?”
