Deniz and the Invisible Rules of Friendship

MY NEURODIVERGENT COMPASS

DENIZ AND THE INVISIBLE RULES OF FRIENDSHIP

I Don't Have to Erase Myself to Fit Into the Group

This time it is about three-person friendships, the silence behind "it's nothing," group photos, and the exhaustion of social masks. Deniz learns that she does not have to know all the invisible rules to be a friend — and that she can choose relationships that are open, mutual and reparable.

TUNCAY SANCAK • HAPPYTUNCAY

Ages 12–16 • 2026

A Note to the Young Reader

A friend can also be close to someone else; that does not mean your place is erased. But if you are constantly excluded, put down, or only expected to fit in, you can talk about it and ask for support.

The Table for Three

Deniz and Ada sat at the same table every lunch break. The table by the window was the corner of the canteen with the least echo, and the two of them had discovered it together.

One Monday Ela came with her tray and asked, "Can I sit here?"

"Sure," said Ada.

Ela spoke fast. Her sentences stuck together and left no room to breathe. Ada began to finish her sentences halfway; the two of them told memories from last summer's camp and laughed.

Deniz listened.

She searched for a gap to get into the conversation. The gap was that little pause between two sentences; Deniz always found it hard to find, but usually it came in the end.

This time it did not come.

At last she tried to break in and began to talk about the model railway exhibition she had visited at the weekend.

Right at her third sentence Ela turned and asked Ada a completely different question.

Deniz's sentence hung in the air. No one had been rude; no one had even noticed.

Deniz did not finish the sentence. Even if she had finished it, no one would have listened.

When she came to the table the next day, Ada and Ela were sitting side by side again.

Deniz stopped, turned around and went to the library. She sat alone, took her sandwich out of her bag and did not eat it.

In her head there was only one sentence, and it felt very final: Ada doesn't want me anymore.

In the evening Pusula settled on her desk. Three chairs appeared on her wings.

The chairs were arranged like a piece of music.

Two voices found each other easily — when one went quiet the other began, and between them was a ready-made harmony.

For a third voice, though, a clear order was needed. It did not form on its own.

"Being the third one doesn't mean being third-rate," said Pusula. "It just needs a different order. Even if no one has bad intentions — if that order isn't set up, someone always stays outside."

"Am I jealous?" asked Deniz. That question unsettled her.

"Noticing a need is not jealousy," said Pusula. "And saying it is not a crime."

That evening Deniz wrote Ada a message. She corrected it three times so it would not sound accusing.

"When there are three of us, I can't get into the conversation and I feel left out. Can we sometimes spend time just the two of us? And when we're three, can we set up a speaking order?"

Ada seemed to be writing for a long time, then sent a short reply:

"I hadn't noticed that at all. I'm sorry."

On Wednesday the two of them walked alone after school, and Deniz told the whole story of the exhibition from start to finish; no one interrupted her.

At the table for three, Ada developed a new habit. When a topic ended, she would say, "When we're done with this, let's come back to Deniz."

The first time it was strange. The second time, less. The third time, no one noticed anymore.

One day Ela turned to Deniz: "Do you have photos of that train exhibition?"

Deniz took out her phone.

Not every three-person moment was easy; on some days she stayed at the edge again.

But Deniz learned two things: wanting closeness with two people was not a crime of jealousy, and wanting a structure for three people was nothing strange.

"When there are three of us, I find it hard to get into the conversation. It helps me if you come back to me and we sometimes spend time just the two of us."

Pusula's tool: The balance of three

Say the concrete moment when you felt left out.

Leave your friend room for other relationships.

Openly ask for your own time with just one person.

Agree on a return sign in the group conversation.

Notice whether all the harmony depends on a single person.

For grown-ups

Give concrete communication support instead of sweeping judgements like "three never works." Do not shame the young person for their jealousy; underneath it can be the fear of loss or the need for a conversation structure.

What Does "It's Nothing" Mean?

On Tuesday morning Ada replied to Deniz's messages with a single word.

"ok"

"fine"

"dunno"

When they met in the corridor at school, Ada turned her head away. It was a move to avoid eye contact; Deniz recognised it, because she did it herself.

At break she went over to her.

"Did something happen?"

"Nothing," said Ada.

Deniz wanted to take the word as it was. When people said something, it was simplest to believe them.

But Ada's voice had come out flat, and so had her face. The word said "nothing"; everything else said something different.

Deniz spent the whole day trying to fit these two things together.

Her mind rewound. The joke she had made on Friday — was it too harsh? In the group project she had said she didn't like Ada's idea — had she put it badly? Had she replied late to a message two weeks ago?

She collected evidence, and the more she collected, the more sure she became: somewhere she had made a mistake, she just couldn't find it, but it was there.

In the evening Pusula unfolded two signs at the window.

On the first was written what was said. Under it was only one thing: it's nothing.

On the second was written what was guessed. Under it was a list: angry. sad. tired. not ready to talk. angry at me. angry at someone else.

"There is a gap between these two," said Pusula. "You fill that gap with yourself — always with the worst possibility, and always by blaming yourself."

"So what should I do? Pushing is rude too."

"You won't push," said Pusula. "You'll say what you see and leave the door open for her."

The next day Deniz asked a second time; but this time she asked differently, and she had thought out her sentence in advance.

"Your words say 'nothing,' but you seem distant. If you don't want to talk, I respect that. If it has to do with me, will you tell me openly when you're ready?"

Ada paused for a moment. Then she took a deep breath.

"It's not about you," she said. "Something is going on at home. I don't want to talk about it."

"Okay," said Deniz.

And it really was okay.

Two things happened in her at the same time. The first was relief — it was not her fault, the mistake she had been looking for had never existed.

The second was a bit harder: that Ada had chosen not to tell also had to be accepted. Deniz was curious and wanted to ask.

She did not ask.

Being a friend did not mean owning every piece of information. Hearing a clearly stated boundary was enough, and that boundary was not the opposite of trust, but a part of it.

Two days later Ada told her on her own.

"If you're not ready to talk, I can wait. If it has to do with me, I'd like you to tell me openly when you're ready."

Pusula's tool: Verify once, then leave room

Say the concrete change you have seen.

Ask a short, clarifying question.

Recognise the right not to want to talk.

Don't declare your worst guess to be the truth.

If the uncertainty stays, go back to your own routine.

For grown-ups

Do not give the young person the task of reading every facial expression correctly. The skill of asking directly but without pressure, and leaving room after the answer, can be practised. Regulating another person's feeling is not their responsibility.

Outside the Group Photo

The day after the return from the school trip, social media filled up with photos.

Deniz opened her phone and searched. In front of the bus, a photo had been taken of the three of them together — Deniz, Ada and Ela. She remembered that shot.

She found the photo.

In the shot were Ada and Ela. The left edge, where Deniz had stood, was cut off; only part of her shoulder remained.

She scrolled on.

In another photo the whole class was smiling with the same colour filter. Deniz had been there at that moment — but during the shot she had moved away from the noise and stood behind the bus.

Under the photo were two words: "Whole crew."

Deniz gripped the phone tightly in her skirt pocket.

She knew it made no sense; no one had deleted her, no one had done anything bad. Even so, a sentence formed inside her: If I'm not in the photo, I wasn't there that day either.

The next day she brought it up with Ela.

Ela shrugged. "You don't like photos anyway."

That was true and at the same time not true.

Deniz did not like being photographed without warning. She did not like a photo being shared within seconds. She really did not like forcing her face into a smile.

But none of this meant "I want to be completely erased."

In the evening Pusula drew a camera frame in the air and wrote a question on each of the frame's four sides.

May it be taken?

How do I want to look?

May it be shared?

Can we delete it later?

"These apply to you too," said Pusula. "When you take photos of others as well."

The next day Deniz wrote in the three-person group. She did not write much:

"Let me know beforehand when photos are being taken. I'm not in every shot, but I want to be in one I choose myself. And show it to me before you share it."

Ela wrote "ok." Ada sent a thumbs-up.

The next event was the spring festival in the schoolyard, and the music was loud.

The three of them moved away from the stage and went into the quiet garden behind it.

"Shall we take a photo now?" asked Ada.

Deniz nodded. She held the edge of her hat and stood naturally; she did not have to smile, because no one said "smile."

Ela showed the photo to all three before she shared it.

Deniz looked at the screen. She was there, not at the edge but in the middle, and her face looked like her own.

"This one works," she said.

"Not wanting to be in every photo doesn't mean I want to be invisible. Ask first, and show it to me before you share it."

Pusula's tool: The four photo permissions

Ask before taking the photo.

Allow a choice of pose and closeness.

Ask again before sharing.

If the mind changes later, talk about removing it.

→ And remember: Don't make a "no" a test of friendship.

For grown-ups

Consent to being photographed and to sharing are two separate decisions. Do not force a young person's image with the reason "for the memories." At school events, offer forms of participation and remembrance that do not require a photo.

The Price of the Mask

When Deniz spoke in class, she controlled four things at once.

Looking at faces — but not too much, because too much was strange.

Laughing in the right place — at the same moment as everyone else, not a second late.

Not winding her hair around her finger.

And correcting the tone of her voice; because once they had asked "are you angry?", though she wasn't.

None of this was the lesson. It all ran in the background of the lesson, at the same time.

At lunch she went to the toilet, locked the door and did nothing for five minutes. Then she came out and looked "relaxed" again.

One Friday she came home.

She took off her shoes, went straight to her room, took off her skirt and put on her wide jogging trousers. She closed the curtains. She sat on the bed and did nothing.

Her mother opened the door a crack.

"You were so cheerful at school, why are you like this now?"

Deniz could not answer.

She actually knew the answer; but saying it also took energy, and there was no energy left. Looking cheerful at school had been a job that lasted all day, and that job was now over.

Pusula came out from behind the curtain. Two batteries appeared on her wings.

The outer battery was green. It looked full; that was what everyone saw.

The inner battery was blinking red.

"The difference between the two batteries is masking," said Pusula. "In some settings it can be necessary and can protect you. But it has a price, and you pay the price — usually after you get home."

That week Deniz kept a list.

Which social behaviour was natural, and which needed constant control?

The results surprised her. Even looking at the other person's eyebrow instead of their eyes — a trick she had come up with herself and which worked — was tiring. It did the job, but never for free.

Then she changed small things.

She gave up the obligation to laugh at every joke. When she found something funny, she laughed, when not, she didn't — and the world did not change.

She got permission from the school counsellor: two days a week she could spend lunch in the quiet corner of the library. That was not fleeing; it was recharging.

She told Ada too: "Sometimes I look away while I'm listening. That doesn't mean I'm not interested. On the contrary — when I don't look, I listen better."

Ada thought for a second. "Okay," she said. "I didn't know that."

Deniz did not take off the mask in a single day, and she couldn't have. Some settings were still not safe, and there she kept using the old rules.

But now she knew where and why she used them.

That difference grew two things: the right to rest and the right to be more real in safe places.

"That I look fine on the outside doesn't mean I have energy. I need a place where I can rest without a mask."

Pusula's tool: The social energy balance

Notice which behaviour you're constantly controlling.

Explain your natural way of communicating to a trusted person.

Build quiet breaks with no expectations into the day.

Plan recovery time after an event.

In unsafe settings, reach out to a supportive adult.

For grown-ups

That a young person seems "trouble-free" at school does not make their difficulty at home invalid. Masking can create heavy exhaustion. Take care not to punish natural communication while rewarding social performance.

Safe Friendship

In the last week of term Deniz and Ada argued again.

The meeting time had changed. Ada had written it in the group but had not tagged Deniz.

Deniz went to the park an hour early and waited an hour. She sat on the bench, looked at her phone, then tried not to look.

When Ada came, Deniz stood up.

"Because of you my whole day is ruined."

Ada's face hardened. "I forgot once."

Both went silent.

Inside Deniz two old reflexes stirred at the same time.

The first: to declare everything over. So we're not real friends.

The second: to swallow the anger. Forget it, it's not important, I'm exaggerating.

She did neither.

From her pocket she took the five stones Pusula had given her — not a real stone, but the order in her head: fact, effect, need, boundary, repair.

She went through them in order.

"The time changed and I got no message," she said. That was a fact; not an interpretation.

"I waited an hour, my plan is ruined, and I'm very tense." That was the effect.

"When something changes, I want a direct message." That was the need.

Ada looked at the ground for a while.

"You're right," she said. "I'd written it in the group but I didn't tag you. From now on I'll send it to you separately."

Then she paused and went on — her voice shaking a little.

"But when you say 'you ruined my whole day,' I feel like I'm the only one to blame. As if everything is my fault."

Deniz wanted to go into defence for a moment. She wanted to say: But it really was ruined.

Then she listened to Ada's sentence again and saw there was something right in it.

She corrected her sentence.

"I felt like my whole day was ruined," she said. "Now I can make a new plan."

The difference was small. But the first sentence declared Ada guilty, the second told what Deniz had felt.

Ada laughed — a bit tired, a bit relieved.

"So what do we do now? We've lost an hour."

"We've lost an hour," said Deniz. "We have three."

They walked through the park and did not bring the argument up again; because it was over.

On the way home Deniz thought about something.

Friendship was not a place where mistakes never happened. Ada would forget again; Deniz would speak too sharply again.

Friendship was the place where a mistake was not denied and both voices were heard.

Neither of them had to be right. Both had to be heard.

"I can say what happened, the effect it had on me, and what I need; and I can also hear your answer."

Pusula's tool: The five stones of repair

Fact: what happened without any interpretation?

Effect: what did it cause in my body and my plan?

Need: what would help next time?

Boundary: what is acceptable for both of us?

Repair: what change will there be in the behaviour?

For grown-ups

Support a safe conversation structure instead of solving the young people's conflict immediately. A friendship continuing is not the only success; stepping back respectfully can be a healthy outcome too.

## Closing activity: My visible friendship rules

Complete five sentences: When the plan changes…, when I can't speak…, when photos are being taken…, when I need time alone…, when a mistake happens…. These sentences are not to control others, but to explain how you take part safely in a relationship.

This story does not replace your school's procedures and the authorities' responsibility in cases of constant exclusion, threats, the sharing of private images or bullying. Do not try to solve it alone.

Keep evidence safely and report it to an adult you trust and to the relevant unit at your school.

- United Kingdom: Childline: 0800 1111 (free and confidential); NSPCC: 0808 800 5000; in an emergency call 999.

- A school counsellor or a trusted teacher at your own school.
