Deniz and My Own Voice

MY NEURODIVERGENT COMPASS

DENIZ AND MY OWN VOICE

My Voice Is Mine Not Only When I Speak, but on Every Path I Choose

In the last book of the Girl Deniz series, Deniz explores silent answers, asking for help, the social roles expected of girls, and small trials for the future. She learns that self-advocacy is not about speaking loudly, but about expressing her own needs and choices in an accessible way.

TUNCAY SANCAK • HAPPYTUNCAY

Ages 12–16 • 2026

A Note to the Young Reader

Your voice can be speech, writing, a message, a picture, a card, or the support of someone you trust. You do not have to break down first in order to ask for help. Being polite does not mean saying yes to everything. Your future does not have to be chosen all at once either.

A Silent Answer Is an Answer Too

On Thursday everyone was going to present their project idea in turn.

Deniz's idea was ready and good — she knew it. Sensory-calmer rest corners at school: where they would be, what they would look like, how long they would take. She had even done a drawing of it.

She had worked on it for two weeks.

The turn moved along. Five people left. Three people left.

Deniz felt a change in her throat. This was not excitement; she knew excitement. This was a door closing.

"Deniz?"

Deniz opened the drawing on her computer. The screen was projected for the class.

She opened her mouth.

No sound came out.

Five seconds passed. Then ten.

Someone in the back row whispered: "Again?"

Deniz heard that whisper, and no one knew she had heard it.

The teacher waited a while, then said "All right, we'll come back later," and moved on to the next student.

They did not come back.

Deniz's project counted as not presented. The drawing stayed on the screen for a few seconds and then disappeared.

In the evening, in her room, Pusula settled on the desk and opened five paths.

The paths did not go through the throat.

Live speech. A pre-recorded voice. Written text. A visual presentation. Supported question and answer.

"All five are a presentation," said Pusula. "Only one of them requires speaking at that moment."

"But everyone uses the first."

"Everyone else's door is open," said Pusula. "Yours closes sometimes. That doesn't mean you can't present — it just means you can't go through that one door."

That evening Deniz wrote the teacher a message. She wrote because she knew she could not say it face to face, and that too was a path.

"My idea was ready; I couldn't speak at that moment. Not because I didn't want to. Can I record the presentation in advance and get the questions in writing?"

The teacher replied the next morning: "That's fine. Send me the recording."

Deniz recorded the presentation at home. She started over three times, and on the fourth she liked it. In her own room, at her own desk, with no one watching, speaking was easy.

The next day Deniz's voice came out of the speaker.

Her drawings moved across the screen in order. Deniz did not stand at the front of the class; she sat at her own desk and did not have to show her face to anyone.

When the presentation was over, the teacher handed out small slips for questions.

Three questions came.

Deniz answered two of them out loud — from her own seat, in short sentences. Her voice came out because she was not unprepared.

The third she wrote down.

The presentation was real.

The content was hers, the drawings were hers, the two weeks of work were hers.

Only the path was different.

And a week later the school management took up the idea of the rest corners.

"I have an answer, but live speech isn't accessible for me right now. I can take part with writing or a recording."

Pusula's tool: The five paths of the voice

Live speech.

A pre-recorded voice or video.

A written answer.

A picture card or drawing.

A supported telling with a trusted person.

For grown-ups

Do not measure participation only by spontaneous oral performance. If the learning goal is preserved, offer alternative ways of expression. Do not dismiss the young person's form of communication as "taking the easy way out."

Asking for Help Needs No Proof

At the school festival Deniz had a role — at the registration table, for three hours.

Two days before the festival she went to the school social worker and asked for two short breaks. When, how long, where — she had written it all down.

The adult on duty at the table looked at the paper.

"But nothing's happened yet," she said. "If you struggle, we'll see."

The sentence was not unkind. It even wanted to be reassuring: you're fine, you don't need it.

Deniz took the paper back.

Something settled inside her, and it was familiar: to get support, you first had to be badly enough off. You needed proof.

On the day of the festival the music was loud. The speakers were right above the registration table. The lights changed fast, and the crowd moved constantly — no one stayed in one place.

Deniz held on for an hour.

Then the sounds began to blur together. People's sentences became a single block; she could not tell who was asking what.

Her words got fewer. First her sentences got shorter, then they came down to single words.

Then she crouched under the table and pressed her hands to her ears.

That was when everyone ran over.

They brought water, asked "are you all right," called someone. Help was there now, and plentiful.

The next day Pusula settled on the edge of the bed. A smoke alarm appeared on her wings — broken, its cover open, the battery removed.

"What does this alarm need in order to work?" she asked.

"Fire," said Deniz.

"No," said Pusula. "Smoke. The alarm waits for smoke, not the fire. But yesterday they asked you for the fire."

That week Deniz wrote a preventive plan, and its heading was: not fleeing the event, but being able to stay in the event.

A five-minute quiet break every forty minutes. Ear defenders — she could do her job even with them on. And an orange card to ask for a change of task.

At the next event there was a new supervising teacher.

She read the plan and asked only one question — and that question changed everything:

"How will I know you need a break?"

"When I show my card," said Deniz. "Even if I look fine on the outside. I usually look fine."

The teacher nodded and put the card not in her pocket, but on the table — so it would be visible.

On the day of the festival Deniz used both her breaks. At the first she was really tired. At the second she was not so tired; even so she went, because that was the plan.

There was no crisis. She did not crouch under the table. At the end of the three hours she was tired but she could talk.

While they were tidying up, someone made a joke:

"So you didn't actually need it."

Deniz laughed.

"Because the plan worked," she said.

"I need support before the crisis. A preventive break lets me take part in the event."

Pusula's tool: The preventive support plan

Identify the risky condition in advance.

Write down the break time or sign.

Explain how the support serves the goal.

Count the absence of a crisis as the plan's success.

If something doesn't work, adjust the support, don't remove it.

For grown-ups

Waiting for the young person to fail first before adapting defeats the purpose of preventive support. Make the need concrete, try it briefly and observe the effect. Do not treat a load that is invisible from outside as if it were not valid.

The Role Expected of Girls

The family gathering was on Sunday, and the living room was full.

When the tea service began, Deniz was in the kitchen — she did not quite remember how she had got there; someone had said something and she had got up.

She was collecting the plates.

From the living room came the voices of her boy cousins; they were playing games and shouting.

An aunt came into the kitchen, saw Deniz and smiled.

"Girls are more thoughtful."

The sentence was said as a compliment.

Deniz put the plate down and looked at her hands.

The same thing happened at school. In group projects the job of taking notes was always given to her. And so was the job of stepping in and smoothing things over when someone started an argument.

"You're organised, you do it," they had said.

Deniz was not organised. Her room was untidy, half her notebooks were missing.

She just didn't object. Not objecting was enough to be thought organised.

In the evening Pusula came to her room and showed an invisible bag in the air.

There were no objects in the bag. There were tasks:

remember. smile. smooth the mood. bring the tea. don't upset anyone. keep track of how everyone feels.

The bag was heavy. Deniz noticed how her shoulders were set.

"Who gave this to me?" she asked.

"No one gave it to you alone," said Pusula. "Everyone put in a piece, and you carried it. And because you carried it, everyone thought it belonged to you."

At the next family gathering, when the tea service began, Deniz did not go to the kitchen.

Then she formed a sentence, and her voice came out normal:

"I want to finish the game too. Let everyone collect the plates in turn."

There was a moment of silence.

Then her father stood up and handed the plates to the cousins — three people, three plates, without argument.

An aunt said "well, look at that," but she was laughing.

At school too Deniz proposed a task chart for the group project. Note-taking, presenting, researching and time-keeping would rotate.

Deniz chose research — the part she liked and that had never been given to her before.

The same week two of her friends argued, and one came to Deniz: "You talk to her, she listens to you."

Deniz used to do this. And she did it well.

"You two need to talk about this," she said. "I won't be the mediator."

As she said it, something tightened inside her; it felt as if she were being rude.

She was not.

She had stayed polite. She just hadn't automatically taken on the invisible work.

Being a girl did not mean being responsible for the feelings of everyone in the room.

"Being thoughtful doesn't mean taking on all the work. Let's share the tasks clearly and fairly."

Pusula's tool: Make the invisible work visible

Who is remembering, preparing and mediating?

Was the task really chosen, or just assumed?

Write the tasks on cards and share them in turn.

Use the sentence "I won't be the mediator."

Count rest and interest time as equally valuable.

For grown-ups

Expecting girls to be more adaptable, caring or socially intuitive can create an extra masking load for neurodivergent young people. Share tasks at home and school not by gender, but clearly, fairly and accessibly.

Small Trials for the Future

The school counsellor opened the file and asked directly:

"Which profession do you want?"

It was not that Deniz hadn't thought about the answer — she had thought about it a lot. The problem was not not thinking.

She loved animals. She loved drawing. She loved putting something in order, making something confusing become understandable. She also got on well with children; her little cousin told her everything.

But in noisy settings she was worn out after an hour. Suddenly ringing phones tensed her. Days with an unclear schedule ate up the whole day.

"There's one thing I don't know," she said.

The counsellor put a long test in front of her. Thirty questions.

Deniz read the first question: "I like working with people."

Her pen stopped in the air.

She loved working deeply on one subject with a single person. In a meeting of ten people she could not say a word.

Both were "working with people."

The boxes felt too narrow for her, and the three professions that came out at the end of the test said nothing to her.

In the evening Pusula settled on the desk. She did not write a profession name.

Instead she opened cards, and on the cards were conditions:

ambient noise. clarity of the task. movement. density of people. creative space. access to breaks.

"A profession name is a box," said Pusula. "Two jobs with the same name can be completely different for you. Look at the conditions."

Deniz did three small trials.

The first: on a Saturday she watched the children's workshop at the library. She loved the children. She did not love fifteen children talking at the same time.

The second: at the animal-care centre she took a three-hour shift during the quiet hour. The animals were good. The repetitive tasks were better than she had expected — knowing what to do was restful.

The third: she watched a designer's online work. Spending hours alone at a screen drew her in; but not speaking to anyone all day felt strange too.

After each trial she did not write "liked it / didn't like it."

She wrote down which condition raised her energy and which lowered it.

The result was not a single profession name.

But a direction emerged: jobs that combined visual storytelling with making clear instructions for children. Illustrated instructions. Understandable signs. Making something complicated into a form a child could understand.

She did not know the name of such a profession, and she did not need to.

Her next step was a short drawing course.

"Before choosing a profession name, I want to explore the working conditions that suit me."

Pusula's tool: The condition compass

The level of noise and light.

The clarity and pace of change of the tasks.

The type and intensity of contact with people.

The chance for movement, breaks and working alone.

Room for curiosity and creativity.

For grown-ups

Treat career choice not as a one-off decision but as a process of exploration. Talk about environmental needs as much as interests. Short observations, volunteering and course trials often provide more insight than abstract expectations.

My Voice Is With Me

The end-of-year review meeting was on Tuesday afternoon.

At the table were three teachers, the school social worker, her mother and Deniz.

The conversation began.

"Quiet but hardworking."

"Sometimes sensitive."

"I think she should take part in the group more."

Deniz was sitting at the table, and no one was looking at her.

At the meeting about her own life she felt like a guest. They were talking about her — nothing bad, most of it was even true. But it was all written from the outside.

Pusula came out from under the table and pulled up an empty chair.

On the chair were two words: My view.

Deniz took her cards out of her bag. She had prepared three of them the night before, because she knew she might not be able to speak at such a meeting.

She read the first one out loud. Her voice was a little quiet, but it was heard:

"That I'm quiet doesn't mean I'm not taking part."

The table went silent.

She handed the second card to the teacher:

"Besides live speech, I'd like written contributions to be counted too."

The teacher read the card, then wrote something in her notebook.

On the third card was a goal for the next term: instead of presentations in front of the big group, a small-group presentation once a month.

Then Deniz turned to her mother.

This was the hardest; it had been easier to tell the teachers.

"When you talk about me in my presence, ask me first."

Her mother paused for a moment. Then she nodded.

"You're right," she said.

For the rest of the meeting the plan was written with Deniz's participation.

Not every one of her wishes was accepted. The small-group presentation would not be once a month, but once every two months — that suited the school's schedule.

But the difference was this: every request that was turned down was talked about with an explanation. It was not "no"; it was "for this reason let's do it this way for now, and we'll look again mid-term."

This was not just her voice being heard. It was influencing the decision.

On the way home the sky was clear and there was a wind.

Deniz took off her orange hat, held it in her hand and let her long hair blow in the wind.

Pusula flew beside her shoulder.

"Do you still hear me?" she asked.

Deniz smiled.

"I hear you," she said. "But now I hear my own voice too."

"In decisions about me, my view must have a place too. I'll use my voice in an accessible way."

Pusula's tool: My voice for the meeting

Something that goes well for me.

A concrete condition I struggle with.

Support that works, or that I want.

A small goal for the next term.

How we'll follow up and when we'll look again.

For grown-ups

In a meeting the young person is attending, do not talk about them as if they were a third person. Share the agenda in advance, offer alternative communication paths and explain how their view is reflected in the decision. Self-advocacy is a skill learned with support.

## Closing activity: My voice cards

Prepare five small cards: "I can speak now," "I want to write," "Time to think," "I want someone with me," "I want to take part in the decision." If you want, use your own symbols. Your cards do not have to be explained in order to be valid.
