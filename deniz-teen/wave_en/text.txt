== ch001.xhtml [] epub:type="bodymatter"> Deniz and the Wave of Feelings MY NEURODIVERGENT COMPASS DENIZ AND THE WAVE OF FEELINGS Even If I Can't Name My Feeling Right Away, I Can Listen to My Body Deniz, with her long hair and orange hat, sometimes cannot say straight away what she feels. The feeling shows up first in her jaw, her stomach or her fingers; the name for it comes later. In this book, overload, the shutting down of the voice, guilt and repair are treated not as character flaws, but as signals that can be understood. TUNCAY SANCAK • HAPPYTUNCAY Ages 12–16 • 2026 A Note to the Young Reader "I don't know" is a real answer. Not finding a feeling straight away does not mean you have no feelings. Your cycle, lack of sleep or stress can affect feelings — but not every one of your feelings can be reduced to "hormones." If you have strong sadness, fear or thoughts of harming yourself, tell an adult you trust right away. ---

== ch003.xhtml ['file0.jpg', 'file1.jpg'] epub:type="bodymatter"> The Feeling Without a Name On Monday morning the teacher went around the class before the first lesson. She stopped beside Deniz's desk. "How are you today?" The question seemed easy. Three words, and everyone else answered in one second. Deniz paused. There was something inside her. She knew it — it lay like a weight on her chest and had been there since morning. But it had no name. In the morning she had argued with her mother; an argument that had started over socks and then had nothing to do with socks. On the bus two people behind her had talked loudly the whole way. When she came into the classroom, Ada was already sitting with someone else, and Deniz's seat had stayed empty. Which of these had caused the weight? All of them? None of them? "I'm fine," she said. The teacher nodded and walked on. At lunch her stomach clenched. She had clenched her jaw so hard that the side of her head began to ache; she only noticed when she tried to loosen it. Ada came with her tray and sat down across from her. "Are you angry with me?" Deniz looked at her. Was she angry? "I don't know," she said. Ada's face fell. Deniz saw it and wanted to fix it; but to fix it she would first have to know what was going on, and she did not. Pusula settled on the edge of the tray and unfolded a small map in front of her. The map looked like a weather map. But there were no feeling names on it — it did not say "anger," it did not say "sadness." Instead there were body signs: hot face. heavy chest. fidgeting leg. tight jaw. empty stomach. "You can't find the name for the feeling," said Pusula. "That's normal for some people; the feeling is there, but the label for it doesn't come. Before you find the name, you can say the weather report. The weather report is information too." Deniz looked at the map. Then she looked at her own body. Stomach: tight. Jaw: clenched. Chest: heavy. She turned to Ada and this time did not say "I don't know." "I don't know if I'm angry with you," she said. "But my stomach is tight, my head hurts, and today I've felt alone." Ada did not move her chair closer. She left the space around Deniz as it was — they had talked about that before. "I sat with Ela for the project today," she said. "I didn't mean to leave you out. I should have told you, I forgot. We can talk later." Deniz nodded. When she lay down in bed that evening, the weight was still there — but now she could see its shape. It was not only anger. There was surprise — that Ada had not told her anything. There was jealousy, and admitting it was hard. There was the tiredness left over from the morning's argument. And there was the fear of being left out; that one was the oldest. All five were there together, and none of them alone was the right answer. That it did not fit into a single word did not make the feeling invalid. "I don't know the name for my feeling yet. But I can tell what is happening in my body." Pusula's tool: The body weather Notice your head, jaw, chest, stomach, hands and legs one by one. Choose one of the words hot, cold, tight, heavy, fast or empty. Check hunger, thirst, pain, sleep and your cycle. Describe the event in a single sentence. If you can't find the name for the feeling now, come back to it later. For grown-ups Asking "What do you feel?" over and over does not make the answer easier. Offer choices through body sensation, energy level and the concrete event. Do not correct the young person's answer; accept that several feelings can be present at once. --- CHAPTER 2
== ch004.xhtml ['file2.jpg', 'file3.jpg'] epub:type="bodymatter"> The Glass That Suddenly Overflowed On Wednesday morning the school corridor was louder than usual. Because the gym was being painted, two classes were waiting in the corridor, and the noise bounced back off the walls. The waistband of Deniz's skirt had been pressing since morning. It was newly washed and had shrunk. Deniz adjusted it ten times, and ten times it slipped back. At the first break the canteen queue was long; Deniz did not join it and did not eat. In science her deskmate began to tap a rhythm on the desk with his pen. Tap. Tap-tap. Tap. Deniz watched the pen. Trying not to hear the sound took up more space than simply hearing it. In the last ten minutes the teacher announced, "The last lesson will be in classroom C, take your things." Something tightened inside Deniz. Each time she said the same thing to herself: Hang on. It was as if there were an invisible glass into which drops were falling. The tight waistband. The noise. The rhythm. The hunger. The change. And the effort, all day, to keep a normal face. The drops piled up, and no one saw the glass — not even Deniz. In the last lesson the teacher handed out photocopies. As Deniz took the sheet, the corner caught on something and tore. A tiny tear. Two centimetres. Deniz threw the sheet onto the desk. "That's enough!" Her voice sounded foreign to her own ears. Her chair tipped back and crashed to the floor. The class went silent. Thirty pairs of eyes turned to her. Deniz walked out into the corridor. She crouched against the wall, pressed her hands to her ears and cried. While she cried, a second wave came — this time shame. Over a sheet of paper. In front of the whole class. Over a sheet of paper. Pusula sat down beside her. She did not point to the sheet on the floor. Instead she drew a glass in the air and filled it from back to front: the tight skirt in the morning, the echo in the corridor, the skipped breakfast, the rhythm of the pen, the classroom change, and the six hours of effort to "look normal." The glass had already been full before the paper tore. "The last drop is not the reason for the glass," said Pusula. At the end of the corridor the school counsellor appeared. She came closer, but without pushing, and did not ask long questions. In her hand was a key; she placed it on the floor beside Deniz. The quiet room was small. There was water in it, a loose spare cardigan, and the curtain was closed. Deniz said nothing. She took the red card from her bag and put it on the table. After forty minutes she had calmed down. The next day she talked to her teacher about the incident, and the conversation had two parts. First part: throwing the sheet and tipping the chair over was not safe. Deniz took responsibility for that, and it was not hard for her. Second part: support would not look only at the last behaviour. While the glass was filling, something could have been done too. Deniz put a small wave card on the corner of her desk. The card was yellow, and it had only one meaning: the glass is filling, I need a short break. She did not have to wait for red. "This is not just the last incident. My glass is full; now I need a quiet break with few words." Pusula's tool: The glass gauge Green: my body is relaxed, I can talk. Yellow: sounds are growing, my jaw or hands are tensing. Orange: words are getting fewer, the urge to flee is growing. Red: I need a safe space, little talking and time. Afterwards: if something was harmed, repair it — talk about the earlier drops. For grown-ups At the moment of overload, demanding explanations and apologies can increase the load. First make sure they are safe and reduce the level of arousal. After calming, discuss the effect of the behaviour together with the accumulated sensory, social and physical burdens. --- CHAPTER 3
== ch005.xhtml ['file4.jpg', 'file5.jpg'] epub:type="bodymatter"> Locked in Silence On Friday there was a maths test. Deniz got stuck on the third question — but not because she didn't know it. She knew the question; the formula was in her head. The wall clock in the classroom ticked. Tick. Tick. Tick. Each tick cut into the middle of Deniz's sentence. The thought would begin, the clock would strike, the thought would scatter. Then she would start again from the beginning. Deniz knew exactly what she wanted. Three things: if the sound of the clock were gone, or if she could sit somewhere else. If she could solve the questions not in order, but in the order she chose. If she could go out for two minutes and come back. All three were clear in her head. The teacher was walking between the rows and stopped beside her. "Is there a problem?" Deniz opened her mouth. No sound came out. The words were stuck somewhere down in her throat and would not come up. This was not a decision; Deniz was not choosing not to speak. The road was closed. The teacher waited, then asked once more. Deniz shrugged. Shrugging was the only thing she could do, and she knew it would be misunderstood. After the test, as the teacher collected the papers, she passed by. "I offered help, you refused." Deniz could not object. Again. At home she went into her room and did not come out until evening. Her mother called through the door twice; Deniz nodded, or thought she nodded. In the dark Pusula settled beside the pillow. Deniz pointed to her throat. "It's closed here." "I know," said Pusula. "But this isn't the only road." Paths appeared on her wings, and none of them went through the throat. Card. Writing. Sign. Message. "Not being able to speak doesn't mean not being able to communicate," said Pusula. "Only one road is closed. Side roads can stay open — but they have to be laid in advance. Once a door is closed, you can't build a new road." The following week Deniz talked to her teacher — on a calm day, at a time with no test, reading from a note she had written beforehand. Together they made a communication plan. When Deniz could not speak, she would put a blue card on her desk. On the back of the card were three options: short break. explain the question in writing. quieter place. When the teacher saw the card, she would ask only one question: "Which one?" Deniz would point with her finger. She did not have to speak. That was the most important point of the plan. At the next test the shutdown came again. Deniz had expected it; she had not thought it was over. She took out the blue card and put it on the corner of her desk. The teacher came closer quietly. She did not lean down, did not change her tone of voice. "Which one?" Deniz pointed to "quieter place." The teacher nodded, and Deniz took her paper and moved to an empty desk in the next room. She had not said a single word. And what she needed, she had got. "I can't speak right now; that doesn't mean I don't want help. I can choose from my card." Pusula's tool: The side roads Prepare a break or help card in advance. Decide on writing, signs, messages or picture options. Ask the adult to ask only one question and wait. Buy yourself time to answer. When speech comes back, go over the plan together again. For grown-ups A shutdown may not be deliberate ignoring. Do not force the young person into eye contact or speech. Accept pre-agreed alternative communication as valid, and do not tie the right to help to an oral performance. --- CHAPTER 4
== ch006.xhtml ['file6.jpg', 'file7.jpg'] epub:type="bodymatter"> The Guilt That Comes Later On the day of the overload, Deniz had shouted at Ada in the corridor. "Leave me alone!" Ada had stepped back, then had actually walked away. In that moment all Deniz had wanted was space, and the space had come. The right result, by the wrong road. When she calmed down in the evening, the sentence came back. Then it came again. When she closed her eyes in bed, she saw Ada stepping back again and again. "I'm a bad friend," she said. The sentence was simple, and there was no room in it for anything else. By the lamp Pusula took out two cards. On the first card was written responsibility , and under it three questions: What happened? Who did it affect? What can I repair? On the second card was written shame . Under it was only one sentence: I am bad. "The two are not the same," said Pusula. "The first takes you to the next step. The second keeps you in bed and repairs nothing." Deniz took her phone and began to write to Ada. She wrote twelve paragraphs. Why she had shouted, how the day had gone, how the glass had filled, how badly she felt. Then she read it all again. The whole message was about herself. There was no place in it for Ada. She deleted it all and wrote again: "It wasn't right to shout at you yesterday. I needed space, but I said it in a hurtful way. I'm sorry. When you're ready, we can talk." She sent it. Ada did not answer at once. Deniz turned the phone over and counted the waiting as part of the repair too. Ada being ready did not have to fit Deniz's timetable. The next day Ada came over to her in the schoolyard. "When you shout, I get scared," she said. "Not because you're angry. Because of the loudness." Deniz wanted to go into defence — she wanted to say I was having a hard time too . She swallowed it and did not say it. "I understand," she said. "I didn't know that." Together they chose a sign. When Deniz made a small wave with her hand, it meant: I need space now, we'll talk later. Shouting was not needed. Explaining was not needed either. Deniz did not clear herself — shouting had still not been right. But she did not make herself small either — one mistake did not make her a bad person. She took responsibility for her behaviour and built the next bridge. "My behaviour hurt you. I'm sorry; I want to listen without making excuses and build a safer way." Pusula's tool: Repair in four steps Name the behaviour clearly. Apologise without playing down the effect. Respect the other person's time and boundaries. Agree on a concrete change for next time. → And remember: Say "I can repair this" instead of "I am bad." For grown-ups A forced, immediately spoken apology may not be a real repair. Give the young person time to calm down; then help them understand the effect and choose a workable change. Neurodivergence does not erase the effect of a behaviour, but it shapes the form of support. --- CHAPTER 5
== ch007.xhtml ['file8.jpg', 'file9.jpg'] epub:type="bodymatter"> Riding the Wave For a month Deniz tried three things: the body weather, the glass gauge and the side roads of communication. Not everything worked every day. On some days she found the name for her feeling in the morning — she could say "today I'm anxious," and that much was enough. On some days she only understood it at night; when she went to bed she said "ah, I was actually sad." Finding it late was finding it too. On some days she did not find it at all and got by only with the body weather. She noticed one more thing: in the days before her period she was clearly more sensitive to sounds. The same corridor, the same echo, a completely different effect. But she did not put every anger down to that, and she did not let anyone else do so either. After sleepless nights she was sensitive too. After a difficult day with friends too. The map had several parts, and all of them were real. Pusula showed her a surfboard. On it were four words: notice, reduce, tell, repair. "You can't stop the wave," she said. "But you can stand on it." One Monday the teacher again announced a classroom change at the last minute. Deniz's stomach clenched at once — the body always gave the news first. She put the yellow wave card on the corner of her desk. She stood in the corridor for two minutes. She adjusted the waistband of her skirt. She drank water. She felt her breath coming back. Then she went to the teacher. "Could you write the order of the change on the board?" The teacher wrote it. The wave had come. Deniz had not stayed under it. On another day she overflowed even though she had done everything right. She had put the card down in time, taken her break, had breakfast — and still she overflowed. In the old days this would have meant everything she had learned was erased: so none of it works. This time she did not think that. She calmed down. She apologised to the person it had affected. She made a plan to reduce the load for the next day and went to bed early in the evening. The tools did not reset the overload to zero. They widened the gap between overloads and shortened the time afterwards. At the weekend Pusula settled on her shoulder. "Do I control my feelings now?" Deniz asked. "No," said Pusula. Deniz laughed. "That clear?" "Control is the wrong word," said Pusula. "You hear them. You notice them early. And you keep yourself company through it. That is much harder than controlling them — and that is exactly what you're doing." Deniz pulled her long hair out from under her hat and looked out of the window. The waves were still there. They would keep coming. But Deniz no longer had to prove every time that she could swim. "Even if I can't solve my feeling straight away, I can notice the signs and ask for support." Pusula's tool: The four movements Notice: catch the body and environment sign. Reduce: lower the load from noise, light, task or talking. Tell: use words, a card, writing or a sign. Repair: after calming, talk about the effect. → And remember: For a recurring pattern, get support from adults and professionals. For grown-ups The goal is not for the young person to suppress their feeling or always seem calm. Support them in noticing early signs, using safe regulation tools and repairing relationships. You can observe the cycle, but do not reduce the young person's whole experience to hormones. --- ## Closing activity: My feelings-wave card Divide a card into four areas: my body sign, what fills me, what reduces it by ten percent, my way of communicating.
