== p1
MY NEURODIVERGENT COMPASS
DENIZ AND THE NEW RHYTHM 
OF MY BODY
My Body Sends New Messages — I'm Learning to Read Them
Deniz is fourteen, a neurodivergent young girl. Her long hair sometimes rests softly on 
her shoulders, and sometimes a single strand at the back of her neck feels like an 
alarm ringing all day. Her orange hat is still on her head. Pusula is with her too; but 
Pusula is not a wizard who decides for her. She only makes more visible the signs that 
come from Deniz's body, her feelings and her surroundings.

== p2
A Note to the Young Reader
In this story you might find something that feels like you, you might find nothing, or 
in some places you might say "Me too!" Bodies do not have to grow at the same 
time, have the same shape, the same smell, the same hairs, the same spots or the 
same period rhythm.
Not liking the texture of a product, being bothered by the pressure of a piece of 
clothing, being startled at the sight of blood, or not being able to say straight away 
what you feel — none of this makes you difficult, dirty or childish. Body care is a skill 
you learn. You can experiment to find the method that suits your needs.
This book does not replace medical assessment. If pain or bleeding clearly affects 
your daily life, if you feel ill, or if something frightens you, reach out to an adult you 
trust and to an appropriate health professional.

CHAPTER 1
Why Is the Seam Shouting?
On Monday morning Deniz stood in front of her wardrobe looking at three black T-
shirts. From the outside all three looked the same. But for Deniz they were not.
The collar of the first sat too close to her neck. The label of the second had been cut 
out, but the small corner left behind poked into her back like a tiny fingernail. The 
third was soft; but after the last wash the chest had started to feel tighter.
Deniz put on the T-shirt. She took a step. The fabric moved. That small pressure 
came again.
"That didn't work," she said.
Her mother called from the doorway, "Twelve minutes until the school bus."
Twelve minutes started a red countdown in Deniz's mind. She took off the T-shirt. 
She put on the next one. The corner of the label touched her back.
"This one didn't work either."
She put on the third. This time, looking in the mirror, she noticed that her body 
looked a little different from before. The new shape developing at her chest was 
faintly visible under the T-shirt. There was nothing wrong with that. Even so, it was 
new to her. New things could sometimes be loud, even when no one made a sound.
Pusula — a small blue butterfly whose wings carry glowing golden compass-lines, 
and who has been with Deniz since childhood — landed on the edge of the hat. The 
golden lines on her wings glowed in three different places on the fabric: the collar, 
the label and the chest.
"Is my body the problem?" asked Deniz.
"Your body is sending new information," said Pusula. "Instead of pointing to a single 
spot as the problem, we can find out which contact is difficult."

== p5
Deniz looked at herself in the mirror. In recent months it was not only her chest that 
had changed. Her underarms sweated more quickly, her hair grew oily faster, and 
small spots appeared on her chin from time to time. Sometimes one breast looked a 
little different from the other. She did not look like the perfect body drawings that 
came up on the internet.
"Everything is changing at once," she said.
Pusula drew a small volume knob in the air. "It can feel like everything is changing at 
once. But you don't have to find a solution to all of it today."
When her mother came into the room, Deniz had folded her arms across her chest.
"Is your clothing bothering you?" her mother asked.
At first Deniz wanted to say "No." Because if she explained the reason at length they 
would miss the school bus. Then she looked at Pusula's golden lines.

== p6
"There are three problems," she said. "The collar is tight. The label pokes. And here, 
when the fabric moves, I notice it all day."

== p7
Her mother did not immediately tug at the T-shirt. She did not look at her body. She 
only asked, "Which one can we reduce today?"
Deniz took her old, loose turquoise overshirt. When she put it on over the black T-
shirt she noticed the movement of the fabric less. They also stuck a soft piece of 
tape over the corner of the label.
"It's not perfect," said Deniz.
"It might be enough for today," said her mother.
In the school changing room, as they got ready for PE, one girl was showing a friend 
the new bra she had bought. "Aren't you getting one yet?" she asked Deniz.
The question was not asked unkindly. But Deniz's face grew hot. She felt as if 
someone else's gaze decided what she should wear.
"I don't know," she said, and turned back to her locker.
Pusula lit a small lock symbol on the metal edge of the locker. Deniz knew it meant 
not "hide" but "private information."
"This is about my body," said Deniz. "When I'm ready, I'll talk to my family."
The girl shrugged. The topic ended.
That evening her mother put three options on the table: a seamless vest, a soft 
supportive top, and the loose vest Deniz already used. They were examples with 
labels cut out and no hard straps.
"You don't have to choose one of these," said her mother. "You can just touch them, 
try them over your clothes, or we can not look at them today at all."
Deniz touched the fabrics with her fingertips. One was too slippery. One was soft but 
the elastic was hard. The third was calmer.
"I can try this one for five minutes," she said.
Five minutes later she took it off. The next day she tried it for ten minutes. Within a 
week she learned which days she preferred the supportive top and which days the 
loose vest.

== p8
Pusula did not show her one single "correct girl's outfit." She showed her a shelf of 
choices.
When Deniz looked in the mirror again, she did not see her body as a mistake she 
had to hide. She saw a body that was growing, sending information and belonging to 
her.

== p9
"My body is changing. I can decide for myself which clothes are comfortable."

== p10
Pusula's tool: The texture and pressure check
1. Where is it uncomfortable: collar, label, seam, elastic, fabric or movement?
2. Is the discomfort constant, or does it increase when you move?
3. Is there a softer, looser, seamless or layered option?
4. Can I try the item for a short while first?
5. Do I have the right to say "I don't want to today"?
For grown-ups
The timing and pace of breast development, and whether the two sides grow at the 
same time, can differ from person to person. Commenting on a young person's body 
in front of others, imposing clothing by saying "you're a young woman now," or 
dismissing their discomfort as shyness can reduce trust. Break product or clothing 
choices into small, reversible trials. Let sensory comfort and privacy be part of the 
young
 
person's
 
decision
 
process.

CHAPTER 2
The Red That Wasn't in the Calendar
On Wednesday, in the second lesson, Deniz shifted uneasily in her chair. There was a 
slight heaviness in her lower belly. She thought it was hunger. Then she noticed a 
feeling like warmth.
"Maybe I've sweated," she thought.
Her mind was busy with the experiment in science class. As she copied the shapes 
from the board into her notebook, she put off the signal her body was sending.
When she stood up at break, she saw a small dark stain on her chair.
Her heart sped up all at once.
Pusula came out of her bag. The lines on her wings did not turn red; they stayed a 
calm turquoise.
"Have I cut myself somewhere?" whispered Deniz.
Before answering, Pusula asked one single question: "Are you safe right now?"
Deniz looked around. The pain was not very strong. There was no dizziness. But she 
did not know what to do, and she was afraid one of the others in the class would see 
the stain.
Ada stood by Deniz's chair. She noticed the panic on Deniz's face but did not say out 
loud, "What happened?"
Deniz went closer to her and whispered, "I think I've got my period."
When she said this sentence, the world did not fall apart. And the class did not go 
quiet and stare at her.
Ada held out her cardigan. "You can tie it round your waist if you like. Shall we go to 
the counsellor's office together?"
Deniz nodded.

== p12
As they walked down the corridor, dozens of questions ran through her mind. Too 
early? Too late? Will everyone understand? Will there be a smell? Which side of the 
pad goes where? What if she does it wrong?
Pusula drew a route in the air. On the route were only three stops: safe adult, clean 
product, private space.
When the school counsellor saw Deniz she was not surprised. She quietly took out a 
small bag. Inside were a few different pads, clean underwear and an opaque pouch.

== p13
"Is it the first time?" she asked.

== p14
Deniz nodded.
"If you like, I can show you how it's used with a picture card. If you like, we can call 
your mother. If you like, you can go to the toilet first and decide afterwards."
Deniz's shoulders dropped a little. There was more than one option.
"The picture card," she said. "But without too much talking."
The teacher put the card on the table. There were simple shapes; no long 
explanations. Deniz picked up a pad. The rustle of the packaging was louder than she 
expected. As she opened the adhesive strip, her fingers trembled.
"I can't do this," she said.
Pusula circled the first picture on the card in gold. "It's only the first try."
In the toilet Deniz locked the door. She pressed her feet to the floor. She placed the 
pad in her underwear. It went on a bit crooked. When she tried to fix it, the adhesive 
stuck to her finger.
She was caught between laughing and crying.
"It doesn't have to be perfect," she said to herself. "Clean and comfortable is 
enough."
On the second try it went better. She put the stained underwear in the opaque 
pouch. She washed her hands. She looked at her face in the mirror. The person in 
the mirror was still Deniz. It was only that a new rhythm had begun in her body.
Her mother said on the phone, "I can come and get you if you like."
Deniz thought. She wanted to go home, but she also wanted to finish the science 
experiment. She rested for ten minutes in the quiet armchair of the counsellor's 
office. Then she said, "I can try to stay until lunch. If the pain gets worse, I'll call."
This was not a test of courage. She did not have to stay. Going home would not be a 
failure either. In that moment Deniz made a choice based on the information her 
body was giving.

== p15
That evening she and her mother put a small preparation pouch in her bag. Inside 
were two different pads, spare underwear, a small opaque pouch and the picture 
sequence card. They did not write any words on the outside of the pouch; Deniz 
chose a small wave symbol that only she knew.
Pusula glowed on the zip of the pouch. "It wasn't in the calendar today," she said.
"But I have a route for next time," said Deniz.

6. Go to a safe adult.

For grown-ups
A first period can be a surprise. Talking about it openly, plainly and without shame 
beforehand; showing who to go to at school; putting spare products in the bag — all 
of this reduces anxiety. Instead of giving the young person a lot of information at 
once, offer a visual sequence, short sentences and choices. In the early years the cycle 
can be irregular; but do not close down a young person's worry about bleeding, pain 
or

"it's

CHAPTER 3
The Chorus of Products
The week after her first period, her mother took Deniz to the personal-care section 
of a shop. On the shelves were dozens of packets. Thin, thick, winged, scented, 
unscented, night, day, different colours and different pictures.
For Deniz the shelves were not choices but a shouting chorus.
"Which one shall we get?" her mother asked.
Deniz froze in front of the packets. One product's surface looked soft but its 
packaging rustled a lot. Another said it was scented. Just imagining the smell before 
even opening the packet gave her a headache.
Pusula flew in front of the shelf and turned the dozens of packets into a single blurry 
wall. Then she drew only three small cards out of the wall: texture, sound, pressure.
"First not the product name, but the body information," she said.
They came home with three small trial packs. On the bathroom table they set up a 
"comfort laboratory." Before using the products Deniz touched them with her hand. 
She listened to the sound of the packaging. She placed one in clean underwear and 
tried it over her clothes for ten minutes.
The first pad was very soft but folded when she moved. The second stayed in place 
but its edges were hard. The third product was thicker; Deniz noticed it constantly.
"None of them is perfect," she said.
"Then which one bothers you the least?" asked her mother.
Deniz pointed to the second. Then she saw period underwear on the internet. At 
first glance she thought it might be more comfortable. But she did not know what 
the wet feeling would be like.
"We can try one," said her mother. "If you don't like it, you don't have to use it."

== p19

== p20
Deniz discovered that different products could work on different days. Period 
underwear at home, a short-wear pad at school, a longer product at night felt safer 
to her. There was no single right answer.
But there was another problem: she kept forgetting to change it.
On the day she got absorbed in her science project, a lot of time had passed by the 
time she noticed the signal from her body. She got angry with herself.
"How could I forget?" she said.
Pusula drew a memory bridge in front of her. On the bridge's supports were symbols 
of the lesson bell, lunch and the way home.
"Feeling time can be hard," she said. "Using a reminder isn't laziness."
Deniz set up a wordless, vibrating reminder on her phone. On the screen only a 
small wave symbol appeared. At two fixed times at school she checked her pouch. If 
it wasn't needed, she did nothing. If it was needed, she changed it.
One day, opening the packaging in the toilet, the sound seemed very loud. She 
thought someone in the corridor would hear. She waited with the packet in her 
hand.
Then she remembered the sentence Ada had said last week: "This isn't a secret, it's 
private information."
Getting your period was not a shameful crime. Even so, Deniz could ask for privacy. 
She opened the packaging slowly, changed her product and went out.
Pusula, by the door, showed the three cards again: texture, sound, pressure. This 
time Deniz added a fourth card: remembering.
"I'm not disorganised," she said. "When my system is visible, I do it more easily."

== p21
"This product bothers me in a sensory way. Can I see another option with a 

== p22
small trial?"
Pusula's tool: The comfort laboratory
10. How is the texture?
11. How is the sound of the packaging?
12. Is there pressure or rubbing when I move?
13. How much does the wet feeling bother me?
14. Which fixed time or symbol helps me remember to change it?
For grown-ups
There are different products such as pads, period underwear, tampons or cups; not 
every product suits every young person. Especially in neurodivergent young people, 
texture, smell, sound, pressure, the wet feeling and the number of steps to change 
can be decisive. Instead of forcing the young person onto a particular product, assess 
the options with small trials according to age, skill, comfort and safe use. Follow the 
manufacturer's and health professional's guidance on product use and how often to 
change.

CHAPTER 4
The Weather in My Belly
A month later, when Deniz opened her eyes in the morning, she felt a tight knot in 
her lower belly. As if an invisible hand inside her were slowly squeezing and 
releasing her muscles.
She got out of bed. The knot came again.
"I don't want to go today," she said.
Her father stood in the doorway. "Do you have a test?" he asked.
"I do. But the problem isn't the test."
Deniz could not find the words to describe the pain. She said "bad." Then she 
thought that wasn't explanatory enough. Her face reddened.
Pusula drew a small weather map in the air: sun, cloud, rain and storm. Deniz 
pointed between cloud and rain.
"It's not constant," she said. "It comes like a wave. Right now it's medium. I can 
walk, but it's hard to focus."
Her father asked, "What helps you?"
Deniz did not know. They tried a warm pad. It eased a little. She drank water. She lay 
down quietly. Because her family had already spoken with her doctor, they followed 
the appropriate pain-relief plan under adult supervision.
Half an hour later the pain had not gone completely but had dropped to the rain 
level.
"I can go to school," said Deniz. "But I might not join PE. And I might ask for a short 
break during the test."

== p24
At school, going up to her teacher felt harder than the pain itself.

== p25
"My stomach hurts," she said.
The teacher answered, "Everyone hurts sometimes, the test is about to start."
Deniz's throat closed. Pusula lit a small lightning bolt in her pocket: this was not 
anger, but the sign that her need had not been heard.
Deniz prepared her second sentence.
"I have period pain. I'm going to take the test. But when the wave comes I need to 
stop quietly for two minutes and, if needed, go to the counsellor's office."
This time the teacher paused. "All right. You can sit near the door."
During the test the pain rose again. Deniz put down her pen, pressed her feet to the 
floor and breathed for two minutes. Then she carried on. She did not finish all the 
questions. But she did not go to war with herself to silence her body.
At lunch Ada said, "Mine hurts a lot sometimes too. I just put up with it."
Deniz thought. "Putting up with it isn't the only option," she said. "If the pain blocks 
my schooling, I need to say so."
That evening she noted down her pain map. The day, the level of pain, how long it 
lasted and what helped. This map was not to create fear, but to make the body's 
information visible.
Two cycles later, one day the pain was stronger and Deniz could not go to her 
lesson. Her family spoke with a health professional. Deniz did not sit silently in the 
appointment. She showed the weather map and said, "This time it was a storm."
Pusula did not speak for her. She only lit the edge of the map.

== p26
"My pain is affecting my daily work. I need rest and, if necessary, health 

== p27
support."
Pusula's tool: The body weather
15. Sun: no pain, or very mild.
16. Cloud: I notice it but I can do my things.
17. Rain: focusing or moving becomes hard; I need support.
18. Storm: I can't do my daily tasks or I feel ill; tell an adult right away.
19. Note the duration, the accompanying symptoms and what helped.
For grown-ups
Period pain can occur; but severe pain, missing school or daily activities, very heavy 
bleeding, unusual weakness, dizziness, or the young person feeling clearly ill should 
be assessed. Do not dismiss a young person's pain by saying "everyone goes through 
it." Plan supports in advance at home and school: rest, access to the toilet, water, a 
suitable product and a short break. Follow health-professional guidance on 
medication in a way that suits the individual's health situation.

CHAPTER 5
My Rhythm, My Word
On Friday there was a class activity on puberty at school. As the teacher talked 
about the changes in the body, some students giggled. One boy said, "Girls get really 
irritable on those days."
A few people in the class laughed.
This time it was not pain that formed in Deniz's belly, but an angry heat. Not 
everyone who has a period behaves the same way. And her being irritable one day 
did not mean that everything she said could be dismissed as "hormones."
She raised her hand. "It's not right to treat someone's feeling as unimportant just 
because she has her period," she said.
The class went quiet. Deniz's voice had shaken, but she had been heard.
At break the same boy came over and said, "I was joking."
Deniz answered, "It wasn't funny to me. I don't want you to comment on my body."
Pusula did not build a wall around Deniz. She placed a small boundary line on the 
ground. The line had a gate; the key was with Deniz.
That afternoon Ada took a group photo. As she was about to share it without 
showing Deniz, Deniz said, "One moment."
In the photo the wave symbol of the small preparation pouch tied at her waist was 
visible. No one might know what it was. Even so, Deniz did not want that photo 
shared.
"Don't share this photo," she said.
Ada deleted it at once. "Okay. Shall we take another?"
"Yes."
It could be this simple: question, answer, respect.

== p29
That evening Deniz made her own rhythm cards in her room. On the cards there 
were symbols more than words: a drop, a calendar, a cloud, a battery, headphones, 

== p30
a warm pad and a trusted adult. These cards were not to control her body, but to 
understand her body's language.
Her mother knocked and came in. "Would you like me to know your period dates 
too?" she asked.
Deniz thought. For help, sometimes her mother knowing could be useful. But she did 
not want to explain all the details.
"I can tell you when it starts," she said. "But let my notes in the app stay private to 
me."
"Okay," said her mother.
In that moment Deniz understood that growing up did not mean carrying all the 
privacy alone. She could get help and still set the boundary of some information 
herself.
Pusula left five route cards on the table: comfort, preparation, choice, support and 
boundary.
"Which is the most important?" asked Deniz.
Pusula closed her wings. "It depends on the day."
Deniz lined the cards up beside her hat. In the first chapter she had seen her body as 
a problem. Now she thought of her body as an instrument that did not make the 
same sound every day. Some days its sound was loud, some days it was quiet. Some 
days its rhythm faltered.
But the instrument belonged to her.
"I can't always control the rhythm," said Deniz. "But I can learn to listen to it."
This time Pusula's golden route lines did not stretch out in front of Deniz. Starting 
from the parrot badge on the hat, she drew a small arc over Deniz's heart.
The route was no longer only outside.

Pusula's tool: The five route cards
20. Comfort: what is happening in my body and what bothers me?
21. Preparation: which product or visual step should I have with me?
22. Choice: which option suits me better?
23. Support: who can I ask for help, and with which short sentence?
24. Boundary: which information is private, and which sharing needs my 
permission?
For grown-ups
Privacy does not mean depriving a young person of health or safety support. But 
comments on the body, checking products, tracking the calendar or sharing photos 
should respect the young person's say. Clear consent, age-appropriate choice and 
reliable help can all exist at the same time. Instead of dismissing what a young person 
says as merely "hormones," listen to both their feeling and their need.
