== ch001.xhtml [] epub:type="bodymatter"> Deniz and the Key to My Boundaries MY NEURODIVERGENT COMPASS DENIZ AND THE KEY TO MY BOUNDARIES I Have a Say Over My Body, My Image and My Space Deniz learns to use the sentences "I don't want to," "explain it first" and "I've changed my mind" in everyday situations like hugs, comments about the body, sharing photos and medical check-ups. She discovers that a boundary is not rudeness, but knowledge for safe relationships. TUNCAY SANCAK • HAPPYTUNCAY Ages 12–16 • 2026 A Note to the Young Reader Your body belongs to you. You do not have to hug someone to greet them. You can say yes to a photo first and then no to it being shared. In health care too, you have the right to ask clearly what will be done and to ask for a trusted adult to be with you. In emergencies, medical staff may sometimes have to act quickly to keep you safe; even so, they should explain as much as possible. ---

== ch003.xhtml ['file0.jpg', 'file1.jpg'] epub:type="bodymatter"> Do I Have to Hug? On the morning of the family gathering they went to the aunt's house. The moment the door opened, the aunt spread both arms wide. "My beautiful girl!" Deniz loved her aunt. She did not need to remind herself of that; she really did love her. But the aunt's perfume was heavy, and it had reached her even before she had gone through the door. Her jumper was woollen; last time, when it had touched her cheek, it had itched for hours. Deniz took a step back. She did not do it on purpose; her body did it. Her mother whispered beside her, "Don't be rude, give her a hug." Deniz pushed her body forward. The hug lasted two seconds. Her shoulders stayed up all evening. The living room filled up. Voices overlapped, tea glasses clinked, the television was on and no one was watching. In the late afternoon her little cousin came running and jumped onto Deniz's neck from behind. "Don't touch me!" Her voice came out much sharper than she had expected. The cousin froze, then began to cry. The living room went quiet for a moment and everyone turned. Deniz went to the bathroom, locked the door and held on to the edge of the sink. She was not angry with her cousin. She was not angry with anyone. Since morning her body had not belonged to her, and the last touch had been the point where it overflowed. Pusula settled on the edge of the mirror. Three keys appeared in front of her. On the first was a wave. On the second, a fist bump. On the third, a hug. All three were the same size; none was bigger than the others. "Hugging is not the only proof of love," said Pusula. "All three keys open the same door. You can choose which one to use." "And if it seems rude?" "We'll talk to the adults about that," said Pusula. "But know this: deciding about your own body yourself is not rude." The following week Deniz talked to her mother, when no one was tired and there was no visitor. "I love my aunt," she began — because that was the first thing her mother would think. "But don't force me when I don't want to hug. I can wave, I can fist-bump. Not because I don't love her." Her mother was silent for a while. "I never liked it at your age either," she said at last. "But no one asked." At the next visit the aunt opened her arms again. Deniz smiled and raised her hand. "Today I feel more like a wave." The aunt was surprised for a moment; her arms stayed up in the air. Her mother stepped in: "Deniz chooses her own greeting." The aunt laughed and waved back. Then she went inside and, as always, asked about Deniz's favourite dessert. In the evening the little cousin came running again. This time Deniz knelt down to her level. "Ask first," she said. The cousin thought. "Can I hug you?" Deniz chose a short hug that day. The same movement was something completely different. Because the choice was hers. "I love you, but today I don't want to hug. We can wave." Pusula's tool: The greeting menu A wave. A smile or a spoken greeting. A fist or elbow bump. A short hug. No touch today. For grown-ups Do not force a child or young person into physical contact as a sign of love. Explain the alternative greetings to the family in advance and do not leave the young person alone when they defend their boundary. The same rule also covers the young person respecting others' "no." --- CHAPTER 2
== ch004.xhtml ['file2.jpg', 'file3.jpg'] epub:type="bodymatter"> Comments About My Body Waiting in the canteen queue, the two people in front of her were talking, and one turned around. "That top makes you look bigger." Her gaze was aimed not at Deniz's face, but at her chest. Deniz did not answer. She did not know what to say, and the words did not come. When she sat down at the table, someone else pointed at the waistband of her skirt. "Have you put on some weight?" She had said it laughing. Like a joke. That day Deniz pulled her top down fifteen times. She left her lunch half-eaten — she was no longer hungry, or she had decided she was not. When she got home she stood in front of the mirror and examined herself in parts. Shoulder. Waist. Arm. As if each part could be scored separately. In the evening her other aunt called on video. "Your hair looks lovely, but if you wore a little make-up…" The sentence began with a compliment and ended with a lack, and the knot inside Deniz tightened. After she hung up, she sat on the bed. Pusula came through the window and stretched a thin rope across the middle of the room — the kind pulled in front of paintings in museums. "This rope isn't around your body," said Pusula. "It's around the talking about your body. You decide who may come in." "People think they're giving a compliment," said Deniz. "They don't mean it badly." "Most don't," said Pusula. "But intention doesn't cancel your right to end the conversation. Even a well-meant comment can be exhausting." That evening Deniz prepared three sentences and repeated each one out loud, so they would come easily to her. "I don't want comments about my body." "Let's not talk about my food." "I decide about my hair myself." The next day the same student in the canteen said something about her skirt again. This time Deniz did not stay silent. "I don't want comments about my body." Then she took her tray and moved away from the table. She did not wait for a discussion; her sentence was already finished. The student said something after her; Deniz did not hear it. The same day she went to her class teacher and said the behaviour had repeated — not once, but three times. At home her mother wrote a short message in the family group. She did not explain at length and did not blame anyone: "Deniz's body, her weight, her chest, her food and her appearance are no longer topics of conversation. Please let us all take care with this." One aunt wrote "oh, how touchy." Another aunt wrote "she's right, we heard a lot of it as children too." The topic ended. Deniz looked in the mirror again that evening. She did not feel obliged to love herself. It was not that kind of day, and it did not have to be that kind of day. But she had achieved one thing: she had stopped her body from being a noticeboard that everyone stuck something on as they passed. "I don't want comments about my body, my weight, my food, or how my clothes sit on me." Pusula's tool: The comment boundary Choose a short, undebatable sentence. Change the topic or move away. If it repeats, tell a trusted adult. Set up a family rule with the people close to you. If thoughts about food and the body intensify, ask for support. For grown-ups Even well-meant body comments can bother a young person. Do not discuss weight, growth, breasts, body hair, skin or eating behaviour in a group. If you notice a clear restriction of eating, strong body anxiety or a rapid change, turn to a health professional without shaming. --- CHAPTER 3
== ch005.xhtml ['file4.jpg', 'file5.jpg'] epub:type="bodymatter"> Who Does My Photo Belong To? Ela shared the video on Thursday evening. The video was ten seconds long. Deniz was dancing at the festival rehearsal in the schoolyard — clumsily and laughing. While she was being filmed, Deniz had seen the camera. She had even laughed. But she had not known the video would go to the whole class group. When the notifications started, her phone was on the table. The screen lit up one after another. Deniz opened it and watched the video. Then she watched it again, this time trying to see herself through someone else's eyes. Someone had added a funny sticker over her hat and re-shared it that way. Her heart sped up. She wrote to Ela: "Why did you put this in the group?" "But you let me film it," came the reply. Deniz looked at the sentence. Something about it was wrong, but she could not name what. Pusula showed four locks above the screen. They stood side by side, and each had its own key. Filming. Storing. Editing. Sharing. "You opened the first lock," said Pusula. "The other three were still closed. One lock opening doesn't open them all." Deniz found her sentence. She wrote to Ela: "I said yes to filming, not to sharing. Please take it down now." Ela replied at once: "You're totally overreacting." Deniz read the message three times, and for a moment the shame came back — maybe I really am overreacting. Then she took screenshots. Of the video, the sticker and the messages. The next morning she went to the class counsellor. The video was taken down. Those who had re-shared it were also asked to delete it, and two did so at once. Ela did not talk to Deniz for a day. On the second day she came and apologised. The apology was a bit mixed — the sentence "I didn't mean any harm" was in it too — but in the end she said: "I didn't ask. I should have asked." The two of them made a rule, and Deniz wrote it in her notebook. First, permission to film. Then a separate permission for which group or platform it would be shared in. For filters and stickers there would be a separate ask too. And most important: even if Deniz had said yes before, she could later ask for it to be removed. Changing your mind was valid. A week later Deniz shared a photo she had chosen herself. In the garden, her hat in her hand, she stood there without smiling. This time the image had not slipped out of her hands. It was her own telling of it. "Saying yes to being filmed is not a yes to sharing. I've changed my mind; please take it down." Pusula's tool: The four digital permissions May it be filmed? May it be stored on the phone? May it be edited or have a sticker added? Where and with whom may it be shared? → And remember: A later wish to remove it always applies. For grown-ups When a young person's private image is shared without permission, do not put the blame on them. Prevent the content from spreading further, keep the evidence safely, and use the school's or platform's procedures. If there are threats or intimate content, get professional safety support. --- CHAPTER 4
== ch006.xhtml ['file6.jpg', 'file7.jpg'] epub:type="bodymatter"> My Voice at the Examination The stomach pain had lasted three weeks, and her mother had made an appointment. The waiting room was glaring. The ceiling lights were white and harsh, the phone at reception rang constantly, and there was a programme on the television with the sound turned down — the sound was down, but the picture moved. Deniz waited fifteen minutes and was already tired in the waiting room. When they went in, the doctor was looking at the computer. "What's the complaint?" she said, speaking fast. "Lie down on the couch." Deniz looked at the couch. Then she looked at the doctor. She did not know what would happen. She did not know how long it would take, where she would be touched, whether she should take off her shoes. Her body tensed. Her mother stepped in and began to answer the questions — when it had started, what she had eaten, where it hurt. She knew everything, and everything was correct. But Deniz was sitting in the room, and no one was asking her. The doctor reached out to touch her stomach. Deniz pulled back. There was a silence. Pusula lit three lights in front of the white coat — like a traffic light, but with three words: explain. ask. stop. Deniz took the card out of her bag. She had prepared the card two weeks earlier; not for exactly this moment, but for a moment like this. She held it out to the doctor. On the card it said: "Explain to me briefly and directly. Before touching me, tell me where and why you will touch me. Let my mother stay with me. When I make the stop sign, wait." The doctor took the card and read it. She turned away from the computer and looked at Deniz for the first time. "Okay," she said. Her voice slowed down too. "First I'd like to listen to your stomach. The stethoscope may be cold. Is that all right?" Deniz nodded. Before each step a sentence came. "Now I'll press on the left side." "Does it hurt here?" "Now it's finishing." At one point the pressure increased, and Deniz raised her hand. The doctor stopped. She did not ask, did not argue, did not say "nearly done." They waited thirty seconds. Then Deniz nodded, and they carried on. When the examination was over, Deniz's body was still tense, and she had not liked any of the touches. But fear was something else, and that had decreased. Because she knew two things: what would happen, and that she could stop it. At the end the doctor asked the questions directly to Deniz. "Does the pain get worse at night or during the day?" Deniz answered. Her mother only added a missing date. "Before each touch, tell me what you'll do and why. I want you to wait at my stop sign." Pusula's tool: The examination card Write down my preferred way of communicating. Ask for an explanation before each step. Say that a trusted person should stay with me. Choose a break or stop sign. Use my right to ask again about a word I didn't understand. For grown-ups Include the young person in the conversation in an age-appropriate way and give them time to answer. In medical procedures, provide as much information, consent and privacy as possible. Explain beforehand that emergencies may go differently. --- CHAPTER 5
== ch007.xhtml ['file8.jpg', 'file9.jpg'] epub:type="bodymatter"> The Key Is With Me Towards the end of the year Deniz wrote the sentences she had used all year on small cards. She attached the cards to a metal keyring — next to her real keys. On one it said "No touch today." On one, "Ask before you share." On one, "Explain first." They were all short. Long sentences would not come out of her mouth at the crucial moment; the short ones did. She was at the school's theatre rehearsal. The costumes had just arrived, and the assistant was going down the row. When she came to Deniz she reached out — to adjust the waistband of the skirt, without stopping, as a routine task. Deniz took a step back. "Tell me first what you want to change." The assistant stopped. She was surprised for a moment — no one had said that to her. Then she showed the clip in her hand. "I want to tighten the waist from behind. You can do it yourself, or ask a friend for help." Deniz did it herself. She looked in the mirror. Then she changed her mind. "This skirt doesn't suit me," she said. "Is there a costume with trousers?" The assistant went to the rack and looked. "Yes." No one said "you just said okay." No one treated it as a whim. After the rehearsal Deniz sat at the edge of the stage, and Pusula settled beside her. "I'm wondering about something," said Deniz. "If I set so many boundaries, won't people move away from me?" Pusula took the keys. She did not hang them on a door. She put them in Deniz's palm. "A boundary doesn't mean shutting everyone out," she said. "It means you know how the door is opened. A house with no door is not an open house — it's an unprotected house." Deniz looked at her palm. That year there had also been days when she hugged. Days when she shared a photo, accepted help, held someone's hand. None of it went against her boundaries. Because all of it had been chosen. And she had learned something else, and that was the harder one: carrying your own key also means recognising the other person's key. Stopping when her cousin said "I don't want to." Not pushing when Ada said "I don't want to talk now." Deleting a photo without arguing when a friend asked. The key was for protecting herself and hearing the other person. Deniz put the keyring in her pocket and went back on stage. "My yes, my no and my 'I've changed my mind' are valid. Another person's boundary is just as valid." 1. I don't want to right now. 2. Will you explain it first? 3. Yes to this part, no to the other. 4. I've changed my mind. 5. I want support from an adult I trust. For grown-ups Teach boundaries not only as protection from danger, but as part of everyday relationships. Meet the young person's "no" with respect; at the same time, teach them to respect others' boundaries. When a boundary is violated, do not blame the young person. --- ## Closing activity: My four doors Draw four headings: touch, comment, image, health. For each, write what is comfortable for you, what you want to be asked about first, and what you don't want. This list can change. If you want, share it only with a person you trust. ## Sources and safety note In cases of threats, forced touch, intimate images or safety concerns, don't carry it alone.
